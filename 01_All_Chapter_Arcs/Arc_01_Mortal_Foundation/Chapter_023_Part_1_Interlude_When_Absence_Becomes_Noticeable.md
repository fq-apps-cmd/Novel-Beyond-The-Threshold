# Chapter 23 - Interlude — When Absence Becomes Noticeable (Part 1)

Chapter 23 —Interlude — When Absence Becomes Noticeable Part 1  

The village did not realize Mateo was gone immediately.  
At first, nothing seemed out of place. The dirt roads carried the usual movement — footsteps pressed into hardened earth shaped by years of training rather  
than design. The paths curved naturally through the village, not in straight lines but in efficient arcs between homes, open training grounds, and scattered  
shops. The morning light filtered through the canopy overhead in its familiar fractured patterns, the same patches of warmth and shadow that had greeted the  
village every day for as long as anyone could remember.  
No one had ever bothered paving the paths. They could have. Stone-laid streets and rune-reinforced roads existed in larger settlements — even in nearby  
regions where trade volume and sect presence demanded infrastructure that a village of this size would never require. But here, where training spilled into  
walkways and sparring matches began mid-conversation before ending without resentment, hard-packed soil worked better. It shifted under pressure, absorbed  
impact, and required no repair after conflict.  
Which was frequent.  
Near the center of the village, a group of young trainees sparred in a loose circle, their movements rough but energetic. The morning air carried the sound of  
wooden weapons meeting, of feet shuffling on packed earth, of the specific grunt that occurred when someone committed too far and received the immediate,  
democratic feedback of the ground against their back. One boy overcommitted to a forward strike — his weight carrying past his balance point, his arm  
extending beyond recovery — and was knocked flat by a counter that was more opportunistic than skilled.  
"Too slow," the victor said, extending a hand.  
"Then stop letting me hit you," the fallen trainee shot back, already pushing himself to his feet without accepting the help. His pride recovered faster than his  
footing.  
No one intervened. No one needed to.  
Further along, the row of simple structures that formed the trading path had opened for the morning. The herb stall remained in its usual position — bundles  
arranged across the low table, sharp scents drifting into the road with the specific bitterness of dried roots and the fainter, more complex aroma of preparations  
that had been aging in sealed jars. The woman behind it sorted her inventory with quiet efficiency, her stained fingertips moving between bundles with the  
automatic precision of someone who had performed this task so many times that her hands operated independently of her attention.  
She didn't look up when she spoke.  
"...He hasn't passed today."  
Across from her, the blacksmith paused mid-strike. His hammer hovered at the apex of its arc — the brief moment of suspension between rising and falling  
where the tool's weight was balanced against the arm's intent. The pause lasted longer than the metal's temperature preferred.  
"...No."  
A beat of silence. The hammer fell. The strike rang. But the rhythm had been disrupted, and both of them knew it.  
"...He usually does," the herb vendor said.  
"...Yeah."  
They said nothing else. The herb vendor returned to her bundles. The blacksmith returned to his work. The morning continued around them in its familiar  
patterns.  
But they noticed.  
The absence of a single set of footsteps — steady, unhurried, arriving at the same approximate time each morning — had created a gap in the village's rhythm  
that was too small to name and too specific to ignore. Like a note missing from a melody that most people could not identify but that everyone felt was wrong.  
Tomas noticed first.  
Not because he paid more attention than the vendors or the blacksmith, but because expectation had formed. Mateo had become part of his rhythm — the  
sparring partner who arrived before the sun was fully up, the presence that sharpened his morning training through the simple fact of existing nearby, the  
person whose absence required explanation because his presence never had.  
"...He's late."  
Tomas stood at the edge of the training yard, his arms folded, his gaze directed toward the path Mateo usually walked. The path was empty. It had been empty  
since Tomas arrived, which was unusual. Mateo was many things — irritating, opaque, deliberately difficult to predict — but late was not typically among them.  
A voice responded from behind him. Quickly. Without the pause that most people inserted before answering a statement that had not been directed at them.  
"...Without explanation?"  
Tomas glanced up briefly.  
Diego.  
He stood a few meters away, leaning against the fence post that he had claimed as his unofficial observation station over the past several months. His posture  
was casual. His expression was not. Beneath the perpetual ease that Diego wore like a second skin, something sharper was operating — the focused attention of  
someone who tracked patterns not because he was told to but because patterns were how he understood the world.  
They hadn't spoken much recently — not out of conflict or distance, but because the rhythms of their days had diverged as training intensified and the village's  
ambient pressure increased. They were not strangers. They were people who crossed paths often enough to recognize patterns in each other's behavior —  
which meant they were also people who recognized when those patterns broke.  
"...Yes," Tomas said.  
Diego studied him for a moment. His gaze moved from Tomas's face to the empty path and back with the systematic attention of someone assembling a  
conclusion from available data.  
"...Then something is wrong."  
Tomas didn't argue. He was already moving.  
Diego followed.  
Neither commented on that.  
They walked the path Mateo usually took — past the training ground where the morning's sparring continued without them, past the herb stall where the  

vendor tracked their passage with a glance that confirmed she had been expecting someone to follow, past the blacksmith's open forge where the hammer's  
rhythm had regained its steadiness but where the smith's gaze lifted briefly as they passed.  
The path continued through the village's eastern section, where the homes sat closer to the forest edge and the canopy pressed lower overhead. The light here  
was dimmer, the air cooler, the sounds of the village's center replaced by the quieter ambient of the boundary zone — fewer voices, more wind, the occasional  
creak of wood that belonged to the forest rather than the settlement.  
They reached the forest line.  
The transition was not a line in any literal sense — there was no fence, no marker, no carved stone that declared where the village ended and the forest began.  
But the change was unmistakable to anyone who had lived here long enough to feel it. The ground shifted — the packed, maintained earth of the village's paths  
giving way to soil that was softer, darker, threaded with root systems that broke the surface in gnarled ridges and then dove back down as though checking  
whether anything had changed below since their last appearance. The canopy, which had been filtering light in the village's familiar fractured patterns, closed  
more densely here — the branches of trees that had never been trimmed or managed pressing together overhead until the sky became a mosaic of green and  
shadow rather than blue and gold.  
The air changed. Not dramatically — not the wall of difference that the deeper forest produced — but in the subtle, graduated way that boundaries announced  
themselves when they were gradients rather than edges. The village's ambient scent — cooking smoke, packed earth, the mineral tang of the forge — thinned.  
What replaced it was damper, more organic, carrying the layered chemistry of decomposing leaves, living bark, and the faint metallic undertone that Mateo had  
noticed during his boundary walks years ago and that Tomas was only now beginning to perceive.  
The sounds changed too. The village's human-generated noise — the forge, the training yard, the conversations that layered into the morning's ambient hum —  
did not stop. It faded. Each step toward the forest reduced its volume by a fraction, as though the trees absorbed human sound the way they absorbed light —  
not blocking it, but filtering it, removing frequencies until what remained was quieter, cleaner, and dominated by the forest's own vocabulary. The creak of wood  
under wind. The rustle of undergrowth. The intermittent call of something that lived in the canopy and communicated in patterns that neither of them could  
decode.  
And beneath all of that — beneath the physical changes, beneath the sensory transition — there was something else. Something that Tomas could not name  
but that his body recognized the way a hand recognized heat before the mind registered temperature.  
The ancestor's residual aura.  
He could not feel it the way Mateo could — as a specific, analyzable presence with identifiable properties. He felt it as absence. The subtle, pervasive sense that  
something existed at this boundary that he was not equipped to perceive — a weight in the air that pressed against awareness without pressing against skin, a  
significance that registered as instinct rather than information. The feeling of standing at the edge of something that was deeper than it appeared, the way a  
person standing at the edge of a calm lake could feel the depth beneath the surface without being able to see it.  
Most villagers crossed this boundary without noticing. Children ran past it. Trainees jogged through it. Elders walked it during patrols.  
Tomas noticed.  
Not because he was more sensitive than the others — not yet. But because Mateo had crossed this boundary before him, and Mateo's passage had left  
something in Tomas's awareness that made the boundary visible in a way it had not been before. Not a mark. Not a trail. Something subtler — the  
understanding that this line meant something, planted by years of watching Mateo regard it with the specific attention that Mateo reserved for things that  
operated on principles most people could not perceive.  
He stopped.  
His foot pressed into the earth at the boundary — the exact point where the village's influence thinned and the forest's indifference began.  
And the earth responded.  
Not dramatically. Not with any force or visible effect. The soil did not move. The ground did not shake. Nothing happened that anyone watching would have  
seen.  
But Tomas felt it.  
A subtle shift in the way the ground received his weight. As though the resistance between his foot and the earth had decreased by a fraction — not softer, not  
weaker, but more responsive. More connected. As though the ground was not merely supporting his weight but acknowledging it. The way a handshake  
acknowledged a presence — not by yielding, but by meeting.  
A faint ripple moved through the soil beneath him — not outward, but through him. Through the contact point of his foot, up through his ankle, into the bones  
of his leg. The sensation was brief, barely perceptible, and it carried no force or energy that he could identify. It was simply contact. Communication. The earth  
speaking to him in a language he did not yet understand but that his body, somewhere beneath conscious awareness, recognized.  
Tomas stilled.  
"...That's new."  
Diego, who had been watching Tomas's face rather than his feet and who had therefore noticed the change in expression without understanding its cause,  
leaned slightly forward. His head tilted — the specific angle of someone whose pattern-recognition had just flagged something that did not fit the established  
model.  
"...Did the ground just listen to you?"  
"...No," Tomas said slowly, his brow furrowed. His weight shifted experimentally — forward, back, testing the sensation, trying to reproduce it. The ground felt  
normal now. Inert. The usual packed earth that he had walked on every day of his life. But the memory of the response lingered — not in his mind, where  
memories lived as images and words, but in his feet, where memories lived as sensation. "...It followed."  
The sensation vanished as quickly as it had arrived — dissolving back into the normal, inert resistance of packed soil, leaving behind only the impression that  
something had been there. Like the warmth that lingered on skin after a hand was removed. Like the echo of a voice that had spoken once and would not  
repeat itself.  
But the impression stayed — subtle, incomplete, closer than before.  
Tomas's level — roughly the fourth stage of the Body Tempering Realm by public assessment — should not allow that level of environmental response. Earth  
affinity at that stage manifested as increased physical stability, enhanced balance, a natural comfort with ground-based techniques. It did not manifest as  
communication with the earth itself. That required depth that the fourth stage did not possess — the kind of depth that came from the middle stages at  
minimum, where the affinity moved from passive alignment to active interaction.  
Which meant something had changed.  
Not in the ground.  
In him.  
The corrections Mateo had made — the needle adjustments, the guided pulse, the smoothing of the dual-affinity interference — had not merely eased the  

symptoms of internal conflict. They had freed capacity. The energy that Tomas's body had been spending to manage resistance it did not know existed was now  
available for other purposes. And his earth affinity, no longer fighting a perpetual, invisible battle against the wood signature that shared its space, had  
deepened by a margin that Tomas could not measure but that the ground beneath his feet had just acknowledged.  
Tomas did not understand any of this.  
He understood only that something had shifted, and that the shift had arrived without his permission or his awareness.  
And that it was connected to Mateo.  
He didn't know how he knew that. The knowledge existed below the level where logic operated — in the same space where the earth's response had registered,  
where instinct lived, where the body understood things that the mind had not yet been told.  
He looked toward the forest.  
"...He went in."  
Diego blinked.  
"...He what?"  
Tomas exhaled quietly. The breath carried the specific weight of someone who had been expecting this and had been hoping to be wrong.  
"...He said things would get interesting when he started going deeper."  
Diego scratched his head — the gesture of someone processing information that did not fit comfortably into any existing category.  
"...Is he always like that?"  
"...Yes."  
"...Does it usually end well?"  
"...No."  
A pause.  
"...So you're concerned."  
"...Yes."  
They didn't move forward.  
Not yet.  

The elders gathered once Mateo's absence was confirmed.  
Not in panic. Not in disorder. That was not how things worked here. The council assembled in the meeting hall — the same hall whose formations Mateo had  
sabotaged and then offered to repair, the same hall whose sound suppression had been restored through Elder Liang's meticulous work and Elder Doña Reyes's  
deep-layer inspection, the same hall that now held conversations with a security that was, ironically, better than it had been before a child had demonstrated its  
inadequacy.  
The room was different from the last time Tomas had seen it. The adjustments the elders had made following Mateo's intrusion — the repositioned seating, the  
adjusted curtain angles, the tightened formation calibration — had produced a space that felt more contained. More deliberate. The air itself carried less drift, as  
though the room's atmosphere had been instructed to take its responsibilities more seriously.  
Elder Marcos sat at the head of the table, his posture composed and unhurried. His hands rested flat on the table's surface — the specific positioning of  
someone who intended to listen before deciding, and who wanted the room to see that intention in his body language. His expression carried the measured  
calm that had defined his leadership for decades — not the absence of concern, but the management of it. Whatever he felt about Mateo's departure was  
stored behind a composure that the council had learned to read not through what it showed but through how still it became.  
Elder Hiro sat to his left, his jaw set with the specific tension of a man who had been proven right about something and was experiencing the particular  
frustration of vindication without satisfaction. His arms were folded — not the casual fold of someone at rest, but the compressed fold of someone containing  
an opinion that was fighting to escape. His eyes moved between the other elders with the rapid, evaluative attention of someone who was already three  
arguments ahead of the current conversation and was waiting for everyone else to catch up.  
He had been the first to arrive after Marcos. That, by itself, communicated urgency. Hiro was never first unless the situation warranted the energy of being first,  
and the energy he was currently containing suggested that the situation warranted significantly more than punctuality.  
Elder Vargas and Elder Liang occupied their usual positions at the table's midsection. Vargas's expression carried the open concern of someone who processed  
worry externally — his brow creased, his posture forward, his hands occasionally gesturing toward points that had not yet been raised but that he was preparing  
to raise. Liang sat beside him with his bark sheet and brush already in hand, his attention divided between the room and the notes he was compiling — a real-  
time record of the meeting that would later serve as the council's official account of whatever decisions were reached. His writing was faster than usual. The  
characters, normally formed with the architectural precision that defined everything Liang did, were marginally less composed — still legible, still structured, but  
carrying the subtle evidence of a hand that was operating under time pressure.  
Elder Park sat with his hands folded, his expression carrying the specific quality of someone who had already identified the question no one wanted to ask and  
was patiently waiting for the conversation to arrive at it naturally. Park's contribution to council discussions was rarely the first word or the last. It was the word  
in the middle — the observation that reframed everything that had preceded it and redirected everything that followed. He had a talent for uncomfortable  
truths delivered at uncomfortable moments, and the room's awareness of that talent was visible in the way several elders avoided his gaze when they spoke.  
Elder Kurosawa's posture was deceptively relaxed — one arm resting along the back of his chair, his body angled slightly to the side, the appearance of  
someone listening with casual interest to a conversation that did not particularly concern him. The deception was practiced and convincing. Beneath it, his  
attention moved between the speakers with the focused tracking of someone who was not merely following the discussion but mapping it — identifying  
positions, predicting arguments, calculating which alliances would form around which conclusions. His gaze lingered on Elder Shen for a fraction longer than on  
the others, as though the window elder's silence carried information that the spoken words did not.  
Elder Tomás Aguilar sat nearest the door, his position unchanged from every previous meeting. His expression was warmer than the others — not softer, not  
less serious, but carrying the specific quality of someone whose concern was rooted in care rather than calculation. Where the other elders processed Mateo's  
absence through frameworks of risk, responsibility, and institutional response, Aguilar processed it through the simpler, more direct framework of a person who  
worried about a child. His hands rested on the table, but his fingers moved occasionally — the unconscious motion of someone whose body expressed the  
anxiety that his face managed to contain.  
Elder Doña Reyes occupied her corner seat with the settled authority of someone who had been sitting in that position since before several of the other elders  
had joined the council. Her silver hair caught the light from the adjusted windows — the same windows that Mateo had identified as a line-of-sight vulnerability  
and that Elder Liang had subsequently corrected with curtain adjustments that the entire council now pretended had been their idea. Her hands rested on the  
table with the patient weight of stone — still, stable, communicating nothing through motion because everything she needed to communicate was contained in  

her presence. Her eyes, however, were active — moving between the speakers with the careful attention of someone who was not evaluating what was being  
said but what was being meant.  
Elder Shen was at the window.  
He had not turned when the others entered. Had not acknowledged the meeting's commencement. Had not shifted his position or adjusted his posture or given  
any indication that the council's assembly was something he considered relevant to his current activity, which appeared to consist entirely of looking at the sky  
through the glass that Mateo had once pointed out was insufficient to prevent visual observation from outside.  
His silence was not absence. It was presence — the specific, weighted presence of someone who was listening to everything and processing it at a depth that  
the conversation's surface could not reach. The other elders had learned, over years, that Shen's silence was proportional to the significance of his eventual  
contribution. Brief silences preceded minor observations. Extended silences preceded the kind of statements that redirected entire discussions.  
This silence had been going on since before the meeting started.  
At the far side of the room, Elder Aurelio sat in his heavy chair with his arms folded. His broad frame occupied the space the way a boulder occupied a  
riverbed — not aggressively, not intentionally, but with the simple, undeniable fact of being too substantial to ignore. His scarred hands rested on the chair's  
arms. His expression was unreadable — not blank, but layered, the kind of face that contained multiple reactions simultaneously and allowed none of them to  
reach the surface.  
He had not spoken when he arrived. He had not greeted the other elders. He had simply sat, folded his arms, and waited with the patience of someone who had  
been waiting for longer things than meetings and who measured urgency on a scale that the council's concerns did not register on.
