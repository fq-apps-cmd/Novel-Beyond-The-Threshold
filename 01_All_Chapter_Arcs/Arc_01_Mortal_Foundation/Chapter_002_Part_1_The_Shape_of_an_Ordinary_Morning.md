# Chapter 2 - The Shape of an Ordinary Morning (Part 1)

Chapter 2  
The Shape of an Ordinary Morning  

Morning in the village did not begin quietly. It began with movement.  
Mateo stepped out onto the packed dirt path as the early light came down through the dense forest canopy that stretched over most of the settlement.  
The air carried damp earth and tree bark, mixed with herbs, smoke, and hot metal. The light arrived unevenly, broken by the canopy into fragments that  
shifted with the wind and laid patterns across the ground that moved independently of anything walking over them. Some stretches of path sat in full  
warmth. Others held a cool shadow that would not lift until midday, if it lifted at all.  
He stood there a moment and let the cold of the packed earth come up through the soles of his shoes.  
The village was not large, and no one had ever attempted symmetry with it. The homes were built from wood and reinforced with stone, shaped by  
practicality rather than by any interest in matching one another. Some had roofs that sloped steeply to shed the rain that came through the canopy in  
heavy irregular bursts. Others had broader overhangs that made shaded porches, where tools hung on pegs and herbs dried in bundled rows, turning  
from green to brown over the course of a season. The wood had aged differently on every structure depending on how much sun and moisture it  
caught, so that no two walls were quite the same color and the village as a whole looked assembled rather than designed.  
The paths curved between the homes because that was where feet had gone, worn into the ground over years rather than laid out and constructed. They  
widened wherever traffic concentrated, near the training yard and the market stalls and the well at the village center, where water was drawn each  
morning in a sequence so consistent that the rope had cut a groove into the stone lip. They narrowed where fewer people walked, some of them  
tapering into traces that thinned out and disappeared into the undergrowth at the village's edge.  
No one here had ever seen the need to replace them with stone.  
They could have. They simply hadn't.  
A loud crack echoed off to his right.  
Mateo glanced over as two of the older trainees clashed near a clearing, their movements rough but purposeful. One of them stepped too far into the  
other's reach and was thrown down hard, hitting the dirt with a sharp exhale that scattered dust in a brief cloud around his shoulders.  
"You're telegraphing every swing," the victor said, extending a hand downward.  
"Then stop reading them," the other shot back, already pushing himself upright without taking the help. He brushed the dirt off his sleeve and did not  
appear to consider the fall worth commenting on.  
Neither of them seemed particularly bothered. The one who had gone down rolled his shoulder twice, set his feet, and gestured for the other to come  
again, and the other did.  
The movements are committed but unrefined. This amount of force is adequate for their stage. The transitions between strikes are not. The first one  
generates power from his shoulders rather than his hips, which costs him both range and recovery speed. The second compensates for slower reflexes with  
aggression, which works until it doesn't, and when it stops working it will stop all at once.  
Mateo kept walking without stopping. His pace stayed steady and his posture stayed loose, though his gaze moved constantly, not in distraction but in  
observation. Every motion around him was noted, weighed, and filed away without any conscious effort on his part.  
A woman passed him on the path carrying a basket of root vegetables against her hip, her steps heavy and her eyes still half-closed against the light.  
Farther up, a child darted between two homes chasing something small and fast that disappeared under a porch before it could be caught. The child  
stopped, crouched, and peered into the darkness beneath the wooden planks for a long and hopeful moment, then stood and ran off in a different  
direction entirely, the original pursuit already forgotten.  
At the well, three people had formed the loose approximation of a line, which in practice meant two of them talking and the third doing the work. The  
rope creaked in its groove, the bucket knocked once against the stone on the way up as it did every morning, and water sloshed over the lip and  
darkened the ground in a ring that never fully dried anymore. One of them said something about the eastern trail being soft after the rain. Another  
disagreed on principle rather than on evidence. Nobody involved seemed to consider the outcome important.  
The village was waking the way it always did, in layers, each one slightly louder than the last, until the accumulated activity reached the threshold that  
passed for normal. Shutters knocked open somewhere behind him. A whetstone started up and stopped and started again. Smoke thickened over two or  
three roofs at once as the morning meals went on, and the smell of it moved down the path ahead of him, mixing with everything else the village put  
into its air before the day was properly underway.  
None of it required his attention. He gave it his attention anyway, because there was nothing else worth spending it on, and because he had discovered  
that he liked it.  
Farther along, a modest line of shops marked what passed for a trade path here. The structures were simple but well used, their entrances open, goods  
displayed with minimal formality. A cloth merchant was arranging bolts of rough-spun fabric on a wooden frame outside his stall, every piece dyed in  
muted earth tones. Beside him, a woman sold clay pots of varying sizes, some glazed and some raw, stacked in careful columns that stopped one layer  
short of collapse.  
Between the two stalls, a conversation was already underway.  
"Your fabric bleeds color in the rain," the pot seller said, not looking up from her arrangement.  
"Your pots crack in the cold," the cloth merchant replied, adjusting a bolt that did not need adjusting.  
"My pots are fired properly."  
"My fabric is dyed properly."  

"Then why is my display cloth turning brown?"  
The cloth merchant paused. He looked at the display cloth draped over the pot seller's table. He looked at the sky. He looked at the cloth again.  
"...That was already brown."  
"It was blue."  
"It was a brown blue."  
"That is not a color."  
"It is now."  
Mateo passed them without slowing, though a corner of his awareness filed the exchange with the same thoroughness it applied to everything else. The  
two of them occupied adjacent spaces, competed for overlapping customers, and expressed their mutual irritation through a daily verbal exchange that  
neither would ever admit they looked forward to.  
It was, in its own way, a form of companionship.  
He had watched entire civilizations fail to arrive at anything better.  
The herb stall stood where it always did, bundles arranged neatly across a low table that years of use had sanded smooth. The scent sharpened here into  
bitter greens, dried roots, and something faintly medicinal that drifted out into the path and stayed there. Small clay jars lined the back of the display,  
each one sealed with wax and marked with characters that described the contents in the herb vendor's precise, angular handwriting.  
The woman behind it looked up as Mateo approached. She was neither young nor old, somewhere in the middle years, with hands permanently stained  
at the fingertips from handling plants and a gaze that missed very little.  
"You're earlier than usual."  
"...Marginally."  
She snorted softly. "That's still earlier."  
Mateo paused briefly, his gaze shifting across the display. The arrangement had changed since his last visit, in small ways that his awareness catalogued  
before he had decided to look at all.  
"You moved these," he said, pointing to a small cluster of leaves set further from the others. They were darker than the surrounding herbs, their edges  
curling slightly inward, dried at a different temperature or for a different length of time.  
"They dry better apart," she stated.  
"...That's true."  
It was. The separation reduced the moisture passing between those leaves and the bundles beside them, which would otherwise have compromised the  
potency of both. It was a practical choice, and it came from actually understanding the material rather than from repeating something she had once  
been told. He noted that as well.  
She studied him a moment before speaking again, already knowing what the answer would be.  
"...You going out again?"  
A short pause followed.  
"...Yes."  
She sighed faintly, though not in disapproval. Reaching beneath the table, she pulled out a small wrapped bundle and held it toward him. The wrapping  
was simple cloth tied with cord, and the contents had clearly been selected rather than gathered.  
"Take these," she said. "A small gift."  
She helped the younger cultivators now and again, in gestures she never drew attention to and that the recipients were wise enough not to publicize,  
since generosity of that kind attracted more demand than her patience could sustain.  
Mateo accepted it without checking the contents.  
"...Thank you," he said.  
Then he continued along the path.  
Behind him, the woman shook her head slightly, though a faint smile followed. She had been selling herbs in this village for longer than Mateo had been  
alive, and she had seen enough young cultivators pass through her stall to recognize the ones who were different. Most of them looked at her herbs and  
saw products. This one looked at them and saw the connections between them, which uses ran together and which ones cancelled out.  
She still wasn't sure whether that was admirable or concerning.  
She suspected it was both.  
The ringing of metal carried across the path ahead.  
Mateo approached the blacksmith's area, a wide open structure rather than an enclosed shop. Thick beams supported a simple roof of clay tiles and left  
most of the space exposed to air and light. The forge required ventilation and the smith required visibility, and between them the workspace had  
become as much a part of the village's public life as the market stalls were.  
The ground there was darker, scorched in places from repeated work. Tiny fragments of metal glinted in the packed earth where years of hammer strikes  
had driven them. Heat from the forge reached the path in slow waves and carried the sharp mineral scent of heated iron with it, underlaid by the fainter  
smell of the coal feeding it.  
A heavy strike echoed as the smith's hammer came down against glowing metal, sparks scattering outward in brief arcs before vanishing. The smith was  
a broad man with thick forearms marked by old burns and years of work, and his rhythm was as much a part of the village's ambient sound as the  
birdsong or the wind.  
Mateo watched for a moment.  
The alignment of the hammer's descent was slightly off. Not enough to ruin the piece, but enough that the force was distributing unevenly across the  
metal's surface. The left side of the strike zone was taking more compression than the right, which would leave a subtle asymmetry in the finished  
product that most people would never detect, and that the smith himself might not notice until the sharpening stage.  
Mateo's eyes flickered faintly, in a way no one in the village had ever been positioned to notice.  
The deviation originates in his right shoulder, which is compensating for fatigue in the elbow. He has been working longer than his body prefers, and for  
several days now. He will keep going, because the order is already promised, and he will notice the elbow properly in about a week, when it stops being  
optional.  
The smith paused mid-motion.  
"...You're staring again."  

Mateo didn't look away. "...Yes."  
"...What is it this time?"  
A brief silence passed.  
"...Your last strike drifted."  
The smith stared at him for a long moment, and his expression worked through several distinct stages: surprise first, then irritation, then the brief and  
unwelcome consideration of whether a child could possibly be right about something he had been doing for twenty years, and finally the reluctant  
memory that this particular child had been right about similar things before.  
Then, "Hmmph."  
"...How would a youngster like you know this? I've been doing this longer than you've been alive."  
"...It's true," Mateo said, and shrugged.  
"...This kid again." The smith turned back to the forge and lifted the hammer. "Let me get back to work."  
Mateo considered that as he walked on. The distinction between accuracy and helpfulness was one he encountered frequently. People did not always  
want to know what was true. They wanted to know what was useful. And usefulness, unlike truth, depended entirely on who was standing there and what  
they could do about it.  
"...Noted."  
Further in, the forest grew denser between the structures, and the boundary between settlement and wilderness stopped meaning much of anything.  
Roots broke through the ground between homes, thick and gnarled, their upper surfaces worn smooth by the feet that had been stepping over them for  
generations. Small creatures crossed the paths without hesitation. A lizard held still on a stone warmed by the morning light. A bird worked at something  
between the roots of a tree that grew so close to a home's wall that its branches brushed the shutters whenever the wind picked up, and no one had  
ever suggested cutting it back.  
This place did not force nature away.  
It adapted around it.  
Which, Mateo found, was peaceful.  
The coexistence was not accidental. The forest was not an obstacle to be conquered but an environment to be negotiated with, continuously, on terms  
that shifted every season. The homes were built around the trees rather than in place of them. The paths followed the terrain rather than reshaping it.  
The training yards used the natural ground rather than leveling it, which produced worse footing and better cultivators.  
From a distance the settlement looked less like something people had built and more like something the forest had allowed. That impression was not  
entirely wrong.  
Something cool shifted against his chest as he walked, the small pendant on its cord settling back where it had been. He barely registered it. It had been  
there since the first morning he could remember, and his mother told the story of buying it often enough that he could recite the vendor's imagined face  
himself.  
"...Hey, Mateo. Are you at it again? That weird way of walking?"  
The voice cut cleanly through everything else.  
Mateo slowed and stopped, finishing the motion he had been running through mentally, a sequence of weight transfers he practiced while walking and  
that should have been invisible to anyone not specifically watching for the subtle shifts in his gait. Then he turned toward the source.  
"...Hey, Inés."  
"I'm not sure what you're talking about."  
Inés stood a short distance away, arms folded, her posture balanced so evenly that it looked like a resting state rather than a stance. Her gaze wasn't  
curious and it wasn't irritated. It was precise. She had been watching him for considerably longer than the question implied, and the question itself was  
not an inquiry at all. It was a statement shaped to sound like one.  
"You're changing your stride as you walk," she said. "You've done it four times since you passed the well."  
Mateo exhaled quietly.  
She counted them.  
The corrections are subtle enough that they should not be visible during casual movement, particularly at this distance. Either she has been watching closely  
for an extended period, or her perception of physical motion is significantly more refined than her cultivation level would suggest. Both possibilities are worth  
noting. Neither is convenient.  
"You're imagining things."  
"No," she replied calmly. "I'm not."  
Her tone carried no challenge and no accusation, only the flat certainty of someone who trusted her own eyes more than she trusted anyone's denial.  
She would not be talked out of it. She would be moved by evidence and by nothing else, and at the moment the evidence was on her side of the path.  
I should be more careful about what I do or say around her.  
She is very observant.  
Inés would require more careful management than most.  
"Are you going to keep denying it," she said, "or are we going to be late?"  
"...Both are possible."  
"Only one of them is happening."  
She turned and started walking, and after a moment he fell into step beside her, deliberately and precisely the way an ordinary boy would walk.  
They went a while without talking. Inés was not the sort to fill a silence for its own sake, which he appreciated more than he intended to admit, and the  
path carried them beneath a stretch of canopy so thick that the temperature dropped noticeably for a dozen strides before the light broke through  
again.  
"You do it more when you're thinking," she said eventually, without looking over.  
"...That's a very broad claim."  
"It's a very specific observation."  
He let that sit. There was no version of the truth he could hand her that would be smaller than the truth itself, so he handed her nothing, and she allowed  
it, which was worse. People who demanded answers could be waited out. People who simply kept watching could not.  
Ahead, past the last of the homes, the ground opened out into the wide packed clearing where the others had already gathered. The sound of it reached  

them first, the flat impact of wood on wood, the scuff of feet finding purchase in loose dirt, someone laughing at someone else's expense and being told  
to be quiet about it.  
This was where everyone practiced and sparred with each other in order to refine their skills.  
It was also where everyone found out, repeatedly and in public, exactly how much of what they believed about themselves was true.
