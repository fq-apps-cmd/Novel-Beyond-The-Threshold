The fireworks had faded, but their absence did not restore normalcy. Deep within the cave, Mateo remained still, one hand resting lightly against the rough wall beside the newly opened passage, the other close to the small cube hidden in his clothing. The laughter had not fully left him yet. It lingered at the edge of his expression, in the quiet ease of his breathing, and in the simple fact that something so absurd had spoken across worlds and then responded to being noticed by insulting him directly. That alone made the entire event worthwhile.
He looked upward again, though there was no real “up” to look toward in the cave. The folded fireworks were gone. The pressure of the announcement had softened. And yet a faint trace lingered, as though a thread of impossible structure still hung through the world.
Mateo exhaled once, almost amused. “…So you can see me.”
The answer came immediately.
“Oh, I can do many things,” the Chihuahua said, its voice appearing in the cave without echo, as wrong in its presence now as it had been during the announcement. “Seeing you was not supposed to be one of them.”
Mateo’s smile widened slightly. “…Unfortunate.”
“It is deeply inconvenient,” the spirit replied with obvious offense. “You are a low-level cave boy. Cave boys should not look back through cross-realm broadcast layers. That is against basic dramatic hierarchy.”
Mateo tilted his head. “…And yet.”
“And yet,” the Chihuahua repeated bitterly. “Here we are. You laughing. Me being disrespected. Reality losing standards.”
Mateo let out a quiet breath that almost became another laugh. “…You started it.”
The spirit gasped. “I gave you a gesture of divine contempt. That is not the same thing.”
“…You gave me the middle finger.”
“Yes,” said the Chihuahua with dignity. “A sacred sign of administrative displeasure.”
Mateo looked deeper into the darkness of the passage, then back toward the impossible source of the voice. “…You’re ridiculous.”
“And you’re in a cave bleeding on old stones while talking to a cosmic event host. Let’s not become judgmental.”
A brief pause settled between them. Then Mateo said, as casually as if commenting on weather, “…You’re basically my child.”
The Chihuahua stopped moving.
Silence.
“…I’m sorry, what?”
Mateo shrugged faintly. “You’re loud, structurally unnecessary, and somehow still functional. That sounds like something I would make.”
The spirit drew itself up. “I am not made.”
Mateo looked at it. “…That’s exactly what something made would say.”
“I am sovereign.”
“That too.”
“I am an independent, self-governing, multirealm event management entity.”
“…That sounds worse, actually.”
The Chihuahua pointed accusingly. “You are being deeply rude to someone whose broadcast budget exceeds your current realm.”
Mateo considered that. “…That does sound expensive.”
“It is expensive.”
“…Did you expense the fireworks?”
“I did.”
“…To who?”
The Chihuahua hesitated. “…That is not your concern.”
“That means no one approved them.”
“That means vision should not be constrained by bureaucracy.”
Mateo’s mouth twitched. “…That definitely sounds like something I would say.”
The spirit narrowed its eyes. “I have no idea what you used to say.”
Mateo looked at it.
The Chihuahua looked away.
“…You know enough,” Mateo said.
“I do not.”
“You do.”
“I know nothing. I am innocent. Uninformed. Purely administrative.”
Mateo studied it for a moment. “…The more specific you get, the less convincing you become.”
The Chihuahua huffed and changed the subject with all the grace of someone falling down stairs in denial. “Well. Since you are apparently capable of noticing things you should not notice, I suppose I can allow you a slightly better explanation than everyone else is getting.”
Mateo’s eyes narrowed slightly. “…Generous.”
“It is. You should appreciate it more.”
Mateo didn’t answer immediately. Instead, he leaned lightly against the cave wall and looked at the spirit as if measuring whether honesty was worth the effort. The Chihuahua noticed and immediately became suspicious.
“…Why are you looking at me like that?”
“I’m deciding whether to answer the next question honestly.”
“…That has never led to anything good.”
“That’s not true.”
“For who?”
Mateo ignored that. The Chihuahua, unfortunately, had a point.
Then the spirit asked it anyway. “…Why did you do it?”
Mateo didn’t answer immediately.
The Chihuahua continued, voice quieter now, though it still tried very hard to sound casual. “If you were… if something like you truly had all that power—if you could shape structure, define worlds, rewrite possibility, and generally ruin the self-esteem of every living being by existing harder than they ever could—then why would you give it up?”
Mateo considered that. Then shrugged. “…It got boring.”
The Chihuahua froze. “…I’m sorry?”
“Everything worked,” Mateo said. “Too well. Nothing surprised me. Nothing pushed back. Nothing resisted long enough to become interesting.”
The spirit stared. “You had absolute authority.”
“Yes.”
“You could make and unmake worlds.”
“Yes.”
“You could observe everything.”
“Yes.”
The Chihuahua blinked. “…And your conclusion was ‘this lacks entertainment value’?”
Mateo nodded once. “…Basically.”
“That is the most irresponsible possible use of omnipotence.”
“It was also accurate.”
The spirit looked offended on behalf of existence itself. “There are beings across higher realms who would destroy lineages just to gain a fraction of that level of control.”
“Yes,” Mateo said. “That’s part of why they shouldn’t have it.”
The Chihuahua paused. “…That is annoyingly reasonable.”
“It usually is.”
“But you didn’t just step away from power,” the spirit said, narrowing its eyes. “You reset yourself. You collapsed your own certainty. Why?”
Mateo looked toward the passage for a moment before answering. “Because certainty is static.”
The Chihuahua blinked once. “…That sounded more serious than I was prepared for.”
Mateo ignored that. “If every outcome is already visible, then choice stops meaning anything. If nothing can oppose you, then nothing reveals you to yourself. I didn’t want worship. I didn’t want perfect control. I didn’t want another era of being the largest thing in a sealed place, watching everything become repetitive. I wanted consequence.”
The Chihuahua tilted its head. “…That sounds dangerously close to wisdom.”
“It’s mostly preference.”
“And here I thought it was a cosmic crisis.”
“It can be both.”
The spirit folded its tiny arms. “…You dismantled godlike certainty because you wanted to be wrong.”
Mateo nodded. “…Being wrong is useful.”
“That is not a sentence powerful beings are supposed to say.”
“That’s why most of them stay boring.”
The Chihuahua looked horrified. “You are impossible.”
“I was worse before.”
“I do not doubt that for a second.”
Mateo’s smile widened slightly.
The spirit squinted at him. “…Also, for the record, if I were something you created—which I am absolutely not admitting, because I do not know what you were and certainly do not have old structural records I am metaphorically standing on—then I would like it noted that I turned out extremely well.”
Mateo looked it over slowly. “You’re wearing sandals while smoking a pipe that blows bubbles.”
“It’s called style.”
“It’s called evidence.”
The Chihuahua pointed at him. “I am memorable.”
“You are unstable.”
“I am charming.”
“You are administratively dangerous.”
The spirit paused. “…That is the nicest thing anyone has ever said to me.”
Mateo looked almost thoughtful. “…That’s unfortunate.”
The Chihuahua ignored that entirely. “In any case, whether or not you are some suspiciously calm cave child with impossible eyes and structurally offensive familiarity, I am still the one running the realm opening.”
“So this entire thing was your idea?”
“Yes.”
“The sandals too.”
“…Yes.”
“The bubbles.”
“They create atmosphere.”
“They create confusion.”
“Atmosphere through confusion.”
Mateo nodded once. “…That also sounds like something I would approve of.”
The Chihuahua stared at him. “…I don’t know whether that’s praise or a warning.”
“Yes,” said Mateo.
The spirit narrowed its eyes. “I dislike how often that answer works for you.”
“It keeps being sufficient.”
The Chihuahua sighed the way only a cosmic being burdened by impossible administrative absurdity could sigh. “Well, since you are apparently capable of noticing things you should not notice, I suppose I can allow you a slightly better explanation than everyone else is getting.”
Mateo’s eyes narrowed just slightly. “…That seems generous.”
“It is,” the spirit replied. “You should appreciate it more.”
Then, without warning, its tone shifted—not into seriousness exactly, because seriousness was not natural to it, but into something larger, more deliberate, as though it were speaking from a stage too vast for ordinary thinking.
“The realm opening,” it said, “is not a single realm.”
The cave seemed to listen.
“It is many realms. Many worlds. Many structures. Some were made. Some were found. Some were stolen, improved, repurposed, abandoned, remembered, forgotten, or all of those at once. Some are tiny. Some are larger than nations. Some are folded over each other. Some only exist if entered correctly. Some reject entire bloodlines. Some change their rules the moment they realize someone has started understanding them. Some are trials. Some are economies. Some are wars pretending to be games. Some are games pretending not to be wars.”
Mateo remained silent.
The Chihuahua continued. “Long ago, there was… something. A being. One that could see too much, understand too much, do too much. It could shape realities the way normal creatures shape intention. It could witness worlds without crossing them. Build laws. Break laws. Refine them. Remove them. Rearrange possibility until even impossibility became administrative.”
A pause drifted through the cave.
“But power has a sense of humor,” the spirit said lightly. “The more complete that being became, the less any ordinary world could hold it. Too much awareness. Too much force. Too much structure in one place. It became trapped—not by chains, not by enemies, not by any external prison, but by the sheer fact that it had become too great to fit anywhere except a sealed existence of its own making.”
Mateo’s expression did not visibly change.
The Chihuahua, very deliberately, did not look directly at him when it said the next part.
“So the sealed place remained. Not dead. Not fully alive. Just… there. A prison formed from excess and intention. And inside or around or adjacent to that structure—depending on which version of the truth you prefer—many lesser worlds, trials, spaces, and game-like domains came into being.”
The spirit took a small drag from the pipe in its mouth and blew a stream of bubbles into the air. They floated briefly, then vanished into folded space.
“That,” it said, “is the realm opening.”
Mateo finally spoke. “…A prison.”
The Chihuahua sniffed. “A prison is a very unfriendly word for what is, in many cases, a truly excellent collection of opportunities. Also yes.”
The spirit’s voice sharpened again. “There are worlds inside it where cultivation behaves differently from anything your current realm accepts. Some allow no spiritual energy at all and force everyone who enters to begin as mortals, stripped of everything except instinct, memory, and whether they panic attractively. Some are race-locked, meaning if you enter incorrectly you may emerge with scales, fur, wings, or deeply inconvenient social obligations. Some are point-based. Some are accumulation-based. Some are ranked by survival. Some are ranked by style.”
Mateo blinked once. “…Style?”
“Yes,” the Chihuahua said firmly. “Dignity matters in certain dimensions.”
The cave remained quiet.
“Some worlds are perfectly serious death trials with points and rankings and reward systems. Some are dungeon realms with shifting floors, hidden bosses, contribution tallies, and participants who believe teamwork means stealing from one another politely. Some are hunt worlds where beasts are the points. Some are herb worlds where enough ignorance can get you eaten by medicinal plants. Which, to be fair, is usually deserved.”
It drifted slightly lower as it warmed to the explanation.
“Some worlds are games. Actual games. Strategy games. Survival games. Hide-and-seek worlds. Racing worlds. Tactical siege worlds. Funny games. Horrible games. Games that begin funny and stop being funny very quickly. Some worlds are made for fighters, yes—but not all of them. There are worlds for blacksmiths where spiritual metals only reveal themselves through proper forging logic. Worlds for healers where preserving a single thread of life matters more than winning. Worlds for alchemists where ingredient compatibility changes every hour. Worlds for rune makers where structure itself is the enemy.”
The Chihuahua paused, then added with grave seriousness, “Some are even cooking worlds, though personally I consider those cruel.”
Mateo let out a soft breath through his nose. “…Of course there are.”
“Of course,” the spirit said. “Why should existence limit itself now, after being absurd for so long?”
Its ears twitched. “There is also a racing realm.”
Mateo looked at it.
The Chihuahua lifted its chin. “An important one.”
“…Go on.”
“Not because I need encouragement,” the spirit muttered, “but because your attitude is finally appropriate. Yes. There is a race realm. A vast convergence road-realm with multiple route types, shifting lanes, infrastructure anomalies, weapon zones, terrain hazards, and extremely poor decision-making.”
Mateo paused. “…What do they race?”
The Chihuahua looked scandalized. “Everything.”
“Define everything.”
“With pleasure. Spirit carriages pulled by wind-bred stag-beasts. Sword-riders standing on broad formation blades barely above the road. Thunder bikes—single-rider lightning frames mounted on rune wheels. Beast-sleds dragged by moonfang wolves. Hovering palanquins that are technically cheating but only slightly. Broomsticks.”
Mateo blinked. “…Broomsticks.”
“Yes.”
A pause.
“…There is no dignity requirement for entry.”
“I assumed not.”
The Chihuahua continued anyway, now clearly enjoying itself. “There are armored road-cars shaped from spirit wood and jade-steel, driven by formation cores. There are chariot-cycles with one wheel in front and two behind, designed by people who fear death but not enough. There are floating swordboards, thunder skiffs, bone-wheeled desert crawlers, cloud-goat wagons, ritual sedan racers, and at least one sect that insists on racing mounted bathtub formations because they claim instability produces enlightenment.”
Mateo was silent for a long moment. “…That sounds fake.”
“It is painfully real.”
The spirit pointed a paw. “And the rules matter. Vehicles must touch the road or hover only slightly above it. No proper flying. No leaving the track entirely unless the track itself decides to leave first. There are multiple route branches, false shortcuts, trap roads, collapsing bridges, and sections where the road judges intent, which is extremely rude.”
“Weapons?” Mateo asked.
“Oh yes,” the Chihuahua said immediately. “Bananas, obviously.”
Mateo stared.
The spirit stared back. “Do not underestimate the banana art.”
A beat.
“Also freeze talismans, temporary axle-lock arrays, teleport switching, smoke curtains, illusion flags, binding chains, wheel-slip sigils, beast-bait scent bombs, and lane redirection plates that convince your opponent the road is somewhere else. Works even on floating vehicles. Very funny.”
Mateo looked almost thoughtful. “…That actually does sound interesting.”
The Chihuahua pointed again. “I knew it. That realm is excellent.”
“It sounds irresponsible.”
“It is beautifully irresponsible.”
The spirit drifted back, bubbles trailing behind its pipe. “Anyway. There are worlds of mythical herbs, where entire valleys shine with living medicine and every plant has enough instinct to resent harvesting. There are spirit marshes where ghost-lotuses bloom beneath moonless skies. Forge worlds where mountains bleed ore. Beast plains where creatures large enough to flatten cities wander around considering whether your bones are worth chewing. There are cities that only exist during certain alignments, forests that move when watched, lakes that reflect other realms, towers that reward failure correctly interpreted, and ruins that can only be opened by people too stubborn to know they should have left.”
Mateo’s gaze shifted slightly toward the passage ahead.
That last one felt pointed.
The spirit continued anyway. “Each world has rules. Some are obvious. Most are not. Some reward force. Some punish it. Some favor talent. Some absolutely despise it. Some elevate the weak if they adapt correctly and bury the strong if they arrive with too much certainty. A few are simply mean. Some adjust to group size. Some don’t allow alliances. Some permit alliances and then design the environment specifically to make those alliances inconvenient.”
The voice softened just enough to become almost thoughtful. “And all of them were shaped by a place born from too much power belonging to one being in one sealed space for too long. That kind of pressure changes things. Leaves traces. Makes oddities. Creates wonders. Creates monsters. Creates opportunity.”
Then, abruptly, the Chihuahua’s normal nonsense returned. “So naturally, I turned it into a six-month event.”
Mateo actually laughed again. “…Naturally.”
“You say that like you question my management.”
“I question all of your management.”
“Very rude,” said the spirit. “And impressively accurate.”
For a moment, the cave felt less oppressive.
Then the pressure beneath the floor pulsed faintly again, reminding both of them that the world below had not stopped existing simply because ridiculous explanations were being offered above it.
Mateo’s expression settled. “…And what happens if people die in those worlds?”
The Chihuahua took a thoughtful pause. “That depends on the world. Some expel you. Some keep score. Some remove only what entered. Some return you altered. Some consider death a lesson. A few consider it a prerequisite.”
A beat.
“Very educational realms.”
“And all of them open in six months?”
“Yes.”
“And everyone was invited.”
“Yes.”
“That seems irresponsible.”
The Chihuahua sounded offended. “It is inclusive.”
Mateo let the silence answer for him.
The spirit ignored that. “In any case, none of this changes your immediate problem. You are still in a cave. There is still something old beneath you. And despite your current injuries, I suspect you are going deeper anyway.”
Mateo glanced toward the narrow opening in the wall, where colder air drifted from below. “…Yes.”
“Of course you are.”
The Chihuahua’s voice shifted once more, playful again. “Well then. Try not to die before the event. It would be tremendously inconvenient for the narrative.”
“Is that concern?”
“No,” said the spirit. “That is scheduling.”
Then it vanished.
Completely this time.
No bubbles.
No pressure.
No trace, except the lingering sense that something impossible had just finished treating the laws of reality like suggestions.
The cave returned. The silence returned. The old air remained.
Mateo stood there for a few moments without moving, letting the shape of the explanation settle where it could. A realm born from excess. Many worlds inside it. Games, trials, craft domains, death spaces, cultivation anomalies, race realms with broomsticks and banana warfare. A prison, a treasury, a battlefield, a joke.
And somehow—
He believed it.
Not because it was reasonable.
Because too much of it felt structurally true.
His fingers brushed lightly against the pendant hidden beneath his clothing. It pulsed once, warmer now, as if the mention of all those worlds had touched something buried within it.
“Interesting,” he said softly.
Then he turned fully toward the opened passage. The stone ahead narrowed into darkness, but not natural darkness. This one felt built, layered, patient. The faint cold rising from below no longer carried only age. It carried attention. Waiting.
Mateo adjusted his breathing, then his stance, letting the injuries in his side and shoulder settle into the kind of awareness that could be moved with rather than ignored. The herbs within the cube rested quietly, separated and stable. His body was not fully recovered.
It was enough.
He stepped forward.
The passage beyond the wall did not descend cleanly. It bent, split, and narrowed, the stone changing from rough cave structure into something more deliberate. Dust lay thick in some places and absent in others. The farther he went, the more the space stopped feeling abandoned and started feeling… arranged.
The cave was over.
Whatever this place was, it had begun.
And now, without chihuahuas, fireworks, impossible jokes, or race realms involving judgmental broomsticks to distract him, Mateo did what he always did when faced with something hidden, old, and potentially catastrophic.
He went deeper.
