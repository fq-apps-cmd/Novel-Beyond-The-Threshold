Chapter 6 — A Moment Between Moments

Later — after the yard had emptied, after Diego had wandered off muttering about food, after Tomas had flexed his healed arm three times as though testing whether the fix would hold and then once more because three had not been convincing enough — Mateo sat alone at the edge of the training ground.
The village continued around him in the specific rhythm that followed disruption — not the normal rhythm, but the one that pretended to be. The sounds were the same. The movements were the same. But the texture had changed, the way fabric changed after being stretched and released — it returned to shape, but the threads remembered.
Somewhere to the east, the blacksmith's hammer struck in its uneven rhythm — three close together, a pause, then two more. The pause was shorter than usual today, as though the smith was working faster to compensate for the time he had lost watching the confrontation from his open forge. He had not left his station during the exchange — his position did not allow for casual departure — but Mateo had noticed the hammer go silent during the apex predator moment, and it had not resumed for several seconds afterward.
The smith had felt it.
He didn't know what he had felt. But he had stopped working, and stopping mid-strike was something the smith did not do unless something in his environment demanded his attention more urgently than heated metal.
A child laughed too loudly somewhere to the north and was immediately shushed — the sharp, reflexive correction of a parent who was more on edge than usual and whose threshold for noise had contracted accordingly. The laugh cut off mid-note, replaced by a brief, wounded silence, and then the quieter sounds of a child recalibrating their behavior without understanding why the rules had changed.
The smell of evening cooking drifted from a home two paths over — something with ginger and too much smoke, the kind of meal that was assembled from whatever remained in the kitchen rather than from deliberate selection. It was comfort food. The kind people made when the day had been harder than expected and the body wanted something familiar rather than something good.
Mateo did not notice any of it.
He was looking at his hands.
Not the way he usually did — cataloging, assessing, filing observations for later use. This was different. This was the quiet, uncomfortable act of confronting something he had not expected to feel.
He had almost not held back.
Not during the first exchange. The first exchange had been clean. Controlled. Simple. The kind of mechanical adjustment that required no more thought than breathing. The outsider had attacked, and Mateo had responded the way water responded to a stone — by flowing around it, by being elsewhere, by making the obstacle irrelevant through the simple refusal to be where it expected him.
That had been easy.
That had been nothing.
But the second exchange —
When the outsider had pushed past sparring into something personal, something fueled by wounded pride and the desperate need to reclaim what had been taken from him publicly —
Mateo had felt something shift.
Not in his technique. Not in his awareness. In his patience.
The patience was not a personality trait. It was not the natural temperament of a calm person or the practiced composure of someone who had learned to manage their emotions. It was a structure — a deliberately constructed framework that Mateo maintained between what he was capable of and what he chose to do. It existed the way a dam existed between a reservoir and the valley below — not as a passive barrier, but as an active decision, maintained continuously, requiring constant attention that became invisible through habit but never became unnecessary.
And for a fraction of a breath — less than that, a sliver of a moment so thin it barely qualified as time — the dam had cracked.
He had not been a boy in a training yard choosing to hold back.
He had been something else.
Something that did not hold back.
Something that responded to challenge the way gravity responded to mass — automatically, completely, and without the possibility of restraint.
The outsider's wrist. The angle of the incoming strike. The precise amount of force required to not merely redirect it but to dismantle the arm that delivered it, to fold the joint past its structural limit, to separate tendon from bone at the exact point where the body's architecture was most vulnerable to lateral force, to end the exchange so absolutely that the concept of "again" would cease to exist as a possibility.
He had seen all of it.
Not as options.
As inevitabilities.
The way a mathematician saw the answer to an equation before completing the proof — not because the proof was unnecessary, but because the conclusion was already visible in the structure of the problem itself. The outsider's arm, extended past the point of balanced recovery, existed in a state where damage was not a possibility but a geometric certainty. All that was required was the application of force along the correct vector, at the correct magnitude, at the correct moment.
And Mateo's body had known the vector.
Had known the magnitude.
Had known the moment.
And for that sliver of a breath, the only thing standing between the outsider's functional arm and something far worse had been a choice that Mateo was not entirely certain he had made consciously.
That was the part that unsettled him.
Not the power. He understood the power. He had always understood it — had carried it, wielded it, and ultimately abandoned it because understanding alone had not been enough to make it meaningful. Power without context was noise. Power without limitation was irrelevance. He had learned that lesson across spans of time that this world could not measure, and the learning had been thorough enough that he had dismantled himself rather than continue existing as an answer without a question.
What unsettled him was the speed of the instinct.
He had chosen this life. He had chosen limitation. He had chosen to exist within boundaries that would force him to grow the way others grew — through effort, through failure, through the slow accumulation of experience that could not be shortcut. He had chosen to feel the weight of bone and the constraint of flesh and the frustration of a body that could not do what the mind behind it had once done without thinking.
He had chosen all of that.
But the instinct did not care about his choices.
It lived beneath them. Older than this body. Older than this world. Woven into whatever remained of what he had been before he fell, and it surfaced not when he was thinking, not when he was deciding, but in the space between — the gap where thought hadn't arrived yet and something deeper filled the silence.
It was the reflex of something vast trying to protect something small, without understanding that the something small was the point.
If Tomas had not stepped in —
Mateo exhaled slowly.
He would have held back.
Probably.
Almost certainly.
...Most likely.
The fact that he could not say "definitely" was the problem.
He sat with that for a long time. Not analyzing it — not yet. Just sitting with it the way one sat with a wound before deciding how to treat it. Acknowledging its presence. Measuring its depth. Understanding that it existed before attempting to understand what it meant.
The training yard had emptied fully now. The grooves in the dirt had already begun to soften at their edges, dust settling back into the spaces that feet had carved out hours before. The shallow arcs of Mateo's footwork were still visible — precise, evenly spaced, documenting the geometry of his movement with the archaeological accuracy that packed earth maintained whether anyone asked it to or not. The outsiders' footprints were there too — deeper, less organized, the marks of people who had been moving with force rather than precision.
And Tomas's prints, between them. Steady. Grounded. The marks of someone who had placed himself exactly where he needed to be.
Mateo looked at those prints for a moment.
Then at his hands one more time.
He closed his eyes and let the evening air settle against his skin. The ground beneath him was cool, still holding the impressions of the day's training. Somewhere nearby, a bird called once and did not repeat itself — a single note that existed briefly in the air and then dissolved, as though the bird had made its point and saw no reason to elaborate.
This is why I chose this.
The thought surfaced clearly, without the usual analytical distance. Not processed. Not filtered. Just present.
Not to be powerful again. Not to prove anything. Not to observe from above.
To be close enough to the edge that the choice to step back actually costs something.
Because a choice that costs nothing is not a choice. It is a default. And defaults do not grow. They do not teach. They do not force the kind of reckoning that transforms understanding from theory into something that lives in the body rather than the mind.
He had chosen to be small enough that the instinct to be vast could challenge him.
And today, for the first time, the challenge had been real.
He opened his eyes.
The training yard was empty. The light had shifted from the warm gold of late afternoon to the cooler tones that preceded evening, and the shadows cast by the surrounding trees had lengthened across the dirt until they merged into a single, continuous shade that turned the yard from a sunlit space into something quieter and less defined.
He looked at his hands one more time.
Then he stood.
I need to be more careful.
A pause.
Not with them.
With myself.
He rolled his shoulders once, letting the tension leave unevenly, and walked toward the village path.
The thought stayed with him.
Not as fear.
As respect.
For the thing he carried that did not yet fully understand it was supposed to be sleeping.
The walk back through the village was slower than necessary.
Not because Mateo was tired — though his body carried the residual tension of the afternoon's exchanges in ways that would not fully dissipate until sleep. But because the village at this hour existed in a state of transition that revealed things the daytime rhythm concealed.
The vendors were closing their stalls along the market path, each one performing the same ritual they performed every evening — covering goods, securing displays, sweeping the ground in front of their spaces with the automatic efficiency of motions repeated thousands of times. The cloth merchant was folding his remaining bolts with a precision that bordered on aggressive, each fold sharp enough to suggest he was working through a frustration that had nothing to do with fabric. The pot seller beside him was stacking her wares in silence, which was unusual — normally the two of them exchanged their daily parting commentary, a final verbal exchange that served as the closing bracket to whatever argument the morning had opened.
Tonight, neither spoke.
Mateo noticed.
Further along, a group of older villagers stood near the well, their conversation low enough that the words were indistinct but their body language was not. They stood closer together than casual conversation required. Their gazes moved outward periodically — toward the training yard, toward the path the outsiders had used, toward the forest edge — before returning to each other. The conversation was not about what had happened. It was about what might happen next.
The village had felt the outsiders' presence the way a body felt a bruise — not immediately, not at the point of impact, but afterward, when the area was touched and the sensitivity revealed itself.
A woman Mateo recognized — the one who sold preserved vegetables near the herb stall — was standing in her doorway, arms folded, watching the path with the unfocused attention of someone who was not looking at anything specific but was looking for something in general. When Mateo passed, her gaze settled on him briefly. Her expression carried something that had not been there that morning — not fear, not anger, but the specific quality of awareness that arrived when a person's understanding of their environment had been updated against their preference.
She nodded at him.
Mateo nodded back.
Neither spoke.
But the exchange communicated more than words would have. She had seen the confrontation. She had seen the outsiders. She had seen Mateo stand in the center of the yard and say two words that had ended everything. And she was still processing what that meant about the boy she had watched walk past her stall every morning for years.
Mateo continued walking.
The village's evening sounds reassembled around him as he moved — doors closing, low conversations resuming, the clink of bowls being set on tables, the creak of wood as homes settled into the cooling air. Somewhere to the south, Diego's voice was audible — not the words, just the cadence, rapid and animated, directed at someone who was probably not interested but who would listen anyway because Diego's voice was not the kind that permitted disengagement.
The normalcy was deliberate. The village was choosing to be normal the way a person chose to breathe calmly after being startled — not because the calm was real, but because the alternative was worse.
Mateo understood that.
He had been doing the same thing all day.
A Small, Quiet Problem
That night, Mateo stood outside the herb shop.
The edge of the village blended directly into the forest here. No walls. No clear separation. Just less light, thicker shadow, and a shift in the air that suggested stepping further meant stepping into something else entirely. The darkness didn't deepen uniformly — some areas held shape, their edges defined by the faint ambient light that leaked from nearby homes, while others seemed to absorb it, creating pockets of shadow so complete that the ground beneath them was invisible.
The shop itself was a modest structure — wider than most homes but shorter, with a low-pitched roof designed to keep the interior cool and dry. The walls were timber, darkened by years of exposure to the medicinal vapors that old man Chen's preparations produced. Even from outside, the scent was detectable — a complex layering of dried herbs, ground minerals, preserved roots, and something sharp and faintly acrid that Mateo suspected was the binding agent Chen used in his more expensive preparations.
The shop was closed.
"Didn't sell to you earlier, did they?" Diego whispered.
He had appeared approximately ten minutes ago, materializing from the direction of his home with the casual stealth of someone who had been waiting for exactly this opportunity and had been tracking Mateo's movements since dinner. His arrival had not been announced. His presence had simply become a fact, the way Diego's presence always became a fact — suddenly, completely, and without any clear mechanism of transition.
"...No."
"...So now we're here."
Mateo studied the door. The latch was old iron, the kind that looked sturdy from the outside but had been opened and closed so many times the mechanism barely held alignment anymore. The metal had worn smooth at the contact points, and the housing had loosened in its mount — not enough to be visually obvious, but enough that someone who understood how latches worked could feel the play in the mechanism from the outside.
Mateo understood how latches worked.
"...Old man Chen refused the sale," Mateo said, his tone calm and entirely unbothered. "He said I was 'too young to be purchasing materials above my comprehension.'"
Diego stifled a laugh. "He said that? To you?"
"He said it loudly. Then he turned to the next customer and sold them the exact same thing without asking a single question."
"...So he's just —"
"Difficult," Mateo said. "Deliberately and consistently difficult. He also overcharges anyone under twenty, lectures customers who ask too many questions, and once refused to sell someone a basic healing root because they mispronounced its name."
Diego pressed his hand over his mouth.
Mateo continued, his expression perfectly neutral.
"He'll survive missing a few supplies."
Diego's shoulders were shaking now. "...You've been keeping track of all of this?"
"...Observation is a habit."
"That's not observation. That's a grudge list."
"...It's a well-organized grudge list. There's a difference."
Diego wheezed quietly. Mateo's mouth twitched — just barely, just at the corner — but it was there. The faintest crack in the composure. Around them, the night sounds of the village continued undisturbed — crickets in the undergrowth, the distant murmur of a conversation two homes over, the faint creak of the forest canopy adjusting to the evening wind.
"Stay quiet," Mateo said.
"...Wait, are we actually —"
Mateo was already moving.
The window latch was simple. Poorly secured. He had noticed the weakness three visits ago when old man Chen had been too busy lecturing a woman about the correct way to store dried bark to notice Mateo studying every entry point in the building. The window sat at the building's western face, partially concealed by an overgrown shrub that Chen had clearly never bothered to trim — either because he didn't care about aesthetics or because the shrub provided cover for the window that he didn't realize needed covering.
Mateo slipped through with the economy of someone who had practiced entry through narrow spaces — not because he had planned this specific visit, but because understanding how to enter and exit spaces efficiently was, in his view, a fundamental skill that most people neglected.
Predictable.
Within moments, he was inside.
The shop's interior was darker than the outside, the single window providing the only source of ambient light. Shelves lined three walls, each one organized according to a system that old man Chen insisted was intuitive and that everyone else considered incomprehensible. Bundles of dried herbs hung from the ceiling beams in rows, their shapes casting strange shadows that moved faintly in the draft from the open window. The air was thick with the accumulated scent of years of herbal preparation — layer upon layer of medicinal compounds that had soaked into the wood and the stone and the clay jars until the building itself smelled like a remedy.
Despite the apparent chaos of the organization, Mateo could see the logic. Chen arranged by interaction properties rather than by type — herbs that should not be stored near each other were separated by maximum distance, while those that benefited from proximity were clustered. It was not intuitive. But it was correct. And the fact that Chen understood herb interaction at that level while simultaneously being the most difficult merchant in the village was, Mateo had to admit, somewhat impressive.
He would never tell Chen that.
Diego followed, landing with significantly less grace and immediately bumping a low shelf. A clay jar wobbled. Diego lunged for it. Caught it. Set it back. Exhaled. The jar, unimpressed by its near-death experience, continued existing.
"...This is a terrible idea," Diego whispered.
"...Yes."
"...We're still doing it?"
"...Yes."
"Think of it this way," Mateo said, moving between shelves with the kind of precision that suggested he had already mapped the interior layout from his previous visits. His hands selected specific bundles without hesitation — dried stems from the second shelf, chosen for their concentration and freshness rather than their size. A sealed jar from behind a row of lesser-quality roots, its wax seal intact, the contents protected from the air exposure that had degraded the jars in front of it. A small cloth pouch tucked into a spot that old man Chen clearly thought was hidden behind a stack of low-grade mineral powder — the kind of hiding spot that worked only against people who did not understand that the most valuable items were always stored behind the least valuable ones.
"He spent twenty minutes yesterday explaining to a farmer that his cough medicine was 'an art form' and then charged him triple."
Diego, crouched behind a barrel he didn't need to be hiding behind — the barrel provided no actual concealment from any angle, but Diego seemed to find comfort in the principle of the thing — let out a sharp breath through his nose. "...You're robbing him out of principle."
"...I'm redistributing resources from someone who hoards them behind unnecessary hostility."
"That's robbery."
"...That's perspective."
Diego pressed both hands over his face. His entire body was vibrating with suppressed laughter — the specific full-body tremor of someone who was trying to be silent and whose body was refusing to cooperate. Mateo glanced over at him, and for a moment — just a moment — the composure broke. A grin spread across his face, sharp and unhidden, the kind of expression that belonged on someone who was enjoying this far more than any reasonable person should.
It was the grin of someone who had spent the afternoon confronting the terrifying depth of what lived inside him and had decided that the appropriate response was to rob an herb shop with his best friend.
The logic was questionable.
The therapy was effective.
"...He's going to blame the wind," Mateo said.
Diego lost it.
A muffled, strangled laugh escaped through his fingers, and Mateo had to physically turn away because the grin on his face had widened into something that would have horrified every elder in the village if they could see it. His shoulders shook once — a single, involuntary tremor of laughter that he suppressed immediately but not before Diego saw it and pointed at him with the triumphant energy of someone who had just witnessed a rare natural phenomenon.
"You laughed," Diego whispered. "You actually —"
"...That did not happen."
"It happened. I saw it. Your shoulders moved."
"...Wind."
"We are indoors."
"...Indoor wind."
"That is not a thing."
"...It is now."
Diego pressed both hands over his mouth again, his eyes streaming. Mateo, whose composure had technically recovered but whose eyes still carried the brightness of someone who was one comment away from losing it again, focused very deliberately on the shelves.
"...We are terrible people," Diego managed.
"...Yes," Mateo said, carefully replacing a jar he had moved to access the one behind it, ensuring nothing looked disturbed. "...But he started it."
They slipped back out through the window. The night air hit them with the cool clarity that the shop's enclosed, herb-saturated atmosphere had been lacking, and the transition felt like surfacing from underwater — the sudden expansion of awareness as the senses readjusted to open space.
Diego wiped his eyes.
"...If anyone asks —"
"...We were never here."
"...Right."
A pause.
"...He's definitely going to blame the wind."
"...Absolutely."
They walked back in silence, though the silence was occasionally broken by Diego's shoulders shaking and Mateo's suspiciously neutral expression that wasn't fooling either of them. The path back to the village center was lit only by the ambient glow from nearby homes and the faint starlight that filtered through gaps in the canopy — enough to see the ground, enough to navigate, not enough to read expressions.
Which was convenient.
Because Mateo's expression, had it been visible, would have told a story that contradicted every piece of carefully maintained composure he had constructed throughout the day.
He was smiling.
Not the small, controlled smile he permitted during conversations. Not the faint acknowledgment of amusement that he occasionally allowed to surface. A real smile. The kind that existed because the person wearing it was genuinely, uncomplicated happy — not about the herbs, not about outsmarting Chen, but about the simple, irreplaceable fact of walking through a village at night with someone who made the world feel less heavy.
Diego did not see the smile.
But he would have understood it.
No wasted motion.
Except for the part where they both nearly lost composure behind a barrel.
That was entirely wasted.
And entirely worth it.
Back under the open sky, Mateo held the pendant again.
He sat on the low wall behind his family's home, the same spot where he had sat on dozens of evenings before — the stone worn smooth by use, the surface cool against his legs, the position offering a clear view of the sky through the widest gap in the canopy. The village had settled into its final rhythm of the day — the deep quiet that came after the evening meals were finished and the conversations had run their course and the only sounds that remained were the ones the night itself produced.
The pendant pulsed.
Warmer now.
The warmth felt more consistent than before — not stronger, but more aligned. As though the pendant's energy, which had previously fluctuated between activity and dormancy, had found a frequency it preferred and was maintaining it with increasing confidence. The pulse arrived at regular intervals — not the rapid, urgent beat of something demanding attention, but the slow, steady rhythm of something that was settling into a pattern it intended to keep.
Something is aligning.
The thought felt closer this time. More defined. Not the vague sense of something happening that he had carried for weeks, but a specific observation — the fragments within the pendant were moving. Not randomly. Not chaotically. Toward each other. Toward a configuration that Mateo could almost perceive but could not yet name.
Not broken.
A pause.
Becoming.
He closed his hand around it.
The warmth intensified slightly at the contact — responding to his touch the way it always did, but with a consistency that suggested the response was becoming more reliable. Less intermittent. More connected.
This world is only the beginning.
The thought arrived with a clarity that surprised him. Not because the content was new — he had always known, on some level, that the mortal world was the lowest rung of a ladder that extended far beyond what anyone here could perceive. But the knowing had been abstract. Distant. The kind of knowledge that existed in the mind without touching the body.
Tonight, it touched.
And for the first time —
That didn't feel distant.
It felt real.
