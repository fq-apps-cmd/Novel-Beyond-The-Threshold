"...Wait."
The word came from Elder Marcos, his expression narrowing as he studied Mateo more carefully. He had been allowing the exchange to proceed — letting the other elders react, measuring Mateo's responses against their questions the way a craftsman measured a joint against a frame. But the boy's composure had not cracked. Not once. And that, more than anything Mateo had said, was what made Elder Marcos lean forward.
"...On whose authority are you conducting this inspection?"
Mateo paused.
He did not turn immediately. Instead, he remained facing the door, his hands still folded behind his back, his posture carrying the specific stillness of someone who had been waiting for exactly this question and was now deciding how much of the answer to share.
"Oh crap," thought Tomas, who could see the back of Mateo's head and recognized, with the sinking certainty of experience, the particular angle of his shoulders that preceded something deeply inadvisable.
Now they are uncertain, Mateo thought to himself while suppressing a grin. The question itself confirms it. If they were certain of their authority, they would not be asking about mine. They would be exercising theirs. The fact that they are asking means the framework has already shifted — they are no longer operating from a position of control. They are negotiating. And they do not yet realize it.
When he did face them again, his expression remained calm.
"...Authority," he said slowly, "is often misunderstood. If a door can be opened, it is not truly closed. If information can be heard, then it is not truly controlled. If presence goes unnoticed..."
He allowed the sentence to trail.
The room felt unsteady for a moment. Not physically — but in certainty. The air held the specific tension of a group of experienced people confronting the possibility that a child had just described their security architecture more accurately than they could.
"...then the limitation only exists in theory."
The elders exchanged brief glances, uncertainty growing in a way that had not existed moments ago. Elder Kurosawa's fingers had steepled beneath his chin, and his gaze had shifted from Mateo to Elder Shen and back, as though triangulating something between the two of them that no one else in the room had noticed. Elder Doña Reyes's hands, which had been resting on the table with their usual patient weight, had gone perfectly still — the stillness of someone who was listening with more than her ears.
"...That still does not explain why you are here," Elder Park pressed. His tone was sharper than the others — the voice of someone who recognized that the conversation was being steered and was attempting to anchor it before it drifted further.
Mateo considered the question, then answered plainly.
"...Because no one stopped me."
That answer lingered longer than it should have. It hung in the room's adjusted acoustics — which were still not functioning properly, a fact that gave the words slightly more resonance than they deserved — and settled across the elders like a verdict delivered without a trial.
Behind him, Tomas closed his eyes briefly.
Oh god, why did I even follow him here. I could have stayed in the training yard. I could have eaten breakfast. I could have done anything other than stand in the doorway of a restricted meeting hall while Mateo systematically dismantles the self-confidence of nine people who could collectively flatten the village if they sneezed too hard.
"...Wait."
Elder Doña Reyes leaned forward. Her movement was unhurried — the deliberate motion of someone who had been listening longer than speaking and had decided that the moment required her specific contribution.
"...You are Rafael's boy."
The moment shifted. Not the conversation — the room itself. The mention of Rafael's name introduced a context that several elders had not yet connected, and the connections they now made were visible in the subtle adjustments of their expressions. Elder Tomás Aguilar's eyebrows rose slightly. Elder Vargas's frown deepened. Elder Hiro's jaw, which had been clenched for several minutes, tightened further — which should not have been physically possible but which his face managed anyway.
Mateo blinked once.
"...Correct."
"...You are not an inspector," Elder Marcos said.
"...Also correct."
"...Then what are you doing here?"
Mateo thought about it. Genuinely. Not the performative pause of someone preparing a clever answer, but the brief, honest consideration of someone deciding between several true responses and selecting the one that was most accurate.
Then answered honestly.
"...Testing something."
"...And the result?" Elder Park asked.
"...It works."
Elder Hiro, who had been growing steadily redder since Mateo first opened his mouth, finally reached his limit. His hair practically stood on end, and his hands gripped the edge of the table so tightly the wood creaked — a sound that was audible to everyone in the room and that the wood itself seemed to resent.
"...GET OUT," he said through clenched teeth, his voice vibrating with the restrained energy of a man who was actively choosing not to vault across the table and throttle a child in front of his colleagues. The restraint was commendable. The fact that it was visibly costing him years off his lifespan was less so.
Mateo nodded once without resistance.
"...That is reasonable."
He turned toward the door.
Then stopped.
Just barely. Just enough that the motion read as an afterthought rather than something planned, though Tomas, who had seen enough of Mateo's afterthoughts to know better, immediately felt his stomach drop with the specific gravity of someone watching a catastrophe develop in real time and being unable to prevent it.
"...One more thing," Mateo said.
Elder Hiro looked like he might actually combust. The vein on his forehead — which had appeared during the initial exchange and had been growing steadily more prominent — now pulsed with its own distinct rhythm, as though it had developed an independent circulatory system powered entirely by indignation.
Mateo turned back to face the room, his expression shifting into something that could only be described as professionally helpful. The kind of expression a merchant wore when they were about to sell you something you did not know you needed at a price you would regret. It was warm. It was approachable. It was, on every conceivable level, deeply suspicious.
"The formations in this room," he said, gesturing casually at the walls as though pointing out a stain on someone's shirt, "are misaligned. That is why your conversations are leaking. The sound suppression runes have drifted from their anchor points, and the spiritual dampening layer has developed gaps wide enough that even a Qi Gathering trainee standing outside could pick up fragments if they knew where to listen."
The room went very still.
Elder Liang, who had checked the wall moments ago and confirmed the misalignment with his own hands, opened his mouth, then closed it. The internal conflict was visible — the part of him that wanted to challenge the claim warring with the part of him that had already verified it independently. He could not argue. And the frustration of not being able to argue was written across his face in the specific handwriting of a man whose own evidence had betrayed him.
Elder Doña Reyes's eyes had sharpened. She was studying Mateo with the careful attention of someone who had just heard a child describe formation architecture with terminology that most cultivators twice his age did not possess — and who was now running through the list of possible explanations for that knowledge and finding none of them comfortable.
"...How do you know about formations?" Elder Vargas asked carefully, his tone carrying the measured caution of someone defusing something that might be explosive.
Mateo shrugged with the casual ease of someone discussing the weather.
"...I dabble."
"...You dabble," Elder Marcos repeated. The two words contained an entire essay on the inadequacy of that explanation.
"In runes and talismans. A bit of formation work. Nothing extensive." He paused, then added with the specific modesty of someone who was being neither modest nor specific, "Mostly self-taught. Some reading. Some observation."
The elders stared at him. Nine faces, nine expressions, nine variations on the same fundamental reaction: the deeply uncomfortable realization that the situation they were in had layers they had not anticipated.
Mateo continued, his tone shifting into something almost businesslike — the register of someone transitioning from conversation to proposal with the practiced smoothness of a vendor who had identified a customer's need and was now positioning the solution.
"I could fix it."
Silence.
"...Fix it," Elder Marcos repeated flatly.
"Realign the anchor points. Patch the dampening gaps. Reinforce the sound suppression lattice. I could also add a secondary layer, something of my own design — a unique formation that would not only seal conversations within the room but create a subtle feedback distortion for anyone attempting to listen from outside. They would hear something, but it would be nonsense. Fragments of old conversations. Ambient noise. Perhaps someone ordering lunch."
A brief, bewildered pause.
"...Someone ordering lunch?" Elder Park echoed, his expression caught between incredulity and the reluctant recognition that he was genuinely curious about the technical implementation.
"It is a design choice," Mateo said. "I find misdirection more effective than silence. Silence tells an eavesdropper something is being hidden. Nonsense tells them there is nothing worth hearing."
The logic was, infuriatingly, sound.
Elder Hiro, who had been ready to throw Mateo through the wall thirty seconds ago, now sat with his mouth slightly open, caught between rage and the deeply uncomfortable realization that the child who had just violated every protocol in the building was also offering the most practical security improvement anyone had suggested in years. His expression cycled through several stages — fury, confusion, reluctant interest, renewed fury at the reluctant interest — before settling into a grimace that communicated all of them simultaneously.
"...And what would this cost?" Elder Marcos asked, his tone careful in a way that suggested he already regretted asking and was asking anyway because the alternative — not knowing — was worse.
Mateo tilted his head, as if the question surprised him. As if the notion that a service might have a cost had only just occurred to him, rather than being the entire point of the exercise.
"...A small fee. Materials, mostly. A few spiritual inks. Some etching tools. Access to the formation base layer so I can integrate rather than override."
He paused.
"...And perhaps," he added, "a formal acknowledgment that my inspection was, in fact, useful."
Elder Hiro made a sound that was somewhere between a cough and a growl — the vocal equivalent of a man's dignity attempting to escape through his throat.
At the far end of the table, Elder Kurosawa had turned his face away from the group entirely, appearing to study the grain of the wooden wall with unprecedented academic interest. His shoulders were suspiciously still — the controlled stillness of someone who was investing significant physical effort into not shaking. Beside him, Elder Tomás Aguilar had raised a hand to his mouth and was coughing with a regularity that had no medical basis whatsoever. His eyes, above his hand, were bright with the specific strain of someone fighting a losing battle against laughter — and who had accepted the loss several seconds ago but was maintaining the performance out of professional obligation.
Elder Doña Reyes sat perfectly still, but the faintest crease had appeared at the corner of her mouth — the kind of expression that on a younger face would have been an open grin but on hers communicated something closer to deeply entertained recognition. She had seen many things in her decades on the council. She had not, until today, seen someone attempt to sell the elders a security upgrade for a vulnerability the seller had created. It was, she had to admit, elegant.
Elder Shen, still by the window, had not moved. But his gaze had shifted from the sky to Mateo, and it remained there now with the focused stillness of someone who had stopped observing casually and begun observing with intent. Whatever conclusion he was forming, it was not yet complete. But the foundation was being laid with each passing moment.
"...We will discuss this," Elder Marcos said finally. The words carried the specific weight of a man choosing to end a conversation not because it had been resolved but because continuing it would cause structural damage to the council's collective composure.
Mateo nodded.
"...Take your time. The formations will continue to degrade in the interim, but that is, of course, your decision."
Elder Hiro stood up.
Tomas grabbed Mateo by the shoulder and physically steered him toward the door with the urgent efficiency of someone evacuating a building that was about to collapse — which, in terms of Elder Hiro's restraint, was not entirely inaccurate.
"We are leaving," Tomas said.
"...I was already leaving."
"You were about to get us killed."
"...That seems like an overreaction."
The door closed behind them.
Inside, the elders sat in silence for a long moment. The kind of silence that followed an event that everyone had witnessed and no one was prepared to be the first to characterize.
Then Elder Marcos spoke.
"...Check every formation in this building. Now."
Chairs scraped against stone as the room erupted into motion. Elder Liang was already at the far wall, his palms pressed flat against the surface, extending his awareness into the stone with the focused urgency of someone who had just been told the foundation might be compromised. Elder Vargas moved toward the foundation stones near the entrance, crouching to examine the inscriptions with a brush he produced from his sleeve. Elder Doña Reyes remained seated but had closed her eyes, extending her awareness downward through the floor into the deeper formation layers that most of the council did not know how to access — the layers she had helped restore forty years ago and that she now examined with the careful attention of someone checking whether their own work had been undermined.
Elder Kurosawa, who had recovered his composure — mostly — caught Elder Tomás Aguilar's eye across the table. Neither spoke. But the look they exchanged communicated a complete thought: That child is going to be a problem of historic proportions, and I am not entirely opposed to watching it happen.
Mateo walked calmly down the corridor with Tomas still gripping his shoulder, though the grip had loosened from urgent to resigned — the hold of someone who knew that physical restraint was not actually preventing anything but who maintained it on principle.
The corridor was dim, lit by narrow windows set high in the walls that allowed light but not direct sightlines. The stone floor carried their footsteps in flat, contained echoes that bounced between the walls and faded quickly — or would have, if the formations had been functioning properly. Instead, the echoes carried slightly further than they should have, blending with the muffled sounds of the elder discussion that was now audible through the walls.
Behind them, faintly, the elders were arguing. Mateo could hear them — fragments of sentences, accusations, questions no one could answer. Elder Hiro's voice was the loudest, which was unsurprising. Elder Liang's was the most technical, which was also unsurprising. Elder Park was asking questions that no one wanted to address. Elder Marcos was attempting to impose order on a situation that had moved beyond orderly.
Obviously he could hear them.
The formation was not working, after all.
Tomas, who had been watching Mateo's face with growing suspicion throughout the walk, finally spoke.
"...You did something."
"...I do many things."
"To the formations."
"...That is a serious accusation."
"Your face is doing the thing."
"...I do not have a thing."
"You absolutely have a thing. You are doing it right now."
Mateo said nothing.
Which was, of course, the thing.
Outside, the air felt lighter.
Not calmer — just less constrained. Sound moved more freely, and the subtle structure of the meeting space no longer held around them. The village resumed its presence — the distant clang of the blacksmith's hammer, a child shouting something about a lost sandal, the herb vendor's voice carrying from the market path in the steady cadence of someone explaining to a customer why a particular root was worth exactly three times what the customer believed.
"...It's not about the root itself," the vendor was saying as Mateo passed. "It's about the soil it grew in. Location determines potency. You can't get this quality from the eastern plots."
Mateo's step did not slow, but his attention sharpened briefly. Location determines potency. She was not wrong. She did not know how right she was, or why, but the principle was sound. The herb vendor understood through experience what Mateo was beginning to understand through observation — that where something grew mattered as much as what it was.
He filed it alongside everything else.
Tomas stood still for a moment on the path, then followed.
"...Explain."
Mateo continued walking as though nothing unusual had occurred.
"...They were already uncertain."
"That is not an explanation."
Mateo glanced at him.
"...It is."
Tomas stopped walking altogether.
"...You walked into a restricted meeting, convinced them you had authority, disrupted their discussion, exposed a security flaw you probably caused, offered to fix it for a fee, and left."
"...Yes."
"...Why?"
Mateo paused.
For once, he did not answer immediately. The silence was genuine — not the calculated pause he used during performances, but the brief stillness of someone choosing between the answer that was clever and the answer that was true.
Then he turned, and for a brief moment, his posture shifted again — subtly, but unmistakably. The casual looseness that defined his public demeanor tightened into something more aligned, more present, more honest.
"...Understanding a system requires testing its boundaries," he said, his voice steady. "If a rule cannot withstand pressure, then it was never solid to begin with. Questioning is not an issue — ignorance is."
Tomas did not interrupt. He simply stared.
"...If no one challenges unnecessary limitations," Mateo continued, "then those limitations persist without reason."
For a moment —
It made sense.
Then Mateo relaxed, the tension in his posture dissolving as quickly as it had appeared.
"...Also," he added casually, "it was funny."
Tomas blinked.
"...What?"
Mateo turned and resumed walking.
"...Watching them question everything was entertaining."
Diego, who had somehow been present the entire time without being noticed — a feat of stealth that was either impressive or concerning depending on one's perspective — rushed up beside them with the specific energy of someone who had been containing an observation for several minutes and had reached the structural limit of his patience.
"...You walked into an elder meeting, broke their security, made them doubt themselves, and then offered to sell them the fix?"
"...Briefly."
"That is the most incredible thing I have ever witnessed."
"...It was a productive morning."
Tomas remained where he stood for a moment longer before finally moving again.
I do not understand him. He constructs arguments with the precision of someone who has been thinking about systems longer than he has been alive. He reads rooms the way others read text — instantly, completely, and with an awareness of subtext that should not exist in someone his age. He speaks like someone far older.
Then ruins it immediately by saying things like that.
"...You are a problem," Tomas said.
Mateo did not look back.
"...That has already been established."
That night, Mateo sat alone beneath the open sky, the faint silence of the village settling around him. The stillness was not empty — it held small inconsistencies, faint sounds that carried too far or seemed to blend into the background, subtle signs that quiet did not always mean absence. A dog barked once, somewhere to the south, and the sound traveled further than it should have before being absorbed by the forest's canopy. Cooking smoke drifted from the homes nearest the market path, carrying the scent of ginger and something sweeter that Mateo could not identify.
The pendant rested in his palm, its fractured star catching the dim light above.
This time —
It pulsed.
Not strongly.
But distinctly.
More consistent now. The intervals between pulses had shortened since the previous evening — not dramatically, but enough that the change was measurable. The rhythm was settling into something steadier, more deliberate, as though the pendant was learning how to sustain its own activity rather than producing it in sporadic bursts.
Mateo watched it closely.
The fragments within the fractured star pattern appeared to be moving — not visibly, not in a way that anyone else would detect, but through the subtle shift in how light caught their edges as he turned the pendant slowly. Each rotation produced a slightly different reflection, as though the internal arrangement was not fixed but fluid, the pieces drifting in slow, deliberate patterns that suggested convergence rather than random motion.
It looks like the fragments are moving on the inside. Not randomly. Toward something. As though they remember a configuration they once held and are slowly finding their way back to it.
The thought came more clearly than before.
Not broken.
A pause.
This item seems incomplete. Not damaged — unfinished. As though the process that created it was interrupted before completion, and the pieces that remain are continuing the work on their own, without instruction, without guidance, following a blueprint that exists in their structure rather than in any external design.
He closed his hand around it.
And for just a moment —
An image surfaced.
A forge.
Heat.
Metal shaping under steady hands — hands that were large, calloused, unhurried. The hands of someone who understood that shaping required patience and that patience required trust in the process. The metal glowed beneath the hammer's rhythm, its color shifting from dark orange to bright amber as the temperature rose, the surface becoming fluid enough to accept correction without losing its form.
A large figure stood behind the hands — unmoving, unwavering. Broad shoulders. A presence that filled the space the way the blacksmith's heat filled his forge — not through force, but through the simple fact of existing in a space too small to contain it comfortably.
Edges sharper than memory.
The glow of metal held longer than it should have, the details more defined than any previous instance — the texture of the anvil's surface, the pattern of sparks, the way the forge light painted the surrounding darkness in shades of orange and amber that shifted with each strike.
Then it was gone.
Mateo exhaled slowly.
"...Gramps."
The word came without certainty — but it did not feel wrong. It carried the weight of a connection that existed below conscious knowledge, a thread linking the image to something in Mateo's history that he could feel but not yet see. The figure at the forge was not a stranger. The hands were not unfamiliar. The rhythm of the hammer was not new.
It was remembered.
From somewhere that memory alone could not explain.
He leaned back slightly, looking up at the sky. The stars were visible through the gaps in the canopy — scattered, fragmented by leaves, their light arriving from distances so vast that the journey itself was more significant than the arrival. Some of them were already dead. Their light continued anyway.
Something was left behind.
Something still forming.
A faint smile touched his expression.
"...Good."
For once, the unknown was not inconvenient.
It was interesting.
And Mateo had always preferred things that were worth understanding.
