Time passed.
Not in any grand or dramatic way. There were no sudden breakthroughs that shook the village, no public declarations of talent, no spectacular moments that made the elders regret letting Mateo remain unsupervised for as long as they had. Instead, the years moved quietly, folding one season into the next as though the village itself had decided to breathe more slowly.
That, in itself, suited Mateo well.
After the trouble he had caused in his younger years, he learned—not restraint, exactly, but refinement. He still slipped into places he was not supposed to enter. He still tested rules just to see whether they deserved to exist. He still caused headaches often enough that more than one elder developed the habit of narrowing their eyes whenever Mateo passed by, as though merely seeing him was a warning of future inconvenience.
But he became more careful.
More selective.
He learned when chaos was useful and when it merely wasted time.

Over time, the village adjusted as well.
Not openly.
Not in ways most would notice.
Certain meeting spaces changed their positions. Paths that once remained open began to shift subtly in usage. There were moments—rare, but present—when Mateo would arrive somewhere he had used before, only to find that the space had been repurposed, restricted, or simply… watched.
The elders did not confront him.
They adapted instead.
 They’re refining their response.
Mateo did not interfere with that.
It made things more interesting.

By the time he was fourteen, most of the village had reached the same conclusion about him: Mateo had become quieter only in the sense that storms were quiet before breaking.
His body had changed more honestly than his habits.
He was taller now, though not imposing. His frame had lengthened and tightened, the softness of childhood burned away through constant training. He carried lean muscle rather than bulk, his strength hidden beneath efficient movement rather than noise. Nothing about him looked excessive at first glance. That was intentional. Mateo did not dislike being underestimated. In many situations, he found it convenient.
What had changed most was not how much stronger he had become, but how well he understood what that strength actually was.

He trained differently than the others.
The village children and young adults still practiced as they always had—through repetition, force, competition, and the simple honesty of getting hit hard enough to remember a mistake. Mateo did not reject that approach. He simply went further.
Where others practiced movement, he studied transfer.
Where others hardened the body, he focused on coordination.
Where others thought of strength as output, he thought of it as a chain that could not afford a single broken link.

Every morning before the village fully woke, he trained in silence.
Not immediately with strikes.
Never immediately.

First came standing.
He would settle his feet into the packed dirt behind the house, toes spreading slightly inside worn training shoes, feeling the texture of the ground beneath him—hard earth, slight give, shallow irregularities shaped by weather and repeated steps.
But over time, even that familiar ground had changed.
Some areas compacted more slowly than before. Others held impressions longer. There were mornings where faint disturbances—footprints not made by villagers—lingered at the edges of his training space before fading into nothing.
Mateo noticed.
 Movement beyond the usual patterns.
He did not investigate.
Not yet.

He would breathe once, then let his weight descend.
Not collapse.
Descend.
The shift started in the soles of his feet, pressure distributing through heel and arch, then balancing back toward the ball where movement could be born cleanly. From there, his ankles aligned—not tensed, but held. His calves tightened in smooth bands beneath the skin, not with strain, but with readiness.
Then knees.
Then hips.
Then spine.
Then breath.

Quietly.

The training yard, such as it was, lay open beneath the filtered light of the forest canopy. It was nothing more than a widened basin of hard-packed dirt between houses and trees, bordered by low fencing in some places and roots in others.
This time though—
There were more observers than usual.
Not all of them familiar.
Not all of them villagers.
Some stood just outside the natural flow of the yard—watching, not participating, their presence quiet but deliberate.

By evening, he had resumed his other routines. He spent an hour near the forge, watching heat collect inside iron until it changed color and intention together. He checked two alchemical mixtures left to settle in covered bowls behind the house, adjusted one of them, discarded the other.

That evening, voices carried differently through the village.
A passing conversation—
“…they’re expecting travelers—”
“…not merchants—something else—”
“…elders said to keep clear—”
Then it faded.
Incomplete.
But sufficient.

Mateo stood at the edge of the village where the dirt path gave way to broken roots and deepening shadow.
The forest had always been there.
At ten, it had been interesting.
At fourteen, it had become necessary.

Beyond the last cluster of homes, the air changed.
Not just in scent—
In behavior.
The forest held less agreement with human movement. The ground did not yield predictably. Sound carried differently.
More honestly.

Behind him, the village still lived in its familiar rhythm—
But it had shifted.
Slightly.
Subtly.
Enough.

He looked down at the pendant in his hand.
It pulsed.
Stronger now. More often.
Closer.

For a moment, the world seemed to stretch again—not truly slow, but resolve differently.
He felt paths before he chose them.
Felt pressure before it arrived.
Felt that strange inner quickening—

Then it passed.

Mateo closed his hand around the pendant and looked back toward the forest.
“Soon,” he said quietly.

This time, when he stepped forward—
He did not stop at the edge.
