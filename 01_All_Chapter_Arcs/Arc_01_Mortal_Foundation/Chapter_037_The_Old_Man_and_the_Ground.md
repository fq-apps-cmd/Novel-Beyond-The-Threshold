Chapter 37 — The Old Man and the Ground  

They should not have come this far.  
Tomas knew it. He had known it since they passed the second ridge — the point where his previous forest excursions had stopped, where the terrain shifted  
from the manageable unevenness of the outer zones to something less accommodating. The canopy thickened overhead, compressing the light into fragments  
so small they barely qualified as illumination. The undergrowth pressed closer, the vegetation denser and more assertive than the tidy growth near the village  
boundary. The air carried weight — not the oppressive density of the labyrinth that Mateo had described, but a fullness that suggested the atmosphere held  
more than oxygen and moisture.  
The energy beneath his feet had changed too.  
Since the training yard — since the morning when the post had cracked along its grain and the ground had responded to his stamp with the faint,  
acknowledging compression that his earth affinity was still learning to interpret — Tomas had been aware of the current. The river that flowed beneath the  
forest's surface, moving northeast with the patient certainty of something that had been flowing since before the village existed. In the outer zones, the current  
was gentle. A suggestion. A pull that his awareness registered without difficulty and that his body navigated without strain.  
Here, the current was stronger.  
Not aggressive. Not overwhelming. But present in a way that demanded acknowledgment rather than permitting it. The energy pressed upward through the soil  
with enough force that Tomas could feel it through the soles of his shoes without crouching, without pressing his palm to the ground, without any of the  
deliberate contact that his earlier experiments had required. The forest's energy was simply louder here — the way a river was louder at a point where the  
channel narrowed and the water was forced through a smaller space at higher speed.  
"...We should turn back," Tomas said.  
Diego, who was walking three steps behind him with the specific posture of someone who was simultaneously alert and pretending not to be, glanced up from  
the herb cluster he had been examining.  
"...You've said that twice."  
"...Because it's been true twice."  
"...And yet we're still walking."  
Tomas did not have a good response to that. They were still walking because the herbs in this zone were significantly better than anything they had found in the  
outer areas — tier two specimens that carried energy signatures dense enough that Tomas's earth affinity could feel the potency through the soil before his eyes  
identified the species. The improvement in quality was not incremental. It was categorical. The difference between the herbs here and the herbs near the village  
boundary was the difference between the village's packed-dirt paths and the forest's living soil — functional versus vital.  
Diego had noticed the quality difference immediately. His family's "recovery specialist" instincts had identified the herbs' value through assessment methods that  
Tomas could not replicate and that Diego could not fully explain — something about the color saturation, the leaf rigidity, the way the stems held their structure  
when bent rather than snapping. Whatever the method, Diego's selections had been consistently superior to Tomas's, and the growing collection in their  
gathering pouches represented more value than either of them had accumulated in months of village-zone harvesting.  
The temptation to push further was real.  
The wisdom of pushing further was not.  
"...The creatures in this zone are different," Tomas said, his voice lower now — not whispering, but modulating. The forest's ambient sounds had shifted since  
they passed the second ridge. Fewer bird calls. More silence between the sounds that remained. The specific quality of quiet that occurred when the ecosystem's  
smaller members were aware that larger members were active and had adjusted their behavior accordingly.  
"...Different how?" Diego asked.  
"...Stronger. The one that passed near us earlier — the predator we hid from — its energy signature was denser than anything in the outer zones. Not just larger.  
Concentrated. The kind of density that comes from decades of absorbing ambient energy in an environment this rich."  
He paused, considering how much to share. Tomas had been developing a vocabulary for what his earth affinity perceived, building it word by word through  
experience rather than instruction. The vocabulary was still incomplete. But some observations were clear enough to articulate.  
"...There are things in this area that could kill us without effort. Spirit Awakening level beasts, at minimum. Creatures that have been absorbing energy from these  
currents for longer than we've been alive. Our Body Tempering cultivation wouldn't register as resistance to them. We'd register as... terrain. Something to step  
over or step on, depending on their mood."  
Diego was quiet for a moment. That was unusual enough to constitute a response in itself.  
"...So we're herb gathering in a zone where the local wildlife considers us furniture."  
"...That's a reasonable summary."  
"...And the herbs are really good."  
"...Also accurate."  
"...I see the dilemma."  
They continued walking — slower now, more carefully, their path angling along the boundary between productive and suicidal with the specific navigation of  
people who understood that the line between the two was not marked and that discovering which side you were on typically happened after the information  
was useful.  
The old man was sitting on a rock.  
Not a remarkable rock — a weathered granite formation approximately waist-height, positioned at the base of an ancient oak whose trunk was wide enough that  
three people could have stood behind it without seeing each other. The rock's surface was worn smooth by time and contact, its edges rounded by centuries of  
rain and the slow, patient erosion that the forest's moisture produced on anything that held still long enough.  
The old man occupied it the way some people occupied chairs — not sitting on it so much as belonging to it, his body arranged with the specific comfort of  
someone who had sat in this exact position many times before and whose body had memorized the stone's contours the way a sleeper memorized a pillow.  
He was old. Not frail — weathered. His face carried deep lines that mapped decades of exposure to conditions that most people experienced only briefly and  
that he appeared to have inhabited permanently. His skin was dark, tanned by time rather than sun, carrying a texture that suggested endurance rather than  
aging. His frame, visible beneath earth-toned robes that were simple in cut and faded in color, was broader than his age implied — the shoulders carrying a  
density that the fabric could not fully conceal, the arms resting on his knees with a weight that spoke of capability rather than rest.  

A wooden staff lay across his lap — not held, not gripped, simply resting there with the settled weight of an object that had been in its owner's possession for so  
long that proximity was maintenance enough. The wood was dark, grained with age, carrying a texture that was not quite bark and not quite polished —  
something between the two that suggested the staff had been grown rather than carved.  
His eyes were amber.  
Tomas noticed the old man before Diego did — his earth affinity registering the anomaly before his vision confirmed it. The ground beneath the rock felt  
different. Not in the way that the predator's passage had felt different — the dense, disruptive compression of something spiritually heavy moving through the  
current. This was the opposite. The ground beneath the old man felt calm. Settled. As though the current that had been pressing against Tomas's awareness  
since they entered this zone had found, at this specific point, a state of perfect equilibrium.  
The energy was not absent. It was balanced. Harmonized. The way a river was harmonized at a point where the channel's width and the water's volume achieved  
exact proportion — no turbulence, no acceleration, no resistance. Just flow.  
The old man was sitting at the center of a pocket of perfect energetic stillness.  
And he appeared to be doing absolutely nothing.  
Tomas slowed. Diego, reading the change in Tomas's pace, slowed with him.  
"...There's someone there," Tomas said quietly.  
Diego's gaze found the old man. His expression shifted through several stages — surprise (there was a person here, in a zone they had assumed was  
uninhabited), assessment (the person appeared to be elderly, alone, and unarmed except for a walking stick), and the specific uncertainty that occurred when the  
visual information and the contextual information did not agree.  
An old man sitting alone in a zone where Spirit Awakening beasts roamed was either very lost or very something else.  
"...Should we...?" Diego began.  
The old man looked up.  
The motion was unhurried — the lift of a gaze that had been resting on the forest floor and that now redirected toward the two young cultivators who had  
entered his vicinity with the subtle grace of people who were trying to be quiet and who were succeeding only by the standards of people who had never tried  
to be quiet in a forest that was actually listening.  
"...You're rather far from home," the old man said.  
His voice was soft. Not weak — soft. The specific softness of someone who did not need volume because the environment already listened when he spoke. The  
words carried without projection, arriving at Tomas's ears with a clarity that the distance between them should not have permitted, as though the air itself had  
decided to cooperate with the delivery.  
Tomas straightened. The instinct to be respectful — trained into him by years of village elder interactions — activated automatically.  
"...We're gathering herbs, Elder. The outer zones have been picked through, and the quality further in is significantly better."  
The old man regarded him for a moment. The regard was gentle — carrying none of the sharp evaluation that the village elders employed when assessing  
trainees, none of the measuring quality that Elder Shen's observations carried, none of the strategic calculation that characterized Elder Kurosawa's attention.  
This was simply looking. The unhurried, patient act of seeing someone without needing to categorize them.  
"...Significantly better," the old man repeated. "...And significantly more dangerous. Do you understand the difference between the two, or have you simply  
noticed that one accompanies the other?"  
The question was not condescending. It was curious — genuinely curious, the way someone asked a question when they wanted to hear the answer rather than  
when they wanted to demonstrate that they already knew it.  
Tomas considered his response. "...We understand the danger. We've been careful about the zones we enter and the time we spend in them."  
"...Careful," the old man said, as though tasting the word and finding it nutritious but insufficient. "...Careful is valuable when the danger is proportional to your  
awareness. But the creatures in this area do not operate on the same scale as the ones you've encountered near your village boundary."  
He shifted slightly on the rock — not standing, not repositioning, just adjusting his weight with the fluid ease of someone whose body responded to intent  
without requiring instruction.  
"...There are three Spirit Awakening-class beasts within a kilometer of where you're standing," he said, his tone carrying the same casual warmth as his previous  
statements, as though the information was practical rather than alarming. "One is territorial and patrols a route that passes approximately two hundred meters to  
your east every afternoon. Another dens in the root system of a collapsed formation tree four hundred meters to your north. The third is transient — it moves  
through this area on an irregular schedule and does not maintain a fixed territory, which makes it more unpredictable than the other two."  
Tomas felt his stomach tighten. Diego's hand, which had been resting casually at his side, moved to his belt — not reaching for a weapon, because they did not  
carry weapons adequate for Spirit Awakening beasts, but seeking the comfort of contact with something solid.  
"...Spirit Awakening," Diego said quietly. "...All three?"  
"...At minimum," the old man replied. "...There are others deeper in that exceed that classification. Some significantly. They do not typically range this far toward  
the outer zones, but 'typically' is a word that describes frequency rather than certainty."  
He looked at them with the specific expression of someone who was not trying to frighten but who considered incomplete information more dangerous than  
uncomfortable information.  
"...You should not be roaming these areas. Your cultivation — Body Tempering, both of you, the fourth and fifth levels respectively if I'm reading your signatures  
correctly — is insufficient for the threats this zone contains. The herbs you're gathering are valuable. They are not worth your lives."  
The assessment of their cultivation levels was casual. Accurate. And delivered with a precision that should not have been possible through visual observation  
alone — cultivation level assessment typically required spiritual scanning, direct energy contact, or at minimum the kind of focused perception that high-level  
cultivators employed deliberately.  
The old man had read them the way Tomas read the ground — through contact that did not require touching.  
Tomas noticed this.  
He filed it without commenting.  
"...How do you survive here?" Tomas asked. The question was genuine rather than challenging — the honest curiosity of someone who was looking at an old  
man sitting alone in a zone that contained creatures capable of destroying them both and who could not reconcile the image with the reality.  
The old man smiled. The expression was warm — genuinely warm, carrying the specific quality of amusement that occurred when someone was asked a question  
they had been asked many times before and that they still found pleasant rather than tedious.  
"...I have been here a very long time," he said. "...Long enough that the creatures who live in this forest have grown accustomed to my presence. I do not hunt  
them. I do not threaten their territories. I do not disrupt the balance that governs their interactions with each other. In return, they extend me the same courtesy."  
He tapped the staff across his lap once — a light, casual gesture that produced a sound deeper than the wood's apparent density should have allowed.  
"...I have made peace with most of them. Not through negotiation in the way that humans negotiate — through contracts and terms and the mutual  
acknowledgment of leverage. Through coexistence. Through the patient, sustained demonstration that my presence does not constitute a threat to their  

existence. It took years. Decades, in some cases. But eventually, even the most territorial creatures learn to distinguish between something that shares their space  
and something that threatens it."  
He paused.  
"...I commune with the forest. Its rhythms. Its seasons. Its moods — yes, forests have moods, though they express them through growth and decay rather than  
through facial expressions. I am no one important. Just an old man who chose to live among the trees rather than among the buildings, and who has been doing  
so for long enough that the trees have stopped minding."  
Diego studied him. His pattern-tracking instincts were active — cataloging details the way his family had trained him to catalog details about anything  
unfamiliar. The robes' quality (simple but well-maintained). The staff's material (unusual — not any wood species Diego recognized). The posture (comfortable  
but carrying hidden density). The eyes.  
The eyes were strange.  
Diego could not have articulated why. They were the right shape, the right size, positioned correctly in a face that was weathered but proportionate. But the  
color — amber, deep, carrying a luminescence that did not match the available light — produced a sensation in Diego's awareness that he associated with things  
that were more than they appeared. The feeling was not threat. It was depth — the visual equivalent of looking into water that was clear enough to see through  
but deep enough that the bottom was not visible.  
He filed the observation.  
He would describe it later as "really weird eyes" and "like looking into a jar of honey that was also somehow looking back at you."  
"...You live out here alone?" Diego asked.  
"...Alone is a matter of perspective," the old man replied. "...I share this forest with more company than most villages contain. The company simply does not  
speak in languages you would recognize."  
"...That's either very peaceful or very lonely."  
The old man's smile deepened slightly — the expression of someone who had considered both possibilities extensively and had arrived at an answer that  
contained elements of each.  
"...It is what it is. The forest does not require my opinion of it. That is, I find, one of its better qualities."  
Tomas's earth affinity was registering something about the old man's presence that he could not fully process. The energetic stillness surrounding the rock was  
not passive — it was maintained. Something about the old man's existence in this space actively produced the equilibrium that Tomas could feel, the way a large  
stone in a river produced calm water in its lee. The old man was not merely sitting in a zone of stillness. He was creating it — his presence harmonizing the  
current's flow in his immediate vicinity the way a tuning fork harmonized the vibrations of the strings around it.  
The ground beneath Tomas's feet felt different here. Not the overwhelming, overloading contact that the guardian's surface presence had produced during their  
previous forest visit. Gentler. More focused. As though the earth in this specific area had been calmed by long proximity to something that understood it at a  
level Tomas could sense but not comprehend.  
"...A boy came through here recently," the old man said.  
The shift in topic was seamless — delivered with the same gentle cadence, carrying no change in tone that would suggest the new subject was more significant  
than the previous one. But Tomas's attention sharpened.  
"...Quiet. Observant. Went deeper than most." The old man's gaze rested on the forest ahead of them — toward the northeast, toward the deeper zones where  
the energy current converged and where the labyrinth waited beneath its canyon. "...He moved through this area with a care that suggested he understood what  
he was walking through, even if his understanding was incomplete."  
"...That sounds like Mateo," Diego said immediately.  
Tomas shot him a look. Diego shrugged — the characteristic shrug of someone who had decided that information was more useful shared than hoarded and  
who did not consider the old man's gentle demeanor a reason for caution.  
"...Your friend," the old man said. Not a question.  
"...Yes," Tomas said.  
"...He went deeper."  
"...Yes."  
The old man nodded once — a small, contained motion that communicated acknowledgment without opinion. Whatever he thought about a fourteen-year-old  
boy descending into the forest's deeper zones alone and injured, he did not share it.  
"...He is persistent," the old man observed.  
"...That's one word for it," Tomas said.  
"...Recklessly determined is another," Diego added.  
"...Annoyingly competent is a third," Tomas said.  
The old man's eyes carried amusement — warm, genuine, the specific quality of entertainment that occurred when someone observed a dynamic they  
recognized from long experience.  
"...He is fortunate to have friends who describe him with such precision."  
"...We try," Diego said.  
Tomas hesitated. The question that had been forming since the old man first spoke about the forest's creatures surfaced.  
"...Is he safe? In the deeper zones?"  
The old man was quiet for a moment. The silence was not evasion — it was consideration. The specific quality of quiet that occurred when someone was  
weighing how much truth to share against how much truth the listener could use.  
"...The forest is not safe," he said at last. "...Not for anyone. Not at any level. But it is structured. It operates according to rules that reward awareness and punish  
carelessness. Your friend appears to possess a great deal of the former."  
A pause.  
"...Whether that is sufficient depends on what he encounters and what he chooses to do about it. I cannot answer that for him. No one can."  
The answer was not reassuring. It was honest. And honesty, Tomas was learning, was more useful than reassurance even when it was less comfortable.  
The old man looked at them again — a steady, unhurried gaze that took in both of them simultaneously, the way someone took in a landscape rather than  
individual features.  
"...You should head back," he said. "...The territorial one — the Spirit Awakening beast that patrols to your east — shifts its route in the late afternoon. It moves  
closer to this area as the light changes. You have perhaps an hour before the path you used to arrive here falls within its patrol range."  
The specificity of the warning was startling. Not "you should be careful." Not "it's getting dangerous." The exact creature. The exact behavior pattern. The exact  
timeline.  
The old man knew this forest the way the village elders knew the village — through decades of sustained, intimate observation that had produced a map so  

detailed it included the schedules of individual predators.  
"...If you follow the ridge to the southwest," he continued, "there is a path that descends through a section of older growth. The canopy is denser there, which the  
territorial beast avoids — it prefers open sightlines for its patrol. The path will bring you back to the outer zones within forty minutes if you maintain a  
reasonable pace."  
He said this the way someone gave directions to a neighbor — casually, practically, as though the information was common knowledge that simply had not  
reached them yet.  
"...Thank you," Tomas said. The words carried genuine gratitude — not just for the directions, but for the warning, the conversation, and the specific quality of  
care that the old man had demonstrated without ever making it feel like concern.  
The old man nodded.  
"...Be more careful about how deep you venture," he said. "...The forest rewards those who learn its patterns. It does not forgive those who ignore them."  
Diego opened his mouth to respond — a question forming about the old man's specific knowledge, about how long he had been here, about whether he had  
seen other visitors or just Mateo — but the words died before they arrived.  
Because the old man was gone.  
Not walking away. Not retreating into the undergrowth. Not moving at all.  
Gone.  
The rock remained. The forest continued. The energy current flowed with its usual northeastern pull. The ambient sounds held their pattern.  
But the space where the old man had been sitting was empty — carrying only the fading impression of the energetic stillness that his presence had created,  
dissolving back into the current's normal turbulence the way warmth dissolved from a surface after the heat source was removed.  
Tomas stared at the rock.  
Diego stared at the rock.  
"...Did he just —" Diego began.  
"...Yes."  
"...He was right there."  
"...Yes."  
"...Old men don't just disappear."  
"...This one did."  
Diego looked at the forest around them — the trees, the undergrowth, the canopy that filtered the afternoon light into the specific golden quality that the old  
man had warned them was the precursor to the territorial beast's schedule shift.  
"...I have questions," Diego said.  
"...So do I."  
"...His eyes were weird."  
"...I noticed."  
"...Like honey. But the kind that watches you back."  
Tomas did not have a response for that. He was focused on something else — a detail that his earth affinity had registered during the conversation and that was  
only now resolving into conscious understanding.  
The old man's footsteps.  
Or rather — the absence of them. Tomas's earth affinity had been reading the ground continuously since they entered this zone — every step, every creature's  
passage, every vibration carrying information about weight, density, and the energy signature of whatever produced it. He had felt their own footsteps. He had  
felt the predator's passage. He had felt the current's flow and the trees' root systems and the herbs' energy signatures.  
He had not felt the old man.  
Not his weight on the rock. Not his footsteps approaching. Not the displacement of energy that any physical presence should have produced in the current's  
flow. The old man had existed in the energetic stillness he created — and the stillness had been so complete that it had rendered him invisible to the very sense  
that Tomas was learning to trust most.  
The ground had not registered him.  
As though the earth itself had decided that the old man's presence was not something to be reported.  
Or as though the old man and the earth had an understanding that predated Tomas's awareness by a margin so vast that his newly developing perception could  
not bridge it.  
"...We should follow his directions," Tomas said.  
"...Agreed."  
"...Now."  
"...Very agreed."  
Then — from somewhere in the forest, carried on air that should not have been able to transmit a voice from a source that was no longer visible — the old man's  
words arrived. Not loud. Not projected. Simply present, the way his voice had been present during the conversation, as though the forest itself was delivering the  
message on his behalf.  
"...The southwestern ridge. Stay below the canopy line. And do be careful with that herb in your left pouch, young man — it reacts poorly to sudden temperature  
changes. Wrap it separately."  
Diego looked down at his left pouch. Looked at the forest. Looked at Tomas.  
"...He knew what was in my pouch."  
"...He knew what was in your pouch."  
"...From a distance."  
"...From wherever he is now."  
"...Which is nowhere visible."  
"...Correct."  
Diego swallowed.  
"...I like him," he said. "...But I'm also terrified of him."  
"...Both of those are appropriate responses."  
They turned southwest. Found the ridge the old man had described. Found the path through the denser canopy — exactly where he said it would be, exactly as  
navigable as he implied, the route threading through older growth whose branches interlocked overhead in a lattice so dense that the ground below existed in  
permanent twilight.  

They moved quickly. Not running — moving with the controlled urgency of people who had been given a timeline by someone who clearly knew what he was  
talking about and who did not intend to test whether the timeline was approximate or exact.  
Twenty minutes into the descent, they heard it.  
Behind them. East. Approximately where the path they had originally walked would have been.  
A sound that was not a roar and not a growl and not anything that the human vocal apparatus could reproduce — a low, resonant vibration that traveled  
through the ground before it traveled through the air, arriving in Tomas's awareness through his feet a full second before his ears registered it. The vibration  
carried density. Weight. The specific signature of something that was spiritually concentrated enough that its vocalizations displaced the ambient energy in its  
vicinity the way a boulder displaced water.  
Spirit Awakening.  
On schedule.  
Exactly where the old man said it would be.  
They did not stop. They did not look back. They continued along the southwestern ridge with the quiet, determined pace of people who understood that the  
margin between their current safety and the alternative was measured in minutes rather than kilometers.  
The forest let them pass.  
The path remained clear. The canopy remained dense. The territorial beast's patrol route remained to the east, its vibrations fading as the distance between them  
increased with each step.  
Forty minutes later — exactly forty minutes, as though the old man's estimate had been a measurement rather than an approximation — they emerged from the  
denser growth into the outer zones where the village's ambient protection began to reassert itself. The energy current softened. The canopy opened. The light  
returned to its familiar fractured patterns.  
Tomas stopped.  
Breathed.  
The ground beneath his feet felt like home — the stable, contained energy of the village's micro-vein, calm and predictable and carrying none of the deep,  
pressing current that the forest's inner zones maintained.  
"...We're not going that deep again," Diego said.  
"...No."  
"...Not until we're stronger."  
"...No."  
A pause.  
"...The herbs were really good though."  
"...Yes."  
"...And the old man was..."  
Diego searched for the word. His vocabulary, which was typically excessive, failed to produce a term that adequately described someone who sat alone in a zone  
full of Spirit Awakening predators, knew the patrol schedules of individual beasts, gave directions with cartographic precision, disappeared without movement,  
spoke through forest air from invisible locations, and identified the contents of someone's herb pouch from a distance that should have made the observation  
impossible.  
"...Something," Diego finished.  
"...Yes," Tomas said.  
"...He was definitely something."  
They walked back to the village in silence. The silence was not empty — it was full, occupied by the specific quality of thought that occurred when two people  
had shared an experience that exceeded their framework for processing it and that would require significantly more conversation to even begin understanding.  
The conversation would come later.  
For now, the silence held everything they could not yet say.  
And somewhere in the forest — sitting on a rock, or moving through the trees, or existing in a state that was neither sitting nor moving but something older  
than both — the old man with the amber eyes watched them go.  
And was satisfied.
