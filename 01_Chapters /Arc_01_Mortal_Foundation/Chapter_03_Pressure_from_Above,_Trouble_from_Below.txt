The assessment had ended without ceremony.
The elders had departed the way they had arrived — unhurried, deliberate, their presence withdrawing from the training yard like a tide pulling back from shore. The ambient weight that had settled over the space during their visit lingered for a few moments after they left, as though the air itself needed time to remember how to behave normally.
The trainees who had been assessed stood in the aftermath with the specific stillness of people processing results they did not fully understand. Some looked at their hands. Others stared at the testing sphere's now-dormant surface, as though it might offer additional information if regarded long enough. A few spoke in low voices, comparing reactions, debating what the colors meant, constructing theories from insufficient data with the confidence that only came from knowing nothing.
Mateo stood slightly apart from the group, his expression unchanged from the moment he had stepped back from the sphere. His result — calm, steady, unremarkable — had drawn no attention. The elders had moved on immediately. No one had lingered. No one had looked twice.
Exactly as intended.
But Tomas had looked twice.
Not at the sphere. At Mateo.
And the look had carried something that Mateo recognized — not suspicion, exactly, but the quiet, persistent awareness of someone who had noticed a discrepancy and filed it away without knowing what to do with it yet.
Mateo had filed Tomas's look as well.
Inés had been watching too, though her attention was less focused on the assessment results and more on the way Mateo moved. She had noticed him correcting mid-motion earlier — the adjustment he made during his practice sequence, the one he stopped before completing — and the observation had not left her.
It would not leave her for some time.
The yard had begun to empty when the conversation shifted.
"Then show her properly."
The words came from Tomas, directed at Mateo with the blunt practicality of someone who had decided that the assessment was over and the real work should resume. He stood with his arms at his sides, posture open, weight balanced — not challenging, but present. Ready.
The words settled into the space like a decision already made.
For a moment, no one moved.
Mateo exhaled quietly and straightened, completing the adjustment he had already been midway through before turning fully toward Tomas.
"...Later," Mateo replied simply.
Tomas blinked.
"...What?"
"Food first," Mateo said, already stepping past him. "Improvement without recovery is inefficient."
There was a pause.
Then Diego snorted.
"...He's right. That's annoyingly correct."
The group shifted without discussion. Not because it was planned — but because no one objected. The unspoken agreement that governed their movement had become familiar enough that it no longer required negotiation. Mateo walked. The others followed. The direction was understood.
The cooking stall sat slightly off the main path, built lower than the surrounding structures, with a wide stone slab at its center that held several iron pots of varying size and age. The largest was blackened from years of continuous use, its surface scarred with the residue of a thousand meals that had been cooked, served, and forgotten. The smallest held something that bubbled with a reluctance suggesting it had not yet decided what it wanted to become.
Steam rose in slow curls, carrying a mix of root broth, herbs, and something faintly bitter beneath it — a medicinal undertone that most customers ignored but that Mateo's awareness cataloged automatically. The bitterness came from dried bark shavings, added not for flavor but for their mild restorative properties. A practical choice. Inexpensive. Effective enough for people who trained daily and needed their bodies to recover faster than rest alone allowed.
The area around the stall was not empty. Two older men sat on a low bench nearby, sharing a single bowl between them and arguing in the comfortable, well-rehearsed manner of people who had been having the same disagreement for so long it had become a form of companionship rather than conflict.
"The broth was better last season," one said.
"The broth is the same as it has always been," the other replied.
"It had more depth."
"You had fewer opinions."
"That is unrelated."
"It is entirely related."
They continued. Neither showed any sign of reaching a conclusion. Neither appeared to want one.
Mateo stopped near the stall. Not for long. Just enough.
"...This place looks good," he said quietly.
The woman behind the stall glanced up. She was sturdy in the way that people who worked with heat and heavy pots became sturdy — her arms thick, her hands calloused, her expression carrying the permanent mild skepticism of someone who had heard every excuse for not paying on time.
"You're not picky today?"
"...Energy intake is more important than selection."
"...That sounds like your normal excuse," she replied dryly.
Mateo didn't respond.
Which was answer enough.
She ladled broth into a bowl — not the largest portion, not the smallest, but the specific amount that she had learned over time was what Mateo actually consumed regardless of how much was offered. She had stopped asking about preferences months ago. He ate what was provided, finished exactly what was given, and never requested more or less.
It was, she had once told another vendor, the most unsettling eating behavior she had ever witnessed in a child.
Tomas folded his arms slightly as he stood nearby, glancing toward the forest edge in the distance. The treeline was visible from here — a dark, uneven wall of green that rose above the village's lower structures and pressed against the sky with the patient weight of something that had been there long before the village and would remain long after.
"...You go out often," he said.
Mateo took the bowl offered to him.
"...Often enough. But only the edges for now."
Diego leaned over slightly, inspecting Mateo's portion with the critical eye of someone who considered himself an authority on food despite having no qualifications beyond enthusiasm.
"...That still looks like something I wouldn't eat willingly."
"You've eaten worse," Tomas said.
"...That was for money."
A vendor across the path — a thin man selling dried strips of something that he insisted was venison but that smelled distinctly of fish — called out to a passing woman with the aggressive optimism of someone who had not made a sale all morning.
"Fresh cuts! Best in the village! Prepared this morning!"
The woman glanced at his display, then at him, then continued walking without adjusting her pace.
The vendor's shoulders sagged fractionally. Then he straightened, adjusted his display, and began preparing to shout at the next passerby with undiminished conviction.
Mateo observed this cycle with the quiet appreciation of someone who recognized persistence even when it was misapplied.
Mateo spoke without looking up from his bowl.
"...What do you know about the forest?"
Both Tomas and Inés glanced at him.
The question was direct. Not casual. It carried the specific weight of something that had been considered before being spoken — not an idle curiosity but a request for information that would be used.
"...Enough," Inés replied after a moment. Her tone was measured, each word selected with the precision that characterized everything she did. "Outer areas are manageable. Mid-range zones are unpredictable. After that..."
She paused slightly.
"...We don't go that far."
Tomas took a breath.
"...My parents mentioned something once," he said slowly. "Travelers passing through. From further regions."
Mateo looked up.
"...Continue."
"They said deeper areas — far beyond where anyone here goes — have creatures that don't behave like what we train against."
A short pause.
"...High-ranking beasts."
Diego immediately leaned in, his food temporarily forgotten — which, given Diego's relationship with food, indicated genuine interest.
"...Define high-ranking."
Tomas didn't look at him.
"...Strong enough that," he said carefully, "someone below the Spirit Awakening Realm wouldn't even be able to approach."
He hesitated.
"...One of them joked that a single breath from something out there could kill someone at our level."
Diego blinked.
"...That feels excessive."
"...They didn't sound like they were exaggerating."
A longer pause settled over the group. The ambient noise of the village continued around them — the vendor still calling out, the two old men still arguing, the cooking stall's pots still bubbling — but the conversation had created a pocket of quiet within it that the surrounding sounds could not quite penetrate.
Then Tomas added quietly:
"...They also mentioned something else."
Mateo's gaze sharpened slightly.
"...Say it."
"...There are things deeper that don't even register as 'beasts.'"
Silence.
"...Meaning?"
"...Meaning they don't follow patterns we understand."
Another pause.
"...Some of them," Tomas said, "...can shatter mountains."
Diego slowly set his bowl down. The gesture was deliberate — the careful placement of something he no longer had the appetite to hold.
"...I think I preferred not knowing that."
Mateo was silent for a moment. His expression had not changed — the same calm, analytical neutrality he wore in every conversation. But something behind his eyes had shifted. Not fear. Not excitement. Something closer to recognition — the look of someone who had just received confirmation of something they had already suspected.
Then —
"...Distance?"
Tomas blinked.
"...What?"
"...How far?"
"...Kilometers," Tomas said. "A lot of them."
Mateo nodded once.
That is very interesting, he thought. The deeper zones operate on a different scale entirely. The power gradient is not linear — it escalates. What exists at the outer edges is manageable. What exists at the core is beyond anything the village can comprehend. And somewhere between those two extremes is the point where training becomes survival and survival becomes education.
One day I will have to check it out.
He stood.
Finished.
"...Alright. Let's continue then."
Return to Sparring
That was all it took.
The open space of the training yard filled again, this time with intent. The ambient quality of the air changed as the group returned — not heavier, but more focused, as though the conversation about the forest had sharpened something in each of them that carried over into physical practice.
The two old men on the bench had paused their argument to watch. The cooking stall vendor wiped her hands on her apron and leaned against the counter, her expression shifting from professional disinterest to mild curiosity. Even the venison-fish vendor had stopped shouting long enough to glance toward the yard.
Tomas stepped forward first. No hesitation now. His posture had changed since the morning — something in the assessment, or perhaps in the conversation about the forest, had settled a weight into his stance that had not been there before. He moved with the deliberate focus of someone who had decided that this session would not be routine.
"...You're not using a weapon?" he asked.
"...Not necessary," Mateo replied.
A flicker of irritation crossed Tomas's expression — not anger, but the specific frustration of someone who took their training seriously being told that his opponent did not consider the encounter significant enough to require a tool.
"...We'll see."
He moved.
The first strike came fast — clean, controlled, direct. The wooden saber cutting through the air along a line that was technically correct and practically predictable. Mateo stepped aside with minimal effort, the movement requiring so little adjustment that it barely qualified as evasion.
Efficient. But too predictable. The line of attack follows the textbook arc exactly. No variation. No feint. No adjustment for the opponent's position. He is fighting the form rather than fighting me.
Tomas adjusted instantly, the second strike tighter, faster — angled lower to cut off the lateral escape route that Mateo had used to avoid the first.
Better.
Mateo redirected it. Not blocked — removed from usefulness. His hand met the flat of the blade at the precise point where Tomas's force was transitioning from acceleration to extension, and guided it past his body along a path that spent its energy without ever threatening his position.
"...You're not moving away," Tomas said, breathing slightly harder.
"...No."
I'm moving around you.
The exchange tightened. Faster. Cleaner. More pressure from Tomas as he recognized that the standard approach was insufficient and began adapting in real time — compressing his strikes, varying his angles, testing different lines of attack to find one that Mateo could not simply redirect.
Mateo stepped forward. Just slightly. A half-step that closed distance rather than created it, which was the opposite of what a defender should do and exactly what someone who wanted to disrupt a pattern would choose.
The strike extended — just enough. Tomas's arm, committed to a path that assumed Mateo would be further away, overextended by a fraction. The alignment that connected his shoulder to his wrist to the blade's edge broke — not dramatically, not in a way that most observers would notice, but enough that the force behind the strike dissipated unevenly.
Mateo shifted the angle.
The alignment broke completely.
Tap.
His open palm touched Tomas's chest. Light. Precise. The contact carried no force — it didn't need to. It communicated everything.
"...You're open."
Silence settled over the yard. The two old men on the bench had stopped arguing. The cooking stall vendor's arms were folded. Even Diego, who had been eating throughout the entire exchange, had paused mid-bite.
Tomas stepped back.
"...Again."
Mateo blinked.
Persistent.
"...Fine."
They repeated the exchange. Again. And again. Each iteration slightly different from the last — Tomas adjusting, adapting, correcting the weaknesses that each round revealed while Mateo remained just ahead. Not faster. Not stronger. More precise. The gap between them was not a gap of power but of understanding, and with each exchange, that gap narrowed by fractions that were invisible to everyone except the two people inside them.
By the fifth exchange, the atmosphere around them changed.
Diego stopped speaking. That alone was notable enough to qualify as an event. Inés remained completely still, her attention fixed on the space between Mateo and Tomas with the focused intensity of someone who was not just watching but studying — cataloging each movement, each adjustment, each moment where the outcome shifted.
"...Something's off," someone muttered nearby — a trainee who had stopped to watch and could feel the change in the exchange's texture without being able to identify its source.
The sixth exchange began.
Tomas pushed harder. Closed distance faster. Compressed his movements and made them tighter, each strike arriving with less warning and more intent than the one before. He was no longer testing. He was committing — putting everything into each exchange with the stubborn, relentless determination that had defined him since the first time he picked up a wooden saber.
Mateo moved —
And something shifted.
The pendant beneath his clothing grew faintly warm.
Again.
The same sensation that had surfaced during previous moments of intensity — the sparring with the outsiders, the times when focus deepened beyond what his current cultivation should have allowed. But this time it arrived faster, settled deeper, and lasted longer.
Everything sharpened.
Angles aligned. Timing simplified. Movement reduced to its essential form — stripped of everything unnecessary, everything decorative, everything that existed because habit put it there rather than because function required it. The world didn't slow. It clarified. The way a lens brought a distant object into focus without changing the object itself.
Tomas's strike existed not as a single motion but as a sequence of positions connected by trajectories — each one predictable, each one carrying consequences that extended forward in time like branches growing from a single trunk. Mateo could see not just where the strike was but where it would be, and where the body behind it would be, and where the balance that supported the body would shift.
The strike —
Disappeared.
Not blocked. Not redirected.
Removed.
Mateo's hand moved through a path so clean, so direct, so fundamentally efficient that it bypassed the strike entirely — not intercepting it but occupying the space it needed to exist in before it could arrive there. The strike had nowhere to go. The motion that produced it collapsed inward, its energy unspent, its trajectory invalidated.
Tomas staggered back.
Not from impact — from the absence of it. His body had committed force that met nothing, and the sudden lack of resistance where resistance was expected threw his balance more effectively than any block could have.
Mateo stopped.
Silence.
The yard held its breath. The two old men on the bench were both leaning forward. The cooking stall vendor had set down the ladle she'd been holding. Diego's food had been forgotten entirely, which was historically unprecedented.
"That wasn't the same," Tomas said quietly. His breathing was heavier now, his expression carrying something that was not quite confusion and not quite concern — something in between that did not yet have a name.
Mateo looked at his hand.
The pendant's warmth was already fading, retreating back to its baseline as though the window of heightened perception had closed on its own schedule rather than his. His hand looked the same as it always had. It felt the same. But for those few seconds, it had moved with a certainty that did not belong to a boy his age, in a village this small, at a cultivation level this modest.
No... it wasn't.
The thought was quiet. Honest. The acknowledgment of something he could not yet explain but could no longer pretend hadn't happened. The pendant had responded to intensity before — warmth during focus, faint pulses during moments of heightened awareness. But this was different. This was not the pendant enhancing what he could do. This was the pendant briefly removing the barriers around what he already was.
And what he already was had moved through Tomas's strike as though it were a suggestion rather than an attack.
"...Forget it," Mateo said.
But Tomas didn't look convinced.
He stood where the stagger had left him, saber held loosely at his side, his gaze fixed on Mateo with the specific intensity of someone who had just felt something they could not explain and was not willing to pretend it hadn't happened. His eyes moved from Mateo's face to his hand and back — the same path the quiet outsider had traced during the sparring match with the Varex boy's group, searching for the mechanism, trying to locate the source of something that defied his framework.
He would not find it.
Not today.
But he would remember.
And Mateo, who understood the weight of being remembered by the wrong people for the right reasons, filed that knowledge alongside everything else and let the moment pass.
The yard resumed.
Slowly. Not all at once — in pieces, the way a room returned to normal after something unexpected. The two old men on the bench leaned back and resumed their argument, though with less conviction than before. The cooking stall vendor picked up her ladle. Diego retrieved his food and ate with the mechanical determination of someone who was processing what he had seen through the only coping mechanism he trusted.
The venison-fish vendor, who had watched the entire exchange with his mouth slightly open, shook himself and returned to shouting at passersby.
"Fresh cuts! Best in the village!"
No one stopped.
Inés remained where she was for a moment longer, her gaze still fixed on the space where Mateo and Tomas had stood. Then she turned and walked away without speaking.
She had seen enough.
For now.
That evening, Mateo sat on the low wall behind his family's home, the pendant resting in his palm. The sky above had shifted from the pale blue of late afternoon to the deeper tones that preceded evening, and the village had entered its transitional rhythm — the space between the day's activity and the night's quiet where sound carried differently and shadows lengthened in ways that made familiar spaces feel slightly less certain.
The pendant was warm.
Not the fading warmth that followed the sparring exchange. Something steadier. More deliberate. As though the earlier activation had not fully subsided but had instead settled into a lower, more sustainable frequency — a background hum that Mateo could feel through his fingertips when he held it but that disappeared the moment he let go.
He turned it slowly, watching the fractured star pattern catch the last of the daylight. The fragments within it seemed to shift as it moved — not dramatically, not in a way that anyone else would notice, but with the faint, almost imperceptible motion of pieces that were not quite settled. Not quite fixed.
Not quite finished.
Something stirred. Not in the pendant — in the connection between the pendant and whatever it was connected to. A resonance. A vibration too deep to hear and too subtle to see, existing only as a sensation that traveled from the pendant through his fingers and into his awareness like a word spoken in a language he almost understood.
He closed his hand around it.
"...Not yet," he murmured.
But closer than yesterday.
And that — for now — was enough.
