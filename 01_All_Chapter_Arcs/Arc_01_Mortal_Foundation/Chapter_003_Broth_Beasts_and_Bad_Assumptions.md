# Chapter 3 - Broth, Beasts, and Bad Assumptions

Chapter 3 — What the Sphere Didn't Show  

The assessment ended without ceremony.  
The elders departed the way they had arrived, unhurried and deliberate, and the weight they carried lingered a few moments after they were gone. Not because anyone  
feared them. Authority simply has a habit of leaving impressions behind.  
The testing sphere sat dormant on the stone platform. Moments earlier it had revealed affinities, hinted at futures, and quietly altered the trajectories of several young  
cultivators. Now it looked like nothing more than a smooth stone.  
The trainees remained scattered across the yard, processing the experience in their own ways. Some spoke quietly among themselves. Others compared results. Several had  
already begun assembling theories out of fragments of information that could not possibly support them.  
One insisted that earth affinity was superior because Elder Marcos had nodded when Tomas received his result. Another countered that Elder Marcos nodded at everyone.  
The argument showed no sign of reaching a conclusion.  
Mateo stood near the edge of the training area.  
His own assessment had passed exactly as intended. Lightning affinity. Mid-range. Stable. Forgettable. The elders had shown no unusual interest, no one had asked questions,  
and no one had looked twice.  
Exactly as planned.  
Yet while the elders had overlooked him, someone else had not.  
Tomas had looked twice. Not at the sphere. At Mateo.  
The look had not carried suspicion, because suspicion implies a degree of certainty. This was quieter than that, the expression of someone noticing that a piece of a puzzle  
did not sit correctly while lacking the information to understand why.  
Mateo noticed the look and filed it away.  
Inés had been watching too, though not for the same reason. The assessment interested her. Everything surrounding it interested her more. The way Mateo had studied the  
sphere before anyone explained it. The way he already seemed to understand its purpose. The way he always appeared to know slightly more than he should.  
But most of all, the movement.  
Earlier that morning she had watched him alter his stride while walking through the village. Not once. Repeatedly. Minor changes in posture, small shifts in balance,  
adjustments so subtle that most people would never register them at all.  
She had.  
Then she had watched him stop the instant he realized she was paying attention.  
That detail bothered her considerably more than the movement itself, because people only hide the things they consider worth hiding. The thought stayed with her long after  
the assessments concluded.  
Around them the yard slowly returned to life. Some trainees resumed practice, others drifted toward the market, and small groups formed and dissolved along the edges of  
the open space. The sphere was carried away. Normality made its attempt to reassert itself.  
Inés found herself looking toward Mateo again.  
He stood quietly off to the side, watching a pair of trainees debate whether affinity mattered more than effort. His expression revealed nothing, as usual.  
"In the village earlier."  
Mateo looked toward her. "What about it?"  
"You stopped."  
A brief pause. "I do that frequently."  
"The movement."  
His expression remained perfectly calm, which Inés found mildly irritating.  
"The adjustment you were making while walking."  
Another pause. "What adjustment?"  
"The one you stopped the moment you realized I was watching."  
Diego became interested immediately, and the transformation was almost visible. One moment he was drinking water. The next he was paying very close attention.  
"Oh, this sounds entertaining."  
"It isn't," Inés said.  
"That's usually when it becomes entertaining."  
She ignored him.  
Mateo considered the situation. "You were analyzing how I walk?"  
"I was observing."  
"Those are the same thing."  
"No."  
"They absolutely are."  
Diego nodded. "I agree with Mateo."  
Neither of them cared.  
Tomas looked between the two of them. His confusion lasted several seconds before understanding arrived, though not understanding of the movement itself, which  
remained entirely unclear to him. What became obvious was that Inés had noticed something, that Mateo knew exactly what she meant, and that both of them had  
immediately started talking around it.  
A solution presented itself. Simple, direct, and effective, which was generally how Tomas preferred his solutions.  
He folded his arms. "Then show her properly."  
The words entered the conversation with the calm certainty of someone resolving a problem that should never have been allowed to become complicated in the first place.  
Silence followed. Several nearby trainees looked over. Diego grinned. Inés blinked. Mateo sighed.  
Tomas continued. "If she's imagining it, she'll see she's imagining it." Then he gestured toward Mateo. "If she isn't..." He shrugged. "...then she'll see that too."  
The logic was infuriatingly reasonable, which explained why everyone immediately turned to look at Mateo.  
For a moment nobody moved. Mateo exhaled, very slowly.  
"...Later."  
Tomas blinked. "What, why?"  
"Let's get some food first." Mateo was already walking. "I'm hungry, and I'm fairly certain all of you are as well."  
There was a pause. Then Diego barked out a laugh.  

"He's right."  
A second pause followed, the group exchanged looks, and nobody objected. Without a word, they fell into step behind him. The discussion would wait. Food came first.  
And somehow, knowing full well that he had successfully avoided answering the question, Inés found herself following anyway.  
The movement remained unresolved. For now.  

The cooking stall sat slightly off the main path, built lower than the surrounding structures, with a wide stone slab at its center holding several iron pots of varying size and  
age. The largest had been blackened by years of continuous use, its surface scarred with the residue of a thousand meals cooked, served, and forgotten. The smallest held  
something that bubbled with a reluctance suggesting it had not yet decided what it wanted to become.  
Steam rose in slow curls, carrying root broth and herbs and something faintly bitter underneath that most customers never registered and that Mateo's awareness catalogued  
without being asked. Dried bark shavings, added not for flavor but for their mild restorative properties. Inexpensive, and effective enough for people who trained daily and  
needed to recover faster than rest alone allowed.  
Two older men sat on a low bench nearby, sharing a single bowl and arguing in the comfortable, well-rehearsed manner of people who had been having the same  
disagreement long enough that it had turned into companionship rather than conflict.  
"The broth was better last season," one said.  
"The broth is the same as it has always been," the other replied.  
"It had more depth."  
"You had fewer opinions."  
"That is unrelated."  
"It is entirely related."  
They continued. Neither showed any sign of reaching a conclusion, and neither appeared to want one.  
Mateo stopped near the stall. Not for long. Just enough.  
"...This place looks good," he said quietly.  
The woman behind the stall glanced up as he approached. She was sturdy in the way people who spent years lifting heavy pots and standing over cooking fires became  
sturdy, her forearms strong, her hands calloused, her eyes carrying the permanent skepticism of someone who had heard every excuse ever invented by a hungry cultivator.  
"You're not going to be picky today?" she asked.  
Mateo looked toward the largest pot. Then toward the smaller one. Then, briefly, toward the sun.  
The woman narrowed her eyes immediately. She recognized that look. That was the look he wore right before he said something strange.  
"Actually," Mateo said thoughtfully, "three days ago I arrived at approximately a quarter past noon."  
The woman sighed. It was beginning.  
"The path I took was slightly longer, which increased physical exertion prior to eating," he continued. "The additional exertion altered my nutritional requirements." He  
pointed vaguely toward the sky. "The sunlight was also stronger."  
"Of course it was."  
"Which means my perception of flavor may have been influenced by environmental factors."  
The woman stared at him. Mateo continued anyway.  
"My palate under today's conditions is operating with significantly different variables."  
A long silence followed. Several nearby customers had stopped listening to their own conversations. The woman maintained a perfectly expressionless face, then scooped  
broth into a bowl.  
"Here you go."  
Mateo accepted it. "...You didn't ask what I wanted."  
"You were going to eat this anyway."  
"...Probably."  
"And if I let you keep talking, the food would get cold."  
A pause. "Also probably."  
The woman shook her head. There was no irritation in it, only familiarity. She had watched Mateo grow up. Years ago she had tried to understand his explanations, and after  
that she had tried correcting them. Eventually she settled on ignoring roughly half of everything he said, and the system had worked remarkably well ever since.  
As Mateo walked away, one of the older men on the bench leaned toward the stall.  
"You know most children just say they're hungry."  
"I know."  
"And you let him keep doing that?"  
The woman glanced toward Mateo's retreating figure. "He enjoys it." A faint smile tugged at the corner of her mouth. "So do I."  
Tomas folded his arms loosely as he stood nearby, glancing toward the forest. The treeline was visible from here, a dark uneven wall of green that rose above the village's  
lower structures and pressed against the sky with the patient weight of something that had stood there long before the village existed and would remain long after it was  
gone.  
"...You go out often," he said.  
Mateo took the bowl offered to him. "...Often enough. But only the edges for now."  
Diego leaned over slightly, inspecting Mateo's portion with the critical eye of a man who considered himself an authority on food despite holding no qualifications beyond  
enthusiasm.  
"...That still looks like something I wouldn't eat willingly."  
"You've eaten worse," Tomas said.  
"...That was for money."  
Across the path, a thin vendor selling dried strips of something he insisted was venison but that smelled distinctly of fish called out to a passing woman with the aggressive  
optimism of a man who had not made a single sale all morning.  
"Fresh cuts! Best in the village! Prepared this morning!"  
She glanced at his display, then at him, and kept walking without adjusting her pace by so much as a step.  
The vendor's shoulders sagged. Then he straightened, rearranged the display, and prepared to shout at the next passerby with entirely undiminished conviction.  
Mateo watched the cycle with the quiet appreciation of someone who recognized persistence even when it was aimed in the wrong direction.  
He spoke without looking up from his bowl. "...What do you know about the forest?"  
Both Tomas and Inés glanced at him. The question was direct rather than casual, carrying the specific weight of something that had been considered before it was spoken.  
Not idle curiosity. A request for information that would be used.  
"...Enough," Inés replied after a moment, her tone measured, each word selected with the precision that characterized everything she did. "Outer areas are manageable. Mid-  
range zones are unpredictable. After that..."  
She paused slightly.  
"...We don't go that far."  
Tomas took a breath. "...My parents mentioned something once," he said slowly. "Travelers passing through. From further regions."  
Mateo looked up. "...Continue."  

"They said the deeper areas, far beyond where anyone here goes, have creatures that don't behave like what we train against." A short pause. "...High-ranking beasts."  
Diego leaned in immediately, his food temporarily forgotten, which, given Diego's relationship with food, indicated genuine interest.  
"...Define high-ranking."  
Tomas didn't look at him. "...Strong enough that someone below the Spirit Awakening Realm wouldn't even be able to approach." He hesitated. "...One of them joked that a  
single breath from something out there could kill someone at our level."  
Diego blinked. "...That feels excessive."  
"...They didn't sound like they were exaggerating."  
A longer pause settled over the group. The village went on around them, the vendor still shouting, the two old men still arguing, the pots still bubbling, but the conversation  
had opened a pocket of quiet inside all of it that the surrounding sound could not reach into.  
Then Tomas added quietly, "...They also mentioned something else."  
Mateo's gaze sharpened slightly. "...Say it."  
"...There are things deeper that don't even register as beasts."  
Silence.  
"...Meaning?"  
"...Meaning they don't follow patterns we understand." Another pause. "...Some of them can shatter mountains."  
Diego slowly set his bowl down. The gesture was deliberate, the careful placement of something he no longer had the appetite to hold.  
"...I think I preferred not knowing that."  
Mateo was silent for a moment. His expression had not changed, holding the same calm analytical neutrality he wore through every conversation, but something behind his  
eyes had shifted. Not fear. Not excitement. Something closer to recognition, the look of a man receiving confirmation of something he had already suspected.  
Then, "...Distance?"  
Tomas blinked. "...What?"  
"...How far?"  
"...Kilometers," Tomas said. "A lot of them."  
Mateo nodded once.  
That is very interesting. The deeper zones operate on a different scale entirely. The power gradient is not linear, it escalates. What exists at the outer edges is manageable. What  
exists at the core is beyond anything the village can comprehend. And somewhere between those two extremes is the point where training becomes survival and survival becomes  
education.  
One day I will have to check it out.  
He stood. Finished.  
"...Alright. Let's continue then."  

That was all it took.  
The training yard filled again, this time with intent. Something in the conversation about the forest had sharpened each of them, and it carried over into the practice. The two  
old men on the bench paused their argument to watch. The cooking stall vendor leaned against her counter, her expression shifting from professional disinterest into mild  
curiosity. Even the venison-fish vendor stopped shouting long enough to glance over.  
Tomas stepped forward first, without hesitation this time. Something in the assessment, or perhaps in the conversation about the forest, had settled a weight into his stance  
that had not been there that morning. He moved with the deliberate focus of someone who had decided this session would not be routine.  
"...You're not using a weapon?" he asked.  
"...Not necessary," Mateo replied.  
A flicker of irritation crossed Tomas's face. Not anger, but the frustration of a man who took his training seriously being told the encounter did not warrant a tool.  
"...We'll see."  
He moved.  
The first strike came fast, clean, controlled, and direct, the wooden saber cutting through the air along a line that was technically correct and practically predictable. Mateo  
stepped aside with so little effort that the movement barely qualified as evasion.  
Efficient, but too predictable. The line of attack follows the textbook arc exactly. No variation. No feint. No adjustment for the opponent's position. He is fighting the form rather  
than fighting me.  
Tomas adjusted instantly, the second strike tighter and faster, angled lower to cut off the lateral escape Mateo had used the first time.  
Better.  
Mateo redirected it. Not blocked, but removed from usefulness. His hand met the flat of the blade at the precise point where Tomas's force was transitioning from  
acceleration into extension, and guided it past his body along a path that spent all of its energy without ever threatening his position.  
"...You're not moving away," Tomas said, breathing slightly harder.  
"...No."  
I'm moving around you.  
The exchange tightened. Tomas recognized that the standard approach was insufficient and began adapting in real time, compressing his strikes, varying his angles, hunting  
for a line that Mateo could not simply redirect.  
Mateo stepped forward. Just slightly. A half-step that closed distance rather than creating it, which was the opposite of what a defender was supposed to do and precisely  
what someone who wanted to disrupt a pattern would choose.  
Tomas's arm, committed to a path that assumed Mateo would be further away, overextended by a fraction. The alignment running from his shoulder through his wrist to the  
blade's edge broke, not dramatically and not in a way most observers would catch, but enough that the force behind the strike dissipated unevenly.  
Mateo shifted the angle, and the alignment broke completely.  
Tap.  
His open palm touched Tomas's chest. Light. Precise. The contact carried no force behind it, and it did not need to. It communicated everything.  
"...You're open."  
Silence settled over the yard. The two old men had stopped arguing. Even Diego, who had been eating throughout, paused mid-bite.  
Tomas stepped back. "...Again."  
Mateo blinked.  
You are very persistent.  
"...Fine."  
They repeated the exchange. Again. And again. Each round differed slightly from the last, Tomas correcting the weaknesses the previous one had exposed while Mateo stayed  
just ahead of him. Not faster. Not stronger. More precise. The gap between them was never a gap of power but of understanding, and it narrowed by fractions invisible to  
everyone except the two people standing inside them.  
By the fifth exchange, the atmosphere around them changed.  
Diego stopped speaking, which on its own qualified as an event. Inés stood completely still, her attention fixed on the space between the two of them, cataloguing each  
movement and each adjustment and each moment the outcome tilted.  
"...Something's off," a trainee muttered nearby, one of several who had stopped to watch and could feel the texture of the exchange changing without being able to name the  

source.  
The sixth exchange began.  
Tomas pushed harder, closed distance faster, compressed his movements until each strike arrived with less warning and more intent than the one before it. He was no longer  
testing. He was committing, throwing everything into each exchange with the stubborn, relentless determination that had defined him since the first time he picked up a  
wooden saber.  
Then the change began, so subtly that most of the onlookers never noticed it at all.  
At first Tomas looked no different than he had a few exchanges earlier. His attacks carried the same strength. His stance remained unchanged. Even his expression barely  
moved.  
But Mateo noticed.  
He had seen it surface before, in the moments when Tomas became completely absorbed in what he was doing. There was nothing mystical about it and it had nothing to do  
with cultivation levels. It was simply the state that emerges when every distraction falls away and all attention settles onto a single task.  
As the match continued, Tomas sank deeper into it. The noise of the yard stopped reaching him. The voices, the conversations, the running commentary from the trainees  
who had gathered to watch all faded out, until only the exchange in front of him remained.  
Tomas attacked again.  
The strike wasn't faster. It wasn't stronger. And yet it was better.  
The motion traveled a straighter path than before. Tomas's shoulder stayed relaxed until the last possible moment. His weight transferred more smoothly through his hips  
and legs. Small adjustments appeared on their own, as though his body had begun correcting its own mistakes without waiting for conscious thought to arrive.  
Another exchange followed. Then another.  
With each one, Tomas wasted less. Extra steps disappeared. Unnecessary shifts of balance vanished. Large motions grew smaller and more precise. It did not look like  
something he was doing on purpose. It looked more as though his body had started recognizing which movements actually mattered and had begun quietly discarding the  
ones that didn't.  
When he attacked, his footing settled more cleanly beneath him. When he defended, his responses took shorter routes. Instead of forcing openings, he began flowing toward  
them.  
To most of those watching, Tomas probably looked exactly the same as he had ten minutes earlier. To Mateo, the difference was obvious.  
Tomas was learning. Not through words, and not through instruction. Through experience. Every exchange delivered immediate feedback, every successful movement  
reinforced itself, and every inefficient action exposed its own weakness.  
For the first time, Tomas wasn't focused on winning the spar. He was focused entirely on understanding it.  
The realization brought a faint smile to Mateo's face. Now he understood why the session felt different.  
Tomas was beginning to see what Mateo had been trying to show him all along. That combat was never about collecting techniques. It was not about memorizing forms. It  
was about recognizing which angles mattered, which movements created opportunities, and which actions existed only because habit had put them there.  
And little by little, Tomas was starting to see the difference for himself.  
The last exchange ended without a tap, without a correction, and without any of the small humiliations that had punctuated the earlier rounds. Tomas simply stopped where  
he stood, saber lowered, breathing hard, and remained there a moment while whatever he had just done finished settling into him.  
"...That one felt different," he said.  
"...It was."  
Tomas looked up. "You're not going to explain it."  
"...You already know what changed. You just haven't finished putting it into words." Mateo turned toward the edge of the yard. "Words come last. That's usually the correct  
order."  
Tomas stood there a while longer, running the exchange back through himself, checking it against the version of it he thought he had performed.  
Diego, who had been eating throughout the entire session and had reached the bottom of whatever he had brought with him, looked between them with the expression of a  
man who had watched something happen and had no framework for it whatsoever.  
"...Did he win?"  
"No," Tomas said.  
"...Did you win?"  
"No."  
Diego considered that. "...Then what was the point?"  
Tomas didn't answer, mostly because the answer would have taken longer than Diego's attention span allowed, and partly because he had only understood it himself about  
ninety seconds ago.  
The yard resumed around them in pieces. The two old men leaned back and picked up their argument again, though with noticeably less conviction than before. The cooking  
stall vendor retrieved her ladle. The venison-fish vendor, who had watched the entire session with his mouth slightly open, shook himself and returned to shouting at  
passersby.  
"Fresh cuts! Best in the village!"  
No one stopped.  
Inés stayed where she was a moment longer, her gaze still fixed on the space where Mateo and Tomas had stood. She had spent the entire session watching Mateo's feet  
rather than his hands, and what she had seen there did not match what anyone else in the yard believed they had watched.  
Then she turned and walked away without a word.  
She had seen enough. For now.  

That evening, Mateo sat on the low wall behind his family's home with the pendant resting in his palm. The sky had shifted from the pale blue of late afternoon into the  
deeper tones that came before evening, and the village had settled into its transitional rhythm, that space between the day's activity and the night's quiet where sound carries  
differently and shadows lengthen until familiar places feel slightly less certain than they had an hour before.  
The pendant was warm.  
It had been warm for weeks now, and the warmth had been changing. Not in strength, but in character. Where it had once come and gone without pattern, arriving during  
moments of focus and fading afterward, it had begun to hold. Steadier. More deliberate. A low background hum he could feel through his fingertips while he held it and that  
vanished the instant he let go.  
He turned it slowly, watching the fractured star pattern catch the last of the daylight. The fragments inside it seemed to shift as it moved, not dramatically, and not in any way  
another person would have caught, but with the faint and almost imperceptible motion of pieces that were not quite settled.  
Not quite fixed. Not quite finished.  
Something stirred. Not in the pendant, but in the connection between the pendant and whatever it was tied to. A resonance. A vibration too deep to hear and too subtle to  
see, existing only as a sensation that traveled from the pendant through his fingers and into his awareness, and that he understood without being able to translate.  
He closed his hand around it.  
"...Not yet," he murmured.  
But closer than yesterday.  
And that, for now, was enough.
