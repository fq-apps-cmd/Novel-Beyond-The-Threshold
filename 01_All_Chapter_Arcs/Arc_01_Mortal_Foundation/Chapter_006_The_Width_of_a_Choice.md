# Chapter 6 - The Width of a Choice

Chapter 6  
The Width of a Choice  

Later, after the yard had emptied, after Diego had wandered off muttering about food, after Tomas had flexed his healed arm three times to test whether the  
fix would hold and then once more because three had not been convincing enough, Mateo sat alone at the edge of the training ground.  
The village continued around him in the specific rhythm that follows disruption. Not the normal rhythm, but the one that pretends to be. The sounds  
were the same and the movements were the same, and the texture underneath had changed anyway.  
Somewhere to the east, the blacksmith's hammer struck in its uneven rhythm: three close together, a pause, then two more. The pause was shorter than  
usual today. The smith was working faster to make up the time he had lost watching the confrontation from his open forge, and Mateo had noticed the  
hammer go silent at the moment everything turned, and stay silent for several seconds afterward.  
The smith had felt it.  
He didn't know what he had felt. But he had stopped working, and stopping mid-strike was something the smith did not do unless something had  
become more urgent than heated metal.  
A child laughed too loudly somewhere to the north and was immediately shushed, the sharp reflexive correction of a parent who was more on edge  
than usual and whose threshold for noise had contracted accordingly. The laugh cut off mid-note and left a brief wounded silence behind it, followed  
by the quieter sounds of a child adjusting their behavior without understanding why the rules had changed.  
The smell of evening cooking drifted over from a home two paths away, something with ginger and too much smoke, assembled out of whatever  
remained in the kitchen rather than chosen. It was comfort food, the kind people made when the day had been harder than expected and the body  
wanted something familiar instead of something good.  
Mateo did not notice any of it.  
He was looking at his hands.  
Not the way he usually did, cataloguing and assessing and filing observations for later use. This was the quiet, uncomfortable business of confronting  
something he had not expected to feel.  
He had almost not held back.  
Not during the first exchange. The first exchange had been clean, controlled, and simple, a mechanical adjustment requiring no more thought than  
breathing. The outsider had attacked, and Mateo had made the attack irrelevant by refusing to be where it expected him.  
That had been easy. That had been nothing.  
But the second exchange, when the outsider pushed past sparring into something personal, fueled by wounded pride and the desperate need to reclaim  
in public what had been taken from him in public, Mateo had felt something shift.  
Not in his technique. Not in his awareness.  
In his patience.  
The patience was not a personality trait. It was not the natural temperament of a calm person, and it was not the practiced composure of someone who  
had learned to manage his emotions. It was a structure, deliberately built and continuously maintained, standing between what he was capable of and  
what he chose to do. It required constant attention, and the attention had become invisible through habit without ever becoming unnecessary.  
And for a fraction of a breath, a sliver of a moment so thin it barely qualified as time, the structure had cracked.  
He had not been a boy in a training yard choosing to hold back.  
He had been something else. Something that did not hold back. Something that answered a challenge automatically and completely, with no restraint  
available anywhere in the process.  
The outsider's wrist. The angle of the incoming strike. The precise amount of force required not merely to redirect it but to dismantle the arm  
delivering it, to fold the joint past its structural limit, to separate tendon from bone at the exact point where the body's architecture gave way to lateral  
force, to end the exchange so absolutely that the concept of again would cease to exist as a possibility.  
He had seen all of it.  
Not as options. As inevitabilities.  
The outsider's arm, extended past the point of balanced recovery, had existed in a state where damage was not a possibility but a geometric certainty.  
All that remained was the application of force along the correct vector, at the correct magnitude, at the correct moment.  
And Mateo's body had known the vector. It had known the magnitude. It had known the moment.  
For that sliver of a breath, the only thing standing between the outsider's functional arm and something far worse had been a choice Mateo was not  
entirely certain he had made consciously.  
That was the part that unsettled him.  
Not the power. He had always understood the power, had carried it and wielded it and finally abandoned it, because understanding alone had never  
been enough to make it mean anything. Power without limitation was irrelevance. He had learned that across spans of time this world could not  
measure, and the learning had been thorough enough that he had dismantled himself rather than go on existing as an answer wit hout a question.  
What unsettled him was the speed of the instinct.  

He had chosen this life. He had chosen limitation. He had chosen to exist inside boundaries that would force him to grow the way everyone else grew,  
through effort and failure and the slow accumulation of experience that could not be shortcut. He had chosen the weight of bone and the constraint of  
flesh and the daily frustration of a body that could not do what the mind behind it had once done without thinking about it at all.  
He had chosen all of that.  
The instinct did not care about his choices.  
It lived beneath them, older than this body and older than this world, woven into whatever remained of what he had been before he fell. It surfaced not  
when he was thinking and not when he was deciding, but in the gap between, where thought had not arrived yet and something deeper filled the  
silence.  
It was something vast trying to protect something small, without understanding that the something small was the entire point.  
If Tomas had not stepped in—  
Mateo exhaled slowly.  
He would have held back.  
Probably. Almost certainly.  
...Most likely.  
The fact that he could not say definitely was the problem.  
He sat with that for a long time. Not analyzing it, not yet. Just acknowledging that it was there, measuring how deep it went, letting it exist before he  
tried to decide what it meant.  
The training yard had emptied fully now. The grooves in the dirt were already softening at their edges, dust settling back into the spaces feet had  
carved out hours before. The shallow arcs of his own footwork were still visible, precise and evenly spaced, documented by packed earth that kept the  
record whether anyone asked it to or not. The outsiders' prints were there too, deeper and less organized, left by people moving with force instead of  
precision.  
And Tomas's prints, between them. Steady. Grounded. The marks of someone who had put himself exactly where he needed to be.  
Mateo looked at those for a while.  
Then at his hands again.  
He closed his eyes and let the evening air settle against his skin. The ground beneath him was cool and still held the impressions of the day's training.  
Somewhere nearby a bird called once and did not repeat itself, a single note that existed briefly in the air and then dissolved, the bird having made its  
point and seen no reason to elaborate.  
This is why I chose this.  
The thought surfaced clearly, without the usual analytical distance. Not processed. Not filtered. Just present.  
Not to be powerful again. Not to prove anything. Not to observe from above.  
To be close enough to the edge that stepping back actually costs something.  
Because a choice that costs nothing is not a choice. It is a default, and defaults do not grow. They do not teach. They do not force the kind of  
reckoning that moves understanding out of the mind and into the body, where it can be relied upon.  
He had chosen to be small enough that the instinct to be vast could challenge him.  
And today, for the first time, the challenge had been real.  
He opened his eyes.  
The light had shifted out of the warm gold of late afternoon into the cooler tones ahead of evening, and the shadows thrown by the surrounding trees  
had lengthened across the dirt until they merged into a single continuous shade, turning the yard from a sunlit space into something quieter and less  
defined.  
He looked at his hands one more time.  
Then he stood.  
I need to be more careful.  
A pause.  
Not with them.  
With myself.  
He rolled his shoulders once, letting the tension leave unevenly, and walked toward the village path.  
The thought stayed with him. Not as fear. As respect, for the thing he carried that did not yet fully understand it was supposed to be sleeping.  
The walk back through the village was slower than it needed to be.  
Not because he was tired, though his body carried the residual tension of the afternoon in ways that would not fully clear until sleep. It was that the  
village at this hour existed in a state of transition that revealed things the daytime rhythm covered up.  
The vendors were closing their stalls along the market path, covering goods and securing displays and sweeping the ground in front of their spaces  
with the automatic efficiency of motions repeated thousands of times. The cloth merchant folded his remaining bolts with a precision that had gone  
slightly aggressive, each fold sharp enough to suggest he was working through a frustration that had nothing to do with fabri c. The pot seller beside  
him stacked her wares in silence, which was unusual. The two of them normally traded a final round of commentary at closing, the bracket that shut  
whatever argument the morning had opened.  
Tonight, neither spoke.  

Mateo noticed.  
Further along, a group of older villagers stood near the well, their conversation low enough that the words were indistinct and their posture loud  
enough that the words were unnecessary. They stood closer together than talking required, and their gazes moved outward at intervals, toward the  
training yard, toward the path the outsiders had used, toward the forest edge, before returning to each other.  
The conversation was not about what had happened. It was about what might happen next.  
The village had registered the outsiders' presence late, the way a bruise announces itself hours after the impact, once something touches it.  
A woman he recognized, the one who sold preserved vegetables near the herb stall, stood in her doorway with her arms folded, watching the path with  
the unfocused attention of someone not looking at anything specific but looking for something in general. When Mateo passed, her gaze settled on  
him briefly. Her expression carried something that had not been there that morning. Not fear, and not anger, but the particular alertness that arrives  
when a person's understanding of their own environment has been updated against their preference.  
She nodded at him.  
Mateo nodded back.  
Neither spoke, and the exchange communicated more than words would have. She had seen the confrontation. She had seen the outsiders. She had  
seen him stand in the center of the yard and say two words that ended all of it. And she was still working out what that meant about the boy who had  
walked past her stall every morning for years.  
Mateo continued walking.  
The village's evening sounds reassembled around him as he moved, doors closing, low conversations resuming, the clink of bowls being set down on  
tables, the creak of wood as homes settled into the cooling air. Somewhere to the south, Diego's voice was audible, not the words but the cadence,  
rapid and animated, aimed at someone who was probably not interested and would listen anyway, because Diego's voice was not the kind that  
permitted disengagement.  
The normalcy was deliberate. The village was choosing to be normal the way a person chooses to breathe evenly after being startled, not because the  
calm is real but because the alternative is worse.  
Mateo understood that.  
He had been doing the same thing all day.  

A Small, Quiet Problem  

That night, Mateo stood outside the herb shop.  
The edge of the village blended directly into the forest here. No walls, no clear separation, just less light and thicker shadow and a shift in the air that  
suggested going any further meant going into something else entirely. The darkness did not deepen uniformly. Some areas held their shape, edges  
defined by the faint light leaking out of nearby homes, while others absorbed it completely and left the ground beneath them invisible.  
The shop was a modest structure, wider than most homes but shorter, with a low-pitched roof built to keep the interior cool and dry. The walls were  
timber, darkened by years of exposure to the vapors old man Chen's preparations produced, and even from outside the scent was detectable: dried  
herbs, ground minerals, preserved roots, and something sharp and faintly acrid underneath that Mateo suspected was the binding agent Chen used in  
his more expensive work.  
The shop was closed.  
"Didn't sell to you earlier, did he?" Diego whispered.  
He had appeared roughly ten minutes ago out of the direction of his own home, with the casual stealth of someone who had been waiting for exactly  
this opportunity and tracking Mateo's movements since dinner. His arrival had not been announced. His presence had simply become a fact, which  
was how Diego's presence always became a fact.  
"...No."  
"...So now we're here."  
Mateo studied the door. The latch was old iron, sturdy-looking from the outside, opened and closed so many times that the mechanism barely held  
alignment anymore. The metal had worn smooth at the contact points and the housing had loosened in its mount, not enough to be visually obvious,  
but enough that anyone who understood latches could feel the play in it from the outside.  
Mateo understood latches.  
"...Old man Chen refused the sale," he said, his tone calm and entirely unbothered. "He said I was too young to be purchasing materials above my  
comprehension."  
Diego stifled a laugh. "He said that? To you?"  
"He said it loudly. Then he turned to the next customer and sold them the exact same thing without asking a single question."  
"...So he's just—"  
"Difficult," Mateo said. "Deliberately and consistently difficult. He also overcharges anyone under twenty, lectures customers who ask too many  
questions, and once refused to sell someone a basic healing root because they mispronounced its name."  
Diego pressed a hand over his mouth.  
Mateo continued, his expression perfectly neutral.  
"He'll survive missing a few supplies."  
Diego's shoulders were shaking now. "...You've been keeping track of all of this?"  
"...Observation is a habit."  

"That's not observation. That's a grudge list."  
"...It's a well-organized grudge list. There's a difference."  
Diego wheezed quietly. Mateo's mouth twitched, just barely, just at the corner, and it was there. Around them the night sounds of the village  
continued undisturbed, crickets in the undergrowth, the distant murmur of a conversation two homes over, the faint creak of the canopy adjusting to  
the evening wind.  
"Stay quiet," Mateo said.  
"...Wait, are we actually—"  
Mateo was already moving.  
The window latch was simple and poorly secured. He had noticed the weakness three visits ago, while old man Chen was too busy lecturing a woman  
about the correct way to store dried bark to notice Mateo studying every entry point in the building. The window sat at the western face, partially  
concealed by an overgrown shrub Chen had never bothered to trim and had never realized was covering anything that needed covering.  
Mateo slipped through with the economy of someone who had practiced entry through narrow spaces, not because he had planned this specific visit,  
but because knowing how to get into and out of places efficiently was, in his view, a fundamental skill most people neglected.  
Predictable.  
Within moments he was inside.  
The interior was darker than the night outside it, the single window providing the only ambient light. Shelves lined three walls, each organized  
according to a system old man Chen insisted was intuitive and everyone else considered incomprehensible. Bundles of dried herbs hung from the  
ceiling beams in rows, casting strange shadows that shifted faintly in the draft from the window. Years of accumulated preparation had soaked into the  
wood and the stone and the clay jars until the building itself had become a remedy.  
Despite the apparent chaos, Mateo could see the logic. Chen arranged by interaction properties rather than by type. Herbs that should not be stored  
near each other were separated by the maximum available distance, while those that benefited from proximity were clustered together. It was not  
intuitive. It was correct. And the fact that Chen understood herb interaction at that level while simultaneously being the most difficult merchant in the  
village was, Mateo had to admit, somewhat impressive.  
He would never tell Chen that.  
Diego followed him in, landing with significantly less grace and immediately bumping a low shelf. A clay jar wobbled. Diego lunged for it, caught it,  
set it back, and exhaled. The jar, unimpressed by its near-death experience, continued existing.  
"...This is a terrible idea," Diego whispered.  
"...Yes."  
"...We're still doing it?"  
"...Yes."  
Mateo moved between the shelves, having already mapped the interior on previous visits, and his hands selected without hesitation. Dried stems from  
the second shelf, chosen for concentration rather than size. A sealed jar from behind a row of lesser roots, its wax intact, the contents spared the air  
exposure that had degraded everything in front of it. A small cloth pouch tucked into a spot Chen clearly believed was hidden behind a stack of low-  
grade mineral powder, which only ever worked on people who had not yet learned that the most valuable items are always stored behind the least  
valuable ones.  
"Think of it this way," Mateo said. "He spent twenty minutes yesterday explaining to a farmer that his cough medicine was an art form, and then  
charged him triple."  
Diego, crouched behind a barrel that concealed him from no angle whatsoever and that he seemed to be using on principle, let out a sharp breath  
through his nose. "...You're robbing him out of principle."  
"...I'm redistributing resources from someone who hoards them behind unnecessary hostility."  
"That's robbery."  
"...That's perspective."  
Diego pressed both hands over his face. His entire body was vibrating with suppressed laughter, the full-body tremor of someone trying to be silent  
while his body refused to cooperate. Mateo glanced over at him, and for a moment the composure broke. A grin spread across his face, sharp and  
unhidden, and it belonged to someone enjoying this considerably more than any reasonable person should.  
It was the grin of someone who had spent the afternoon confronting the terrifying depth of what lived inside him and had decided the appropriate  
response was to rob an herb shop with his best friend.  
The logic was questionable.  
The therapy was effective.  
"...He's going to blame the wind," Mateo said.  
Diego lost it.  
A muffled, strangled laugh escaped through his fingers, and Mateo had to physically turn away, because the grin on his face had widened into  
something that would have horrified every elder in the village. His shoulders shook once, a single involuntary tremor that he suppressed immediately,  
though not before Diego saw it and pointed at him with the triumphant energy of someone who had just witnessed a rare natural phenomenon.  
"You laughed," Diego whispered. "You actually—"  
"...That did not happen."  
"It happened. I saw it. Your shoulders moved."  
"...Wind."  

"We are indoors."  
"...Indoor wind."  
"That is not a thing."  
"...It is now."  
Diego pressed both hands over his mouth again, eyes streaming. Mateo, whose composure had technically recovered but whose eyes still carried the  
brightness of someone one comment away from losing it again, focused very deliberately on the shelves.  
"...We are terrible people," Diego managed.  
"...Yes," Mateo said, carefully replacing a jar he had moved to reach the one behind it, making sure nothing looked disturbed. "...But he started it."  
They slipped back out through the window. The night air met them with a cool clarity the shop's enclosed, herb-saturated atmosphere had been  
missing, and their senses opened back up into the space around them.  
Diego wiped his eyes.  
"...If anyone asks—"  
"...We were never here."  
"...Right."  
A pause.  
"...He's definitely going to blame the wind."  
"...Absolutely."  
They walked back in silence, though the silence was broken at intervals by Diego's shoulders shaking and by Mateo's suspiciously neutral expression,  
which was not fooling either of them. The path to the village center was lit only by the glow from nearby homes and the faint starlight coming through  
gaps in the canopy, enough to navigate and not enough to read expressions.  
Which was convenient.  
Because Mateo's expression, had it been visible, would have contradicted every piece of composure he had maintained all day.  
He was smiling.  
Not the small controlled smile he permitted during conversations. Not the faint acknowledgment of amusement he occasionally allowed to surface. A  
real one. The kind that exists because the person wearing it is simply, uncomplicatedly happy. Not about the herbs, and not about outsmarting Chen,  
but about the plain and irreplaceable fact of walking through a village at night beside someone who made the world weigh less.  
Diego did not see the smile.  
But he would have understood it.  
No wasted motion.  
Except for the part where we both nearly lost composure behind a barrel.  
That was entirely wasted.  
And entirely worth it.  
Back under the open sky, Mateo held the pendant again.  
He sat on the low wall behind his family's home, the same spot he had used on dozens of evenings before, the stone worn smooth and cool against the  
backs of his legs, the position offering a clear view of the sky through the widest gap in the canopy. The village had settled into its last rhythm of the  
day, the deep quiet that arrives after the meals are finished and the conversations have run their course and the only sounds left are the ones the night  
produces on its own.  
The pendant pulsed.  
Warmer now.  
The warmth felt more consistent than before. Not stronger, but more aligned. The pendant's energy, which had previously wandered between activity  
and dormancy, seemed to have found a frequency it preferred and was holding it with increasing confidence. The pulse arrived at regular intervals, not  
the rapid urgent beat of something demanding attention, but the slow steady rhythm of something settling into a pattern it intended to keep.  
Something is aligning.  
The thought felt closer this time. More defined. Not the vague sense of something happening that he had carried for weeks, but a specific observation.  
The fragments inside the pendant were moving. Not randomly, and not chaotically. Toward each other. Toward a configuration he could almost  
perceive and could not yet name.  
Not broken.  
A pause.  
Becoming.  
He closed his hand around it.  
The warmth intensified slightly at the contact, responding to his touch the way it always did, but with a reliability that had not been there before. Less  
intermittent. More connected.  
This world is only the beginning.  
The thought arrived with a clarity that surprised him. The content was not new. He had always known, at some level, that the mortal world sat at the  
lowest rung of a ladder extending far past anything anyone here could perceive. But the knowing had been abstract and distant, the kind that lives in  

the mind without ever touching the body.  
Tonight, it touched.  
And for the first time, it did not feel distant.  
It felt real.
