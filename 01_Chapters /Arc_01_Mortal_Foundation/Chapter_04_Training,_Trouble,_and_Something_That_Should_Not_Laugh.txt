Chapter 4 — Training, Trouble, and Something That Should Not Laugh

The sun had not fully risen when Tomas arrived.
The forest still held most of its shadows, long strips of dim light slipping unevenly through the canopy above. Some beams fell cleanly to the ground, landing in warm pools that would shift as the morning progressed and the angle changed. Others seemed to fragment before reaching the earth, broken by layers of leaves into scattered pieces that left patches of space slightly dimmer than expected — places where the light arrived but could not quite commit to staying.
The training yard — if it could be called that — was nothing more than a widened patch of dirt carved out by use rather than design. No one had planned it. No one had leveled it. No one had drawn a boundary or posted a marker that said "train here." It had simply emerged over years, the way paths emerged — through the accumulated weight of repetition pressing the same ground in the same patterns until the earth itself conceded and hardened into something that resembled a surface.
The ground was uneven in places, marked by shallow grooves, scuffs, and compacted sections where repeated motion had shaped it over time. Some sections held firmer than others — the area near the eastern roots where Mateo usually practiced had been compressed by so many sessions that the dirt there was nearly as solid as stone. Other patches, closer to the edges where fewer feet landed, still gave slightly under pressure, the soil softer and less certain of its purpose.
The differences were subtle enough that most would ignore them completely.
Mateo did not ignore them.
He had mapped every variation in this ground over the past year. He knew where the soil compressed cleanly and where it shifted. He knew where hidden stones created unexpected resistance and where root systems running just beneath the surface produced faint ridges that altered the angle of a planted foot by fractions of a degree. He knew which sections held morning moisture longest and which dried first, and how the difference in friction between the two affected the transition speed of a pivoting step.
The yard was his instrument. He had tuned it through repetition the way a musician tuned a string — not by changing it, but by understanding it so completely that its imperfections became features rather than obstacles.
Tomas did not call out or announce himself. Instead, he stood at the edge of the yard, his wooden saber resting lightly against his shoulder, silently observing the quiet movements of the boy already there.
Mateo noticed immediately.
He continued his motion regardless — stepping forward, shifting his weight with deliberate precision, and letting his stance settle naturally before coming to a stop. As his foot settled, the ground held slightly differently than the step before. Not wrong — just not identical. A patch of soil that had been compressed during yesterday's session had partially recovered overnight, and the resistance it offered was fractionally less than what his muscle memory expected.
He adjusted without thinking. The correction was invisible.
Only then did he glance over his shoulder.
"You're early," Mateo said calmly.
Tomas didn't move. His gaze remained steady. "You're later than you should be."
Mateo tilted his head slightly, glancing briefly toward the sky where the sun had only just begun to rise through the dense spread of trees. The light was still more grey than gold, the kind of pre-dawn illumination that made everything look slightly less real than it would in an hour.
"The sun isn't even fully up."
"Exactly."
A brief silence followed.
Somewhere beyond the yard, a dull thud echoed through the village — someone already training, or possibly arguing in a way that required physical reinforcement. The sound carried farther than expected, bouncing off the timber walls of nearby homes before fading more quickly than it should have, as though the morning air was not yet warm enough to sustain it. Neither of them acknowledged it.
Closer by, the first sounds of the village waking had begun to layer into the background — a door opening, footsteps on a porch, the distant clatter of someone setting up a cooking station with the resigned efficiency of a person who had performed the same task every morning for years and had stopped pretending to enjoy it.
Mateo sighed quietly, rolling his shoulders as if preparing himself for something unnecessarily repetitive.
This is going to be exhausting. Not because the training itself will be difficult. Because Tomas has arrived with the specific energy of someone who spent the night thinking about something and has decided that today is the day he addresses it. That energy always produces longer sessions.
"What are we doing?" he finally asked.
Tomas stepped forward, lowering the saber into a ready position. His stance settled smoothly — no wasted motion, no hesitation. Even the slight shift of his footing distributed evenly across the uneven ground, each adjustment so natural it looked like the ground had been designed to accommodate him rather than the other way around.
"No holding back."
Mateo blinked slowly.
We have already had this conversation. Multiple times. Each time, the definition of "no holding back" has shifted to accommodate whatever Tomas observed during the previous session. The phrase no longer means what it meant originally. It has become a moving target — which, given Tomas's persistence, is probably the point.
"Define that," he said.
"Stop correcting only halfway," Tomas answered immediately.
Mateo studied him more closely this time. The change was subtle, but present. There was less impatience than before, less of the need to prove something immediately. Instead, there was focus — intent that had taken shape overnight, crystallizing from the vague frustration of their previous sessions into something more structured. More purposeful.
He is trying to understand it rather than rush through it. He spent the night analyzing what happened and arrived with a specific objective rather than a general desire to improve. That is a meaningful shift in approach.
That is good.
Annoying, but good.
"You won't like the result," Mateo said after a short pause.
Tomas didn't hesitate.
"...Try me."
Mateo exhaled once, then stepped fully into position.
"Fine."
Training began differently that morning.
Tomas slowed everything down.
Instead of pressing forward as he had before, he broke movement into pieces — stance, breath, alignment, then strike. Each part was deliberate, repeated until it felt natural before continuing. The rhythm settled across the yard, not rushed, but not stagnant — the pace of someone who had realized that speed was the reward for precision rather than a substitute for it. Even the dust that lifted from their movements seemed to follow the pattern, rising in small clouds with each step and settling in inconsistent arcs that mapped their positions across the yard like a slow-motion record of the session.
"Like this," Tomas said, demonstrating a controlled swing, then resetting carefully. The saber moved through a clean arc — tighter than his usual style, the transition between extension and recovery smoother than it had been even a week ago. "Again."
Mateo watched closely, his eyes narrowing slightly.
Controlled pace. Reduced variation. He is isolating the structure of each movement instead of forcing the outcome. The swing's arc has tightened by approximately three centimeters since our last session, and his recovery has shortened by nearly a full beat. He has been practicing this specific motion independently.
"...Fine," Mateo said, stepping forward.
They moved together now — step, pivot, strike. The rhythm formed quickly, each repetition smoothing out inefficiency. Dust shifted underfoot, marking their steps in shallow arcs across the already worn ground, though some lines held longer than others before fading — the firmer soil retaining the impression, the softer patches releasing it almost immediately.
This time, Tomas wasn't trying to win.
He was watching.
Waiting.
"There."
Tomas stopped suddenly mid-motion.
Mateo halted as well, his hand still raised from the previous movement.
"What."
"You shifted too early. Changed your footing too," Tomas said, pointing slightly downward toward the impression Mateo's lead foot had left in the dirt. The print was angled differently from the one beside it — rotated a fraction outward, the weight distribution favoring the outer edge rather than the center. "Before I committed."
A brief silence passed.
He caught that. The adjustment was made during his preparatory phase, before his weight had committed to the strike's trajectory. The fact that he noticed means he was watching my feet rather than my hands — which means he has stopped reacting to the visible attack and started reading the structural preparation beneath it. That is a significant development.
Mateo lowered his stance slightly, letting his weight settle more evenly.
"...Yes."
Tomas nodded slowly. "As if you already knew what I was going to do."
Mateo didn't respond immediately.
Because I did. The preparatory weight shift in your right hip telegraphs the angle of your swing approximately three-tenths of a second before your arm begins to move. Your breathing pattern compresses slightly before a committed strike, which distinguishes genuine attacks from feints. And your left foot rotates inward by a fraction of a degree when you intend to follow up with a second strike, which tells me whether to expect one motion or two. I knew what you were going to do because your body announced it before your mind gave the order.
"You're predictable," he said instead.
Tomas frowned. "Am I really that predictable?"
Mateo tilted his head slightly.
"Everyone is."
"Maybe to you, you monster," Tomas said, a chuckle breaking through the frustration — the specific laugh of someone who was annoyed enough to be honest and amused enough to not mind.
"Then why do others not notice?" Tomas thought, rolling his eyes.
Before the silence stretched too far, a loud voice cut across the yard.
"HEY! If everyone is predictable, predict this!"
Neither Mateo nor Tomas turned right away.
"Something loud and unnecessary," Mateo replied.
There was a pause.
"...Okay, that's fair," Diego admitted, already halfway through stepping on — and then blaming — a rock that had not moved at all. His foot had found it with the unerring accuracy of someone who could locate the single obstacle in an otherwise clear path and collide with it as though drawn by gravitational force.
Tomas glanced over, watching Diego argue with the ground as though the ground had initiated the conflict. Diego's case was thorough, impassioned, and entirely one-sided. He gestured at the rock. He pointed at his foot. He looked around for witnesses to corroborate the rock's aggressive behavior. The rock offered no defense and showed no remorse.
The rock, unsurprisingly, did not respond.
"...He doesn't bother you?" Tomas asked.
Mateo finally looked over.
Diego had now committed to his position entirely, explaining to the rock why it was responsible for everything that had just happened. His argument had expanded from the immediate injury to include a broader indictment of the rock's character, its placement, and its general attitude toward passersby. A woman walking past on the adjacent path slowed, stared, decided the situation was not her concern, and continued with the deliberate pace of someone pretending they had not seen anything.
"...No," Mateo said calmly. "...I just ignore him."
Tomas stared at Mateo, his expression carrying genuine shock — not at the statement itself, but at the absolute sincerity with which it was delivered.
"At least he doesn't overthink things," Mateo added.
Tomas exhaled quietly, something close to amusement flickering across his expression — the reluctant acknowledgment that Mateo had, in five words, described the precise quality that made Diego simultaneously infuriating and invaluable.
"...I see why he likes you."
Mateo blinked once.
What does that even mean? Diego likes everyone. He likes food. He likes rocks, despite the current dispute. He likes mornings that involve watching other people train while he eats. His approval is not selective enough to serve as meaningful data.
He chose not to investigate the comment further.
Training resumed, but something had shifted.
It was no longer a test, nor even structured practice. It had become exchange — a conversation conducted through motion rather than words, where each adjustment communicated an observation and each response confirmed or corrected it.
Tomas adjusted continuously now. His stance refined, his timing tightened, his awareness broadened. He was no longer reacting to what Mateo did — he was beginning to anticipate possibility instead of action. Not accurately. Not consistently. But the intent was there, and intent, when paired with Tomas's persistence, had a way of becoming capability faster than most people expected.
Mateo allowed more.
Not evenly. Not completely. But enough to give Tomas something real to work with — enough resistance to be meaningful, enough variation to be educational, enough of his actual capability to create a gap that Tomas could see and reach toward without it being so vast that reaching felt pointless.
He is improving faster than expected. The isolation approach is working — by breaking each movement into components, he is identifying weaknesses that were invisible when the movements were performed as a continuous sequence. His body is learning the corrections individually and then integrating them into the whole. That is not intuition. That is method. And method, unlike talent, does not plateau.
"Again," Tomas said.
"You've said that ten times."
"Then we'll make it eleven."
"You're persistent," Mateo replied.
"You say that like it's a flaw."
"It depends on the outcome."
Another exchange. Then another. The dust in the yard had been disturbed so many times that it hung in the air in a fine haze, catching the morning light that had finally committed to arriving and turning the space between them into something that looked almost golden.
At one point, Tomas overextended — only slightly, just a fraction beyond where his balance should have held. The shift was small, but once it occurred, it carried through the rest of his motion like a crack propagating through glass. His shoulder moved past the line of his hip. His weight committed forward beyond the point of clean recovery. His saber's arc widened by centimeters that he could not reclaim.
Mateo reacted.
This time, his movement changed.
His hand cut across Tomas's motion with sharper precision than before — faster, but not only in speed. More certain. The quality of the movement shifted from responsive to inevitable, as though the path his hand traveled had been decided before Tomas's overextension occurred and was simply waiting for the moment to execute.
For an instant —
His awareness narrowed.
Not everything slowed. Just his perception. The world continued at its normal pace, but the information it contained arrived with a clarity that felt different from his usual observation. Heightened. Refined. As though a filter had been removed and the raw data beneath it was, briefly, accessible.
The shift in Tomas's weight — precisely 1.3 degrees past his balance point. The angle of the blade — descending at an arc that would reach Mateo's left shoulder in approximately four-tenths of a second. The space between movement and outcome felt clearer. More defined. Not predicted — perceived. As though the future existed not as possibility but as geometry, and geometry could be read the way text could be read, if you knew the language.
Again.
The pendant beneath his clothing grew faintly warm.
Options unfolded — not as thoughts but as visible paths, each one branching from the current moment into a different consequence. Then collapsed — all but one, the one that was most efficient, most clean, most aligned with the principles Mateo had spent years refining.
He moved.
The strike deflected cleanly, breaking Tomas's balance just enough to force him back a step. The motion required almost no force — only precision, only timing, only the exact placement of contact at the exact point where Tomas's structure was weakest. The saber passed harmlessly to the side. Tomas staggered — not from impact, but from the sudden absence of the resistance he had expected to meet.
Mateo stopped immediately.
Silence settled over the yard. The dust that had been hanging in the air seemed to pause with it, suspended in the golden light as though waiting for permission to continue falling.
"That wasn't the same," Tomas said quietly.
Mateo looked down at his own hand. It looked the same as it always had. The fingers, the palm, the calluses that training had built along the ridge beneath his knuckles. Nothing visible had changed.
But something had moved through that hand — something that did not belong to a boy his age, in a yard this small, at a level this modest. Something that had surfaced from beneath his suppression without his permission and had operated with a certainty that his conscious mind had not authorized.
No... it wasn't.
"...Forget it," he said.
But Tomas didn't look convinced.
He stood where the deflection had pushed him, saber held loosely at his side, his breathing slightly elevated, his gaze fixed on Mateo with the quiet intensity of someone who had just felt something that did not fit any category he possessed. He would not forget it. He would add it to the growing collection of observations that did not yet form a picture but that were accumulating mass.
Mateo saw this happening.
He allowed it.
Because some things could not be prevented. Only managed.
They stepped aside briefly.
Recovering.
Diego had progressed significantly during this time.
Not in training.
In eating.
He had acquired — through means that Mateo chose not to investigate — a collection of food items that he had arranged on a flat stone beside the yard with the careful attention of someone curating an exhibition. A rice wrap. Something fried that smelled better than it looked. A small clay cup of what appeared to be broth, steaming faintly in the morning air.
"Where did you even get that?" Mateo asked, eyeing the food in Diego's hands.
"I brought it," Diego said proudly.
"You brought food... to watch us train?"
"Obviously."
Mateo stared at him.
There is a strange efficiency to this approach that I may have underestimated. He has optimized his morning to include entertainment, nutrition, and social interaction without expending any physical effort whatsoever. From a systems perspective, this is arguably the most rational use of time anyone in this yard has demonstrated today.
"You didn't bring enough."
Diego froze mid-bite.
"...For who?"
Mateo gestured slightly — to Tomas, who was wiping sweat from his forehead with his sleeve. To Inés, who had arrived quietly at some point during the training and stood at the yard's edge with her arms folded, observing with the specific stillness of someone who had been there longer than anyone realized. And to himself.
Diego looked down at the small amount of food.
Then back up.
"...Wow."
"Yes."
"You're all terrible people."
That night, the village quieted the way it always did — not completely, but enough that sound traveled farther than intended. Some noises lingered longer than they should have, while others faded too quickly, leaving gaps where sound felt incomplete. The transition from day to night was not a clean line in this village — it was a gradual dimming, a slow compression of activity into smaller and smaller spaces until only the most persistent sounds remained.
Somewhere in the distance, someone was still training — the rhythmic thud of a strike against a practice post, steady and unhurried, the sound of someone who trained not because they were told to but because stopping felt wrong.
Elsewhere, a quiet argument continued between two voices that Mateo could not identify — the words indistinct, the tone carrying the specific texture of a disagreement that had been ongoing long enough to lose its original subject and had become an argument about the argument itself.
The forest remained present. Watching. Breathing. Its canopy stretched over the village in a dark lattice that turned the sky into fragments, and the sounds it produced — the creak of wood, the rustle of leaves, the occasional call of something that hunted after dark — wove through the village's own sounds like a second conversation happening beneath the first.
Mateo lay still, allowing his body to rest. The pendant sat against his chest, warm in the way it had been warm for weeks now — a constant, low-grade presence that he had stopped noticing the way one stopped noticing a heartbeat. It was simply there. Part of the background.
Sleep came quickly.
But what followed —
Was not sleep.
Color replaced darkness.
Not gradually. Not with the slow fade of a dream assembling itself from fragments of memory and sensation. Immediately. As though someone had thrown open a door into a room that had been lit before he arrived.
Bright, unnatural bursts of light expanded outward, forming something that felt halfway between controlled creation and excessive presentation. The colors didn't move like light — they expanded in structured layers, folding slightly as they spread, each one arriving with a precision that suggested design rather than spontaneity. Purples bled into golds that sharpened into whites, and the whites fractured into prismatic edges that held for exactly long enough to be noticed before dissolving into the next layer.
It was, Mateo realized, a performance.
Someone — or something — was showing off.
The environment shifted. Space twisted — not violently, but with intention. The darkness that had surrounded him reorganized itself into geometry, flat planes assembling beneath his feet and curved surfaces arching overhead. Constructed. Deliberate. Like a stage being built in real time by someone who wanted the audience to watch the construction and be impressed by it.
Mateo blinked.
"...What is this."
A platform formed beneath his feet, smooth and faintly glowing. It didn't rise — it simply existed where nothing had been a moment before, as though the concept of "floor" had been introduced to this space and the space had reluctantly agreed to comply.
Then —
Something dropped from above.
With a soft pop — the specific, slightly cartoonish sound of something arriving in a space that had not been designed to accommodate arrivals — a small figure landed in front of him.
It stood upright.
Ears too large for its head, pointed and slightly asymmetrical in a way that suggested they had grown independently of each other and had never quite agreed on a final shape. Its body was compact, covered in short fur that might have been grey or might have been silver depending on the light, which was changing frequently enough that the distinction was impossible to settle. Its eyes were disproportionately large, round, and carried the specific luminance of something that was fully aware of its own importance and expected everyone else to be equally aware.
Its presence was too confident for its size.
A microphone was held in one paw.
"...Welcome!" it announced, its voice carrying across the constructed space with the practiced projection of someone who had rehearsed this moment extensively. It spread its free paw outward in a gesture of theatrical generosity, as though presenting the surrounding void as a gift.
Then paused.
Looked around at the incomplete, flickering, still-assembling space.
Then directly at Mateo.
"...Wait."
Mateo stared back.
What is that. It is small. It is holding a microphone. It arrived with a sound effect. It appears to have prepared a speech. None of these observations assemble into anything coherent.
The creature narrowed its eyes.
"...You're not supposed to be here yet."
"I could say the same," Mateo replied.
A pause. The creature's expression shifted — not quite offended, not quite amused, but somewhere in the territory between the two where small creatures with large ears and excessive confidence tended to reside.
"...This is a preliminary broadcast," the creature said stiffly, drawing itself up to its full height, which changed nothing about the power dynamic but seemed to make it feel better. "Please disregard."
Mateo glanced at the microphone.
"...You're holding a device specifically designed to be noticed."
"...Presentation matters."
"...Clearly."
The creature tapped the microphone against its paw. Once. Twice.
"...Testing. Testing."
Nothing echoed. The sound left the microphone and traveled approximately six inches before being absorbed by the surrounding space as though the space had decided it was not yet ready to support acoustics.
The creature frowned.
"...We'll fix that."
"Is the 'we' in that sentence referring to you and someone else," Mateo asked, "or you and the microphone?"
"...That is not your concern."
"It became my concern when I arrived in a space I did not choose to enter, occupied by a creature I did not expect to meet, who is holding equipment that does not function."
The creature stared at him for a long moment.
Then it sighed — a sound that was far too deep and far too weary for something its size, as though the weight of the exhale had been borrowed from a much larger being.
"...You're going to be a problem later, aren't you?"
Mateo considered the question. It was, based on available evidence, a reasonable prediction.
"That seems evident."
The creature muttered to itself, turning partially away and speaking at a volume that was clearly intended to be overheard.
"...Entry filters unstable... access controls broken... unauthorized consciousness detected in preliminary staging area..." It glanced back at Mateo. "...This is what happens when you rush the calibration sequence."
Mateo folded his arms. "Who are you?"
The creature straightened. Drew itself up again. Adjusted the microphone in its paw with the deliberate care of someone arranging a prop for maximum visual impact.
"I am the Realm Spirit."
"...Of what."
"...Classified."
"...Of course it is."
The creature stared at him.
"...You're very calm about this."
"I've seen worse."
That was not entirely accurate. Mateo had, in fact, been the worse that other beings had seen. But the statement served its purpose — it communicated that whatever the creature expected its appearance to provoke, it would not find that reaction here.
A pause.
Then the creature sighed again.
"...You're going to be a problem later, aren't you."
"You already asked that."
"I was hoping the answer had changed."
"It has not."
The world flickered slightly — not collapsing, just failing to fully settle. The constructed geometry around them shimmered at its edges, the colors destabilizing briefly before reestablishing themselves with slightly less conviction than before.
"...This isn't fully open yet anyway," the creature said, waving a paw in a gesture that was meant to be dismissive but that carried the subtle urgency of someone who knew the infrastructure beneath them was less stable than it appeared. "Come back later."
"I didn't ask to be here."
"Too bad."
Everything collapsed instantly.
Not dramatically. Not with the violent implosion of a space being destroyed. It simply stopped — the colors, the geometry, the platform, the creature, all of it ceasing to exist in the same instant, as though someone had closed a book and the characters within it had vanished with the page.
Mateo's eyes opened.
The night returned. Still. Quiet. The ceiling of his room above him — wooden beams, uneven, familiar. The sounds of the village at rest filtering through the walls in the muted tones that distance and timber produced.
He remained unmoving, staring upward.
That was not a dream. Dreams are constructed from memory and processed through the subconscious mind's interpretive framework. That space was not interpreted — it was constructed. Externally. By something that was not me. The creature — the Realm Spirit — existed independently of my awareness and was surprised by my presence, which means the space was not generated by my mind but accessed by it. The pendant was the bridge. It has been growing warmer, more active, more responsive. Tonight, its activity crossed a threshold, and the result was a connection to a space that is not yet complete.
The pendant rested in his hand.
Warm.
He turned it slowly, feeling the pulse that had become so familiar over the past weeks — but that felt, tonight, slightly different. Not stronger. Not faster. More directed. As though the pendant's energy, which had previously radiated outward in all directions, was now orienting itself toward something specific.
Fragments. Forming something.
He exhaled quietly and closed his eyes again.
I will deal with it later. The space is incomplete. The creature confirmed as much. The entry was accidental — a consequence of the pendant's increasing activity rather than a deliberate access. There is nothing to act on yet.
A small pause followed.
Then —
That thing was ridiculous. It had a microphone. It announced itself like a performer at a festival. It attempted to conduct a sound check in a space that could not support sound. And it sighed at me twice, as though my existence was a personal inconvenience it had not budgeted for.
A faint smile appeared on Mateo's face.
I have questions.
It was not willing to answer them.
That is fine.
I am patient.
Then — stillness.
Not empty.
Not silent.
Waiting.
