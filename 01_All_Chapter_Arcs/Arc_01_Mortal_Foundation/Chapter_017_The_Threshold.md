Chapter 17 — The Threshold  

The forest did not greet him. It simply continued.  
That, more than anything, was what he found agreeable about it.  
By the second hour the ground had stopped defeating him. Not because it had become predictable — it had not, and he was beginning to suspect it never would in  
the way the training yard had — but because he had stopped trying to predict it in advance and started reading it at the moment of contact instead. The correction  
arrived a fraction later that way and cost slightly more, and it worked, which was the only criterion that mattered.  
Where roots twisted beneath the surface in ridges that would have caught a less attentive foot, his ankle corrected before the imbalance could form, the adjustment  
beginning at the hip and traveling down. Where the soil softened without warning, giving way as though saturated from below rather than above, his toes spread and  
redistributed the pressure through the ball of the foot rather than sinking into it.  
The weighted bands stayed in his pack. He had considered wearing them and decided against it, since the terrain provided its own resistance and layering artificial  
unpredictability on top of the genuine article would have taught him nothing.  
Also, if something decided to chase him, he preferred not to be carrying an extra hundred kilograms.  
That seemed prudent.  
There were traces of passage that did not belong to the ecosystem. Not obvious, and not recent, but present. Bark broken at an angle that wind does not produce —  
the clean compressed fracture of something pressed against with controlled force. Shallow depressions where weight had settled and lifted without hesitation, left by  
someone who stopped to look rather than to rest. A branch cut through at chest height instead of snapped, the exposed wood darkened but not fully oxidized.  
Days, not weeks. One individual, traveling light. The cuts are trained but not disciplined — someone taught him and nobody has corrected him since. He was passing  
through rather than establishing anything. Not the pair at the boundary. A fifth.  
Mateo did not slow, and did not turn his head toward any of it.  
He noticed all of it.  

What held his attention was not the human sign. It was the ground.  
He had felt it at the boundary, that first sensation of energy moving unevenly beneath the surface, registering through his feet before his mind had a category ready.  
Deeper in, it had texture.  
Some stretches felt supported. The earth there carried a firmness that had nothing to do with soil composition, as though something below were reinforcing it. His  
steps landed lighter. His breathing settled without being asked to. The ambient energy moved through those places smoothly, in patterns his awareness could follow  
without effort.  
Other stretches were less organized. The ground gave more than expected. The air carried a faint turbulence that made his breathing adjust before he had decided to  
adjust it, and his body responded the way it responded to uneven terrain, with automatic correction and raised attention and the understanding that the rules had  
shifted slightly and he had not been told how.  
He could not see the cause. He could feel it, and the feeling sharpened with every hour.  
Not random. Whatever is beneath this runs in channels, and the channels have character. Some of them support what stands above them. Some of them do not.  
He had no framework for it yet. No terminology, no system, no map connecting the sensation to any larger structure. He registered it, noted the characteristics, and  
filed it with everything else that had been accumulating since he first stood at the boundary and felt something pull at his attention.  
The forest was not uniform, and the non-uniformity was not accidental.  
That was enough to be going on with.  

By midday he had stopped treating the forest as a challenge and started treating it as an environment.  
The distinction mattered. A challenge demanded tension. An environment demanded attention. He had a great deal of the second and preferred to spend the first  
sparingly.  
He found a break in the canopy where a tree had come down sometime in the past year, its trunk now hosting moss and several species of fungus conducting what  
appeared to be an unresolved territorial dispute across its surface. Light fell through the gap in a warm column exactly wide enough for a person to lie down in,  
provided that person was not large and was willing to use a mound of roots as a pillow.  
He was not large. He had used worse pillows.  
He lay back with one arm behind his head and looked up. The sky broke into pieces through the branches, blue fragments set into green and brown, with the  
occasional bright flash that might have been a bird or a leaf catching light at the correct angle. The wind moved without purpose. Somewhere close by, fallen fruit was  
going sweetly bad.  
Something small came through the undergrowth to his left, stopped, regarded him with whatever passes for suspicion in a creature that size, and continued on, having  
concluded he was not worth the trouble.  
Mateo closed his eyes.  
"...This is better."  
He did not sleep. His breathing slowed and his pulse evened and the surface layer of his mind went quiet, the part that tracked conversations and social dynamics and  
maintained the constant unglamorous work of appearing ordinary.  
His awareness went outward instead.  
He could feel movement through the ground, minor shifts in pressure where creatures passed, the faint vibration of something burrowing several meters to his right,  
the barely perceptible rhythm of root systems taking up warmth. He could feel air displaced by something larger moving between trees at a distance, not near enough  
to matter, present enough to enter the map he was building without deciding to build it.  
The forest, experienced with the eyes closed, was extraordinarily alive. Not only in the obvious way. Alive in its structure. The energy in this gap pooled rather than  
passed through, gathering and circulating in slow cycles that resembled breathing, as though the break in the canopy had created a pocket of concentration as well as  
a pocket of light.  
Then, beneath all of it, something else.  
A ripple in the pattern that did not come from the forest. Brief, and distant, and arriving from a direction his awareness could not fix, like the echo of a sound made  

somewhere far away and carried through materials that had distorted it past recognition.  
Gone before it finished forming.  
Origin unknown. Not immediate. Not relevant to this position.  
He filed it and let it go, and he was aware, filing it, that he had no way to know whether that judgment was correct.  

Then the forest tested his understanding of relaxation.  
The air changed. The temperature held and the wind did not shift and the light kept falling through the gap in its warm column, but something in the texture of the  
space tightened.  
His eyes opened slightly. Not fully. Enough to see through his lashes without the motion carrying.  
Footfalls. Heavy, controlled, several. Coming from the northwest through the undergrowth with a coordination that indicated pack movement rather than individual  
hunting, spaced too evenly to be casual and varied too much in weight to be one large animal.  
Then they came out of the deeper shadow.  
Seven wolves. Their fur was the deep charcoal of creatures adapted to places where blending with shadow served better than blending with foliage, and their eyes  
caught the filtered light and held it, throwing it back in amber and pale gold.  
The alpha came first.  
Its fur carried a faint shimmer under the broken light, not metallic but reflective in a way that suggested structure underneath softness. It was larger than the others by  
a wide margin, and the size was the least interesting thing about it. Every movement was measured. It placed its paws with an awareness of what lay beneath the  
ground as well as on it — a sensitivity to terrain that Mateo recognized immediately, because he had spent four years building the same thing on purpose.  
Not simply large. Cultivated.  
Not the way people cultivated, through meditation and meridian refinement and the deliberate handling of spiritual energy. Through exposure. Decades of living  
inside an environment saturated with ambient energy, absorbing it passively through every breath and every meal until it had become as much a part of the animal as  
blood.  
Early stage. The core has not finished forming. The concentration through the musculature is consistent with long passive absorption rather than anything directed.  
Behind it the younger wolves held close, less controlled and already dangerous, watching him with the flat unblinking attention of predators deciding whether  
something qualified.  
The alpha stopped. Its eyes settled on him.  
Mateo, still lying on the root mound with one arm behind his head, did not move.  
"...You're in the way," he said.  
The silence that followed had a particular quality to it. It was the silence of a predator discovering that the thing on the ground had said something, and needing a  
moment.  
The wolf growled, low enough that the sound arrived through the ground as much as through the air, and its haunches compressed as its weight shifted back.  
Mateo sighed.  
"...I am not food. I am not a threat. I am also comfortable, and I would prefer to remain that way."  
The wolf blinked.  
That was new. In its experience, which was considerable, given that it had been the dominant thing in this stretch of forest for longer than most of the surrounding  
trees had been standing, creatures found lying on the ground did one of three things. They ran, they froze, or they died.  
None of them expressed a preference.  
Mateo tilted his head and studied it with equal interest, extending his awareness toward it, not as a probe but as an observation. Underneath the growl and the  
posture and the display of dominance that was at least half habit, he could feel confusion wrapped around genuine uncertainty.  
The animal did not understand what it was looking at, and it was intelligent enough to find that unpleasant.  
"...You should leave," he added. "There are better uses of your time."  
The pack did not move. One of the younger wolves came forward a step, hackles up, lips pulled back from teeth that were impressive the way all sharp things are  
impressive when aimed at a person.  
The alpha stopped it with a single sound, not a bark and not a growl but something between them that carried absolute authority, and the young wolf retreated so  
completely that its boldness might never have existed.  
The alpha's attention stayed where it was. The question was not spoken and never could have been, but it arrived anyway, through posture and the intensity of the eye  
contact and the specific stillness an intelligent animal adopts when something has failed to fit anything it knows.  
Why are you not afraid.  
"...Because I would prefer not to be inconvenienced," Mateo said.  
He said it the way a person explains a scheduling conflict. Apologetic in tone. Immovable in substance.  
The alpha's ears twitched, and the confusion in it began hardening into something more structured, which was worse.  
Mateo exhaled slowly. This was taking too long.  
There were two options. The first was to release enough of his suppressed presence to override curiosity with instinct, which would scatter the pack immediately and  
would also announce him to anything within several kilometers capable of registering spiritual pressure, which was the entire category of things he had come here to  
avoid meeting on their terms.  
The second option was more entertaining.  
He sat halfway up. Rolled one shoulder, loose, working out a stiffness.  
Then flinched.  
The motion was small and entirely deliberate, controlled down to the specific muscles involved. His eyes widened a fraction — not with fear, which would have been  
wrong, but with the sharp attention of someone whose gaze had just been caught by something behind the wolves that the wolves had not yet noticed. He fixed on a  
point in the deeper forest with an intensity he had not shown at any moment of the conversation.  
"...Ah."  
One syllable, delivered exactly as a person delivers it upon realizing something unfortunate.  
The pack reacted instantly, and not to him. Every head turned. Every ear rotated. The alpha went from confrontation to assessment in under a second, its whole  
sensory apparatus swinging toward the threat his performance had proposed.  
He let the relaxation drain out of his posture, replacing it with a tension controlled enough to look involuntary.  
"...You might want to move," he said, quieter now, as though volume itself carried risk.  
The alpha looked back at him. Then at the forest behind it. Then at him again. Its nostrils flared, searching the air.  
It found nothing, because there was nothing.  
But absence of evidence was not evidence of absence, not for something that had survived this long by trusting instinct over confirmation, and the calculation had  
already been made: an unknown human behaving incorrectly, plus an unidentified possible threat, equaled too many variables.  

"...It's coming this way," Mateo said.  
The pack went. Not in disorder, and not in fear — an immediate coordinated withdrawal on a single sharp command, the whole group pouring into the undergrowth  
with a speed and silence that would have impressed him if he had not been the one who caused it.  
Within seconds the clearing held nothing to suggest seven wolves had ever occupied it.  
Mateo lay back against the root mound.  
He allowed himself a moment of satisfaction, not because the wolves had been dangerous — they had not been, and both he and the alpha had understood that,  
which was why the standoff lasted as long as it did — but because it had been clean. No energy spent. Nothing revealed. Nothing given to anything that might have  
been watching.  
Diego would be insufferable about this.  
Diego would also have been behind a tree throughout, but he would have been insufferable from behind the tree.  

He did not stay long after that.  
The gap had served its purpose, and the pendant was doing something he wanted to think about somewhere other than a clearing seven predators had just left.  
The pull had been there for days, a fractional warmth on one side that never varied. It had strengthened at the boundary. Here it had become something he could take  
a bearing from. Not a tug and not a compulsion — simply the fact that if he faced one direction the warmth against his chest was measurably greater than if he faced  
any other, and the direction had not changed once since he entered the trees.  
It is not responding to the forest. It has been pointing at the same thing the entire time, and only now am I close enough to resolve it.  
He filed that and did not act on it. Not yet.  
Then the forest above him changed first.  
A shadow crossed the ground, too defined to be cloud and too fast to be weather and far too large to belong to any bird he had encountered.  
He looked up.  
The hawk's wingspan crossed the visible sky, sixty meters at least from tip to tip, which should not have been possible for anything bound by the laws that governed  
ordinary biology. Its flight made no sound. The only evidence of its passage arrived seconds late, a pressure wave that bent the grass and stirred loose leaves in a  
direction that briefly disagreed with the wind. Its feathers carried a faint sheen along their edges, catching light at angles that made no sense, splitting it into brief  
prismatic flashes that vanished as the geometry changed.  
It was not hunting. Its line was too straight and too steady for that. It moved the way a patrol moves across territory, with ownership, with the settled certainty that  
everything underneath it fell within its jurisdiction.  
Mateo went still.  
The concentration is orders of magnitude past the wolves. Older. Deeper. Fully integrated rather than accumulated. That is not something that lives in this forest. That is  
something the forest arranges itself around.  
He suppressed immediately, dropping his presence to its floor, tightening everything down until what remained would register to anything scanning from above as a  
boy of fourteen with modest cultivation sitting in a clearing.  
Nothing worth investigating. Nothing worth remembering.  
The shadow slid across the ground and over him and past, and then the hawk was gone beyond the canopy line to the east, and the sky went back to being fragments  
of blue in green.  
Mateo remained where he was for some time after it had gone.  
Not from fear. He examined for fear and did not find any, which was consistent with every previous examination and had stopped being informative years ago. What  
he found instead required more attention.  
He had just hidden from something.  
Not concealed his abilities from people whose reactions he preferred to manage. Not suppressed to avoid inconvenient questions. Hidden, in the plain sense, from  
something that outranked him and could have done as it liked, and the calculation had been so fast and so total that his body had executed it before he finished  
forming the thought.  
In the void there was nothing above me. There was nothing above me because there was no above.  
I removed that on purpose. I remember removing it. I designed this.  
He turned it over the way he turned over everything and found, again, the thing he had found on the first night in a body and had not resolved since. The sensation  
would not sit in any category. It was not distress. It was something closer to vertigo, and something adjacent to it that he suspected, without being able to confirm,  
might be excitement.  
Against his chest, the pendant had gone quiet.  
Not cold. Not dormant. Its pulse had simply thinned to almost nothing while the shadow was overhead, and it stayed thin for several seconds afterward, and only  
when the hawk was well beyond the eastern treeline did the rhythm come back to what it had been.  
He put two fingers over it.  
I did not do that.  
The pull resumed, steady, unchanged, pointing where it had been pointing since the boundary.  
Mateo stood.  
"...Alright."  
A pause.  
"...Less casual from here."  
He went east, which was not the direction the pendant wanted, and made note of the fact that he had chosen to see what the hawk was patrolling before he went to  
see what had been calling him.  
He would examine the reason for that later.
