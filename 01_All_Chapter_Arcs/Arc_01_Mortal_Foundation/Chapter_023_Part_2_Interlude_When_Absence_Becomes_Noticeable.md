# Chapter 23 - Interlude — When Absence Becomes Noticeable (Part 2)

Chapter 23 —Interlude — When Absence Becomes Noticeable Part 2  

Tomas stood before them, delivering the report with the measured calm of someone who understood that emotional delivery would undermine factual content.  
"...He left without telling anyone," Elder Hiro said. The words arrived with the precise shade of irritation that translated to I told you this would happen and that  
was further complicated by the undercurrent of I told you this would happen and I am furious that being right does not fix it.  
"That is not surprising," Elder Kurosawa replied, his tone carrying the flat acknowledgment of someone confirming a prediction rather than reacting to news.  
"...He is fourteen," Elder Park added. The observation was simple. Its implications were not. Fourteen was young enough to be reckless and old enough to be  
deliberate, and the distinction between those two things — when applied to Mateo — was the question that the entire meeting would eventually need to  
answer.  
"...He is Mateo," Elder Shen said from the window, without turning.  
Two words. Delivered without emphasis, without volume, without any of the rhetorical tools that the other elders employed to give their statements weight. The  
weight was inherent. The name itself had become, within this room, a category — a shorthand for a set of behaviors and capabilities that the council had spent  
years trying to classify and that resisted classification the way water resisted being held in an open hand.  
That ended it.  
Not the concern. But the surprise. No one in the room was surprised. Several of them had been expecting this — Elder Shen through observation, Elder Marcos  
through strategic assessment, Elder Kurosawa through the logical extension of the patterns he had been tracking, Elder Aurelio through the quiet  
understanding of someone who recognized patterns that others could not see because they required a perspective that only age and experience could provide.  
Elder Doña Reyes had been expecting it too, though her expectation was rooted in something different from the others — not observation or strategy, but  
history. She had seen gifted young cultivators reach the limit of what a village could teach before. She had watched them pace the boundary between  
settlement and wilderness the way Mateo had been pacing it for months. And she had learned, through decades of watching, that the pacing always ended the  
same way. They left. The only variable was when.  
Elder Aurelio sat quietly for a moment, his broad frame still, his scarred hands resting on the arms of his chair. The room waited — not because anyone  
instructed it to, but because Aurelio's silences carried the specific gravity of something that would eventually produce words, and those words would matter  
more than whatever filled the space before them.  
"...He went into the forest," Tomas said.  
"I expected it," the old man replied. His voice carried no alarm. No urgency. The calm of someone who had watched something approach for a long time and  
was not startled by its arrival. If anything, the faintest trace of satisfaction existed beneath the composure — the kind that surfaced when a prediction was  
confirmed and the prediction had been, on balance, a positive one.  
Silence.  
"...Should we retrieve him?" Elder Vargas asked. The question was genuine — not rhetorical, not performative, but the honest inquiry of someone who needed  
to know whether action was required because his instinct was to act and he needed permission to override it.  
Elder Aurelio's expression shifted — the faintest suggestion of amusement crossing features that were otherwise as readable as stone. The amusement was not  
at Vargas's expense. It was at the situation's — the specific humor of being asked whether a force of nature should be redirected by people who had already  
demonstrated that redirecting it was beyond their capability.  
"If he can be retrieved easily, he has not gone far enough to matter."  
"...And if he has?" Elder Hiro pressed. His tone carried the specific frustration of someone who recognized that the answer would not satisfy him and was asking  
anyway because the alternative — not asking — was worse.  
A faint smile. The kind that existed not on the mouth but in the eyes — the specific warmth of someone who understood something about the subject of  
discussion that the others did not and that he was not yet willing to share. The smile carried decades of context that the room could feel but could not read —  
the accumulated knowledge of a man who had watched his great-grandson grow from an infant who stared at ceilings with too much comprehension to a boy  
who walked into elder meetings and dismantled their certainty to a young man who had now walked into a forest and would, Elder Aurelio was quietly certain,  
walk back out carrying something that would change the village's understanding of what was possible.  
"...Then he is already solving it."  
The answer settled through the group. Not reassurance. Recognition. The acknowledgment that whatever Mateo was doing in the forest, it was not random and  
it was not reckless — or if it was reckless, it was the specific kind of recklessness that produced results, which was a category the elders had been forced to  
create specifically because of Mateo and that they still did not entirely know how to manage.  
Elder Tomás Aguilar's fingers stilled on the table. The anxiety that had been expressing itself through motion settled — not because the worry had diminished,  
but because Aurelio's answer had given it a container. The boy was not lost. He was not in danger beyond his capacity. He was doing what he was supposed to  
be doing, even if "supposed to" was defined by a framework that only Aurelio and possibly Shen could see.  
Elder Doña Reyes nodded once — a small, contained motion that communicated agreement without requiring elaboration. She had reached the same  
conclusion through different means, and the convergence of her assessment with Aurelio's gave the room the structural stability of a bridge supported from  
both sides.  
Elder Hiro's jaw remained tight. His arms remained folded. His vindication remained unsatisfying. But his posture had shifted — fractionally, almost  
imperceptibly — from combative to watchful. He was not satisfied. But he was willing to wait.  
For now.  
Diego, who had been permitted to remain at the back of the room because no one had explicitly told him to leave and because Diego interpreted the absence  
of prohibition as implicit invitation, leaned slightly toward Tomas.  
"...That is both reassuring and deeply concerning," he whispered.  
"...Yes," Tomas whispered back.  
Elder Hiro's gaze snapped toward them with the speed and precision of someone whose hearing had been refined through decades of cultivation and whose  
tolerance for sidebar conversations during council meetings had been refined through decades of Elder Hiro.  
They stopped whispering.  

Outside the hall, the mission board had already become a problem.  
Not because of its content — Elder Liang's precise notices communicated the village's current needs with characteristic clarity. The tasks were organized by  
urgency, categorized by difficulty, and annotated with resource requirements. It was, by any objective measure, a well-designed system.  
Diego stood in front of it, arms crossed, head tilted at an angle that suggested he was examining a piece of art that had failed to meet his expectations.  
"...This system is flawed."  
Tomas, who had followed him outside after the elder meeting concluded, stood nearby with the resigned posture of someone who recognized the opening  
notes of a Diego performance and understood that intervention was futile.  
"...You've been here for five minutes."  
"That's enough time."  
"...It's a mission board. It lists tasks. People take them."  
"And yet," Diego said, pointing at a specific notice, "'moderate difficulty.' What does that mean? Moderate for who? For that guy?" He pointed randomly at a  
trainee crossing the yard — a boy approximately their age who was currently struggling to carry a bucket of water without spilling it.  
"...That's how difficulty descriptions work," Tomas said.  
"No. That's how confusing systems are created. 'Moderate' is relative. Without a baseline, it communicates nothing. I could post a mission that says 'moderate  
difficulty: fight a bear' and someone who has never seen a bear would take it because 'moderate' sounds manageable."  
Tomas opened his mouth.  
Closed it.  
Because the logic was, irritatingly, not entirely wrong.  
Diego reached for one of the wooden markers that indicated task status — a flat piece of wood that could be flipped between "available" and "claimed."  
He flipped it.  
Nothing happened.  
"...Unfortunate," Diego said.  
"...Diego, do not reorganize the mission board."  
"I am improving it."  
"You will get banned."  
"...That would be unjust."  
A pause.  
"...I should test the limits first."  
"...Don't."  
Too late.  
Diego was already rearranging the task notices — pulling them from their positions and reordering them according to a system that existed entirely in his head  
and that he was constructing in real time. High-reward tasks migrated to the top. Low-effort tasks clustered in the middle. Tasks that Diego personally found  
uninteresting were relocated to a section he had mentally designated as "boring but necessary" and that he placed at the bottom with the specific energy of  
someone filing paperwork they did not intend to complete.  
"You stacked high-reward missions at the top," Tomas observed.  
"...Yes."  
"That's not how difficulty scaling works."  
"...It could be."  
Tomas rubbed his face with both hands — the gesture of someone whose patience was being tested by a force it had not been designed to withstand.  
"...Your family makes more sense now."  
"...That's rude."  
Diego paused. His hands, which had been mid-rearrangement, stilled. The perpetual motion that characterized his every interaction slowed, and what replaced  
it was something quieter — a stillness that looked unfamiliar on him, the way formal clothing looked unfamiliar on someone who lived in training gear.  
"My family does make sense," he said. His voice had shifted — still light, still carrying the cadence that made everything Diego said sound like it might be a  
joke, but with something beneath it that was not joking at all. "Just not in a way most people understand."  
That was new.  
Tomas looked at him.  
Diego shrugged, but the shrug was smaller than his usual ones — less performance, more punctuation.  
"They don't fight. They don't mine. They don't run shops. They don't cultivate in the way this village defines cultivation — no sparring records, no assessment  
rankings, no public demonstrations of progress."  
"...Then what do they do?"  
Diego smiled slightly. The smile was different from his usual grin — less wide, more specific, carrying the particular warmth of someone sharing something they  
valued rather than something they found amusing.  
"...We find things."  
He said it simply. Without elaboration. Without the verbal excess that usually accompanied his statements. Just three words, delivered with the quiet confidence  
of someone defining a family tradition rather than describing a hobby.  
"Lost items. Broken tools. Rare herbs. Things people swear are gone — that they've searched for, given up on, accepted the loss of."  
A pause.  
"...Sometimes things they didn't know they lost."  
Tomas frowned. "...That sounds like nonsense."  
"...It isn't."  
"...That sounds illegal."  
"...It sounds efficient."  
"...You steal things."  
"I prefer 'recovery specialist.'"  
Tomas sighed — the long, slow exhale of someone who had just received an explanation that answered several questions he had not known he was asking and  
that raised several more he was not sure he wanted answered.  
"...Of course you do."  

Diego grinned — the full grin this time, the one that carried the specific energy of someone who had shared something real and was now returning to the  
comfortable territory of being Diego.  
Then the grin faded slightly. His gaze shifted — away from the mission board, away from Tomas, toward the forest that pressed against the village's eastern  
edge. The treeline was visible from here, dark and layered, holding its silence with the patient indifference that the forest maintained regardless of what  
happened within the village beside it.  
"...That Mateo kid you spar with..." Diego said.  
Tomas looked at him.  
"...Yeah?"  
"...He's the type to wander into something stupid, right?"  
A pause. Tomas considered the question — not its content, which was obvious, but its implication. Diego was not asking about Mateo's tendencies. He was  
asking about Mateo's situation.  
"...Yes."  
"...And dangerous."  
"...Yes."  
"...And valuable."  
A longer pause. The word "valuable" carried weight that the previous answers had not. Stupid was expected. Dangerous was accepted. Valuable suggested that  
whatever Mateo found in the forest would matter beyond the finding — that the discovery itself would have consequences that extended past the discoverer.  
"...Yes."  
Diego nodded. The nod was small, decisive — the acknowledgment of someone who had completed an assessment and was now storing the conclusion.  
"...Good to know."  
He folded his arms and looked at the forest.  
"...And we're just standing here."  
"...Yes."  
"...That feels wrong."  
Tomas didn't answer.  
Because he agreed.  
Far below the forest, deeper than the roots reached, deeper than the veins that carried energy through the earth's hidden channels, deeper than the  
convergence points and the water-fed basins and the stable zones that Mateo had been learning to feel —  
Something shifted.  
Not awake.  
But no longer still.  
Across realms — something responded.  
Not to the shift beneath the forest. Not to anything that had happened in the Mortal World specifically. The response was larger than that — older, broader,  
connected to a system that extended beyond the boundaries of any single world the way a river's tributaries extended beyond the boundaries of any single  
valley.  
Something was moving.  
Not through space.  
Through structure.  
And then — a voice appeared everywhere.  
Not in one location. Not broadcast from a single point outward. It simply existed — simultaneously, instantaneously, without origin or direction — in every  
realm, every world, every layer of existence that possessed the capacity to perceive sound. It arrived the way sunlight arrived — not as something that traveled,  
but as something that was already there once the conditions for its perception were met.  
"...HELLOOOOO EVERYONE!!!"  
The voice was small. Absurdly, inappropriately small for the scale at which it operated. It carried the specific pitch and energy of something that was  
approximately the size of a melon and that possessed a confidence inversely proportional to its dimensions. It was cheerful in the way that natural disasters  
were cheerful — without malice, without restraint, and without any consideration for the structural damage it was causing to the listening comprehension of  
every being across multiple planes of existence.  
In the Spirit World, a council of ascended cultivators who had been mid-deliberation on matters of territorial sovereignty froze. The voice had not entered  
through their formation-sealed chamber. It had not bypassed their wards. It had simply been inside the room, as though it had always been there and had only  
now chosen to make itself known.  
One of them — a woman whose cultivation had taken three thousand years to refine and whose composure had never been disrupted by anything less than a  
continental-scale spiritual event — blinked.  
"...What," she said.  
In the Celestial World, a divine beast that had been sleeping for four hundred years opened one eye. The eye was the size of a lake. It focused on nothing in  
particular, because the voice had come from everywhere and therefore could not be located through conventional perception. The beast considered the  
situation for approximately two seconds, decided it was not worth the effort of opening the second eye, and went back to sleep.  
In the Dao World, where the laws of reality were not merely observed but manipulated by beings who understood the fundamental architecture of existence,  
three figures who had been discussing the nature of causality paused.  
"...Did something just shout at us?" one asked.  
"...Across all layers simultaneously," another confirmed.  
"...That should not be possible."  
"...And yet."  
They exchanged glances.  
"...Interesting."  
In the Mortal World — in villages and cities and sect compounds and isolated caves and mountain peaks and underground chambers — cultivators at every  
level of development heard it. Some dismissed it as hallucination. Some recognized it as spiritual communication and began analyzing the technique. Some  
panicked. A few — the very old, the very powerful, the very experienced — simply listened, because they had learned that the universe occasionally did things  
that defied explanation, and that the correct response to the inexplicable was attention rather than alarm.  
Fireworks followed.  

Not physical fireworks — not the chemical combustion of powder and pigment that mortal celebrations produced. These were structural. Reality bent. Space  
folded. Light emerged from points where light had no business existing, expanding outward in patterns that were too organized to be natural and too beautiful  
to be accidental. Colors that the visible spectrum did not technically contain bloomed across skies in every world simultaneously — deep violets that carried the  
weight of gravity, golds that hummed with frequencies that the ear could feel but the eye should not have been able to see, whites so pure they made  
everything around them look slightly incorrect by comparison.  
The fireworks did not explode and fade. They assembled. Each burst constructed itself from the surrounding space, pulling ambient energy from the  
environment and reorganizing it into momentary structures of breathtaking complexity before releasing it back in waves that carried the unmistakable  
signature of showmanship.  
Someone was performing.  
Someone was performing across every realm in existence simultaneously.  
And they were having an absolutely wonderful time doing it.  
Many could tell immediately: this was not an attack. The energy carried no hostile intent, no destructive force, no compulsion or control. It was pure  
spectacle — the kind of excess that only something with limitless power and zero restraint would produce, not because it needed to but because it wanted to  
and because no one had ever successfully told it to stop.  
But it was not natural either.  
Something was moving. Not through space — through structure. Through the connective tissue that linked one realm to another, through the channels that  
most beings did not know existed and that the few who did know existed had always assumed were inert. The fireworks were not the event. They were the  
announcement.  
For a brief moment — everything aligned.  
Reality complied. Not because it was forced. Because something within reality's own structure had been activated — something dormant, something old,  
something that had been waiting for a signal that it had now received.  
And at the center of all of it — visible to no one, perceptible to almost no one, but undeniably present to the handful of beings across all realms whose  
perception extended to the level where structure became visible —  
A small figure.  
Ears too large.  
Presence too confident.  
Microphone held in one paw.  
Back in the cave — Mateo stopped.  
He had been resting. The wounds were healing. The paste was working. His thoughts had been drifting between the forest's deeper mysteries and the memory  
of Tomas's sleeping face when something brushed against the edge of his awareness.  
Not force. Not intent.  
Structure.  
The same kind of spatial distortion he had just used during his alchemy — the compression of environment, the manipulation of the relationship between space  
and the things that occupied it. But on a scale that made his attempt feel like a child pressing a finger against the surface of a lake while standing beside an  
ocean.  
The distortion was everywhere. It did not come from a direction. It did not propagate from a source. It simply existed — a momentary shift in the fundamental  
architecture of the space around him, as though the rules that governed distance and position and separation had briefly been rewritten and then restored.  
Mateo turned. Looked up. Not at the cave ceiling — through it. Through the stone, through the forest, through the canopy, toward whatever had just touched  
every layer of reality simultaneously.  
The pendant pulsed.  
Not its usual steady rhythm. A rapid, irregular burst — the first time its pattern had broken since the acceleration began. It pulsed three times in quick  
succession, then went still, then pulsed again. The warmth intensified — not gradually but immediately, as though the pendant had recognized something in  
the distortion and was responding to it with the urgency of a compass needle spinning to find a pole that had just appeared.  
Then — within the distortion, within the structural shift that was already fading back to normalcy — he heard it.  
The voice.  
Small. Familiar. Carrying the specific indignation of something that had been told to wait and had decided that waiting was beneath its dignity.  
"...ANYWAY!"  
The Realm Spirit.  
Mateo's awareness sharpened. The voice was not coming from the pendant. It was not coming from the pocket dimension. It was coming from everywhere —  
broadcast across a medium that Mateo could feel but could not name, transmitted through the structural channels that connected realms the way veins  
connected zones within the forest.  
The creature was making an announcement.  
Across every world.  
Simultaneously.  
"You want in?" the Realm Spirit's voice declared, carrying the theatrical confidence of a street vendor who had just acquired the most valuable merchandise in  
existence and was now offering it to every passerby within earshot — which, in this case, was literally everyone. "You get one!"  
Tokens. Mateo could feel them materializing — not physically, not in his hands, but in the structure around him. Points of crystallized intent appearing across  
every realm like seeds being scattered across a field. Each one carrying a signature that matched the pocket dimension's energy — the same fractured,  
contradictory, reality-defying signature that the powerful beings in Chapter 1.5 had failed to touch.  
"...Prepare if you want."  
A pause — dramatic, deliberate, timed with the precision of someone who had rehearsed this moment.  
"...Or don't."  
Another pause.  
"I'll see you all soon."  
Gone.  
The voice vanished. The structural distortion settled. The fireworks — which had been assembling and disassembling themselves across every sky in existence —  
dissolved back into the ambient energy from which they had been drawn.  
Reality returned to normal.  
Or something close to it.  

In the cave, Mateo sat perfectly still.  
His expression had not changed during the broadcast. His posture had not shifted. His breathing had remained even. But his eyes — his eyes had changed.  
They carried the specific brightness of someone who had just received information that connected to something they had been wondering about for a very  
long time.  
The Realm Spirit. The pocket dimension. The space he had left behind when he shattered himself and fell. It was not merely persisting. It was activating. The  
creature — the ridiculous, microphone-wielding, excessively confident creature that had appeared in his dream and told him to come back later — had just  
announced itself to every realm in existence.  
Which meant the pocket dimension was no longer dormant.  
Which meant Phase 2 had begun.  
Which meant things were about to become significantly more complicated.  
Mateo stared at the cave ceiling.  
Then — slowly — he smiled.  
Not the controlled smile he wore during performances. Not the brief, genuine smile he allowed in front of Tomas. Something wider. Something that carried the  
specific amusement of someone who had just watched a being the size of a small dog announce its existence to gods, immortals, divine beasts, and  
transcendent entities across multiple planes of reality using fireworks and a microphone.  
"...Of course you would do that."  
Then — within his awareness, faint but unmistakable — the Realm Spirit's presence flickered. Brief. Directed. Not the broadcast — something smaller.  
Something personal. A thread of connection between the pendant against his chest and the space the Realm Spirit occupied, activated by the broadcast's  
structural resonance.  
For a fraction of a second, Mateo could see the creature.  
It was standing on nothing. Floating in a space that was still assembling itself around it — the pocket dimension's interior, incomplete, unstable, but more  
formed than it had been during their first encounter. The creature's ears were perked. Its microphone was still in its paw. And its expression carried the specific  
smugness of something that had just done exactly what it wanted to do and was deeply satisfied with the results.  
It looked at Mateo.  
Mateo looked at it.  
The Realm Spirit slowly raised one paw.  
And extended a single finger.  
Silence.  
The gesture was unmistakable. Universal. Transcendent of language, culture, realm, and species. It communicated, with absolute clarity and zero ambiguity, a  
sentiment that could be summarized as: I did what I wanted, you can't stop me, and I am aware that this will inconvenience you specifically.  
Mateo stared.  
Then burst out laughing.  
Not the controlled exhale of amusement he usually permitted. Not the suppressed smile or the brief crack in composure. Actual laughter — the full,  
unrestrained sound of someone who had been caught completely off guard by something so absurd, so perfectly timed, and so fundamentally in character that  
the only available response was surrender.  
"...Of course you would do that," he said again, the words breaking apart around the laughter.  
The Realm Spirit held the gesture for exactly long enough to ensure the message was received, then lowered its paw, adjusted its microphone, and vanished.  
The connection dissolved.  
The cave returned to silence.  
Mateo's laughter subsided — slowly, in stages, the way a fire burned down from flame to ember. His ribs protested the exertion. His shoulder objected  
strenuously. His body informed him, through multiple channels simultaneously, that laughing while wounded was inadvisable.  
He did not care.  
That had been worth it.  
"...That thing," he said, still smiling, "is going to cause more problems than I ever did."  
A pause.  
"...And I am entirely looking forward to watching."  
The pendant settled back into its rhythm — steadier now, the rapid bursts of the broadcast fading into the familiar pulse that had been strengthening for  
weeks. But the warmth was different. Deeper. More connected. As though the broadcast had opened a channel that had previously been closed, and the  
pendant was now communicating with something on the other end.  
The pocket dimension was waking up.  
And its guardian had just introduced itself to the entire universe with the subtlety of a firework in a library.  
Back in the village — Tomas stood at the edge of the training yard.  
He had felt it.  
Not the broadcast — he was not at a level where cross-realm structural distortions registered on his awareness. But the aftermath. The settling. The way the  
ambient energy in the village had rippled briefly and then smoothed, like the surface of a pond after a stone had been thrown into its center from a great  
height.  
The ground beneath his feet responded again — subtle, controlled, closer than before. The earth affinity that Mateo's corrections had freed from its internal  
conflict pressed against his awareness with a steadiness that had not been there that morning. Not learning. Not yet. But no longer resisting him completely.  
The connection that had flickered at the forest boundary was present here too — fainter, less distinct, but real.  
Something had changed.  
Not just in him.  
In the world.  
He looked toward the forest. The treeline held its usual darkness, its usual patience, its usual indifference to the concerns of the village beside it.  
"...Mateo..."  
The name carried more than a call. It carried the specific weight of someone who understood, without being able to explain how, that whatever had just  
happened was connected to the person who had walked into those trees and had not come back.  
This time — there was no doubt.  
Something had changed.  
And whatever it was — Mateo was already inside it.
