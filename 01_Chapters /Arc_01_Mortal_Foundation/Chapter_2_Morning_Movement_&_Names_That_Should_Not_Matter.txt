Morning in the village did not begin quietly.
It began with movement.
Mateo stepped out onto the packed dirt path as the early light filtered through the dense forest canopy that stretched over much of the settlement. The air carried the scent of damp earth and tree bark, mixed faintly with herbs, smoke, and metal — signs of a place that did not separate daily life from cultivation. The light itself arrived unevenly, broken by the canopy into fragments that shifted with the wind, creating patterns on the ground that moved independently of anything walking across them. Some patches of path sat in full warmth. Others remained in cool shadow that would not lift until midday, if it lifted at all.
The village itself was not large, nor was it designed with symmetry or display in mind. Homes were built from wood and reinforced with stone, shaped more by practicality than uniformity — some with roofs that sloped steeply to shed rain, others with broader overhangs that created shaded porches where tools hung and herbs dried in bundled rows. The wood had aged differently on each structure depending on its exposure to sun and moisture, giving the village a patchwork quality that would have looked disorganized to an outsider but that carried, to anyone who lived there, the specific comfort of a place that had grown rather than been built.
Paths curved naturally between the homes, worn into the ground by years of repetition rather than intentionally constructed roads. They widened where foot traffic concentrated — near the training yard, near the market stalls, near the well at the village center where water was drawn each morning in a sequence so consistent that the rope had worn a groove into the stone lip. They narrowed where fewer people walked, some tapering into traces that disappeared into the undergrowth at the village's edges.
No one here had seen the need to replace them with stone.
They could have.
They simply didn't.
A loud crack echoed nearby.
Mateo glanced to his right as two older trainees clashed near a clearing, their movements rough but purposeful. One stepped too far into the other's reach and was thrown into the dirt with a sharp exhale that scattered dust in a brief cloud around his shoulders.
"You're telegraphing every swing," the victor said, extending a hand downward.
"Then stop reading them," the other shot back, already pushing himself to his feet without accepting the help. He brushed dirt from his sleeve with the practiced indifference of someone who had been thrown into the ground often enough that it no longer qualified as an event.
Neither seemed particularly bothered.
The movements are committed but unrefined. Force output is adequate for this stage, but the transition between strikes lacks fluidity. The first trainee generates power from his shoulders rather than his hips, which limits both his range and his recovery speed. The second compensates for slower reflexes with aggression, which works until it does not. Inefficient, but effective enough for this level.
Mateo continued walking without stopping. His pace was steady, his posture relaxed, though his gaze moved constantly — not in distraction, but in observation. Every motion around him was noted, evaluated, and categorized without conscious effort. A woman carrying a basket of root vegetables walked past him on the path, her steps heavy with the specific weariness of someone who had been awake since before the light. A child darted between two homes chasing something small and fast that disappeared under a porch before it could be caught. The child stopped, crouched, peered into the darkness beneath the wooden planks, and then ran off in a different direction entirely, the original pursuit already forgotten.
The village was waking the way it always did — in layers, each one slightly louder than the last, until the accumulated activity reached the threshold that passed for normal.
Further along, a modest line of shops marked what passed for a trade path in the village. The structures were simple but well-used, their entrances open, goods displayed with minimal formality. A cloth merchant was arranging bolts of rough-spun fabric on a wooden frame outside his stall, each piece dyed in muted earth tones that suggested the dyes were locally sourced and the ambitions were modest. Beside him, a woman sold clay pots of varying sizes, some glazed, some raw, all arranged with the careful precision of someone who had learned through experience exactly how many pots could be stacked before gravity filed an objection.
Between the two stalls, a conversation was already underway.
"Your fabric bleeds color in the rain," the pot seller said, not looking up from her arrangement.
"Your pots crack in the cold," the cloth merchant replied, adjusting a bolt that did not need adjusting.
"My pots are fired properly."
"My fabric is dyed properly."
"Then why is my display cloth turning brown?"
The cloth merchant paused. Looked at the display cloth draped over the pot seller's table. Looked at the sky. Looked at the cloth again.
"...That was already brown."
"It was blue."
"It was a brown blue."
"That is not a color."
"It is now."
Mateo passed them without slowing, though the corner of his awareness filed the exchange with the same thoroughness it applied to everything else. The vendor dynamic was familiar — two people who occupied adjacent spaces, competed for overlapping customers, and expressed their mutual irritation through a daily verbal exchange that neither would admit they looked forward to.
It was, in its own way, a form of companionship.
The herb stall stood as it always did, bundles arranged neatly across a low table that had been sanded smooth by years of use. The scent was sharper here — bitter greens, dried roots, and something faintly medicinal drifting into the path. Small clay jars lined the back of the display, each one sealed with wax and marked with characters that described the contents in the herb vendor's precise, angular handwriting.
The woman behind it looked up as Mateo approached. She was neither young nor old — somewhere in the middle years that the village's lifestyle kept ambiguous — with hands that were perpetually stained at the fingertips from handling plants and a gaze that missed very little despite the appearance of casual attention.
"You're earlier than usual."
"...Marginally."
She snorted softly. "That's still earlier."
Mateo paused briefly, his gaze shifting across the display. The arrangement had changed since his last visit — subtle differences that most customers would never notice but that his awareness cataloged automatically.
"You moved these," he said, pointing to a small cluster of leaves set further from the others. They were darker than the surrounding herbs, their edges curling slightly inward in a way that suggested they had been dried at a different temperature or for a different duration.
"They dry better apart," she stated.
"...That's true," Mateo replied. The separation reduced moisture transfer between the leaves and the adjacent bundles, which would otherwise compromise the potency of both. A practical choice that reflected genuine understanding of the materials. He noted that as well.
She studied him for a moment — the specific look of someone deciding whether to ask a question they already knew the answer to.
"...You going out again?"
A short pause followed.
"...Yes."
She sighed faintly, though not in disapproval. Reaching beneath the table, she pulled out a small wrapped bundle and held it toward him. The wrapping was simple cloth, tied with cord, but the care with which it had been assembled suggested the contents had been selected rather than gathered.
"Take these," she said. "A small gift."
She was known for helping the younger cultivators now and again — small gestures that she never drew attention to and that the recipients were wise enough not to publicize, lest the generosity attract more demand than the herb vendor's patience could sustain.
Mateo accepted it without checking the contents.
"...Thank you," he said.
Then he continued along the path.
Behind him, the woman shook her head slightly, though a faint smile followed. She had been selling herbs in this village for longer than Mateo had been alive, and she had seen enough young cultivators pass through her stall to recognize the ones who were different. Most looked at her herbs and saw products. This one looked at them and saw systems.
She wasn't sure yet whether that was admirable or concerning.
She suspected it was both.
The ringing of metal carried across the path ahead. Mateo approached the blacksmith's area — a wide, open structure rather than an enclosed shop. Thick beams supported a simple roof of clay tiles, leaving most of the space exposed to air and light. The design was intentional — the forge required ventilation, and the smith required visibility, and the combination produced a workspace that was as much a part of the village's public life as the market stalls.
The ground there was darker, scorched in places from repeated work. Tiny fragments of metal glinted in the packed earth where they had been driven by years of hammer strikes — a permanent record of every piece that had been shaped on the anvil above them. The heat from the forge reached the path in waves, carrying with it the sharp, mineral scent of heated iron and the fainter undertone of the coal that fed it.
A heavy strike echoed as the smith's hammer came down against glowing metal, sparks scattering outward in brief arcs before vanishing. The smith was a broad man with forearms that looked like they had been carved from the same material he worked — dense, scarred, and functional without being decorative. His movements followed a rhythm that was as much a part of the village's ambient sound as the birdsong or the wind.
Mateo watched for a moment.
The alignment of the hammer's descent was slightly off — not enough to ruin the piece, but enough that the force was distributing unevenly across the metal's surface. The left side of the strike zone was receiving more compression than the right, which would create a subtle asymmetry in the finished product that most people would never detect and that the smith himself might not notice until the sharpening stage.
Alignment is slightly off. Force distribution uneven. The deviation originates in his right shoulder, which is compensating for fatigue in the elbow. He has been working longer this morning than his body prefers.
The smith paused mid-motion.
"...You're staring again."
Mateo didn't look away. "...Yes."
"...What is it this time?"
A brief silence passed.
"...Your last strike drifted."
The smith stared at him for a long moment. His expression cycled through several stages — surprise, irritation, the brief consideration of whether a child could possibly be right about something the smith had been doing for twenty years, and the reluctant acknowledgment that the child had been right about similar things before.
Then —
"...That's not helpful."
"...It was accurate."
"...That doesn't make it helpful."
Mateo considered that. The distinction between accuracy and helpfulness was one he encountered frequently. People did not always want to know what was true. They wanted to know what was useful. And usefulness, unlike truth, depended on context.
"...Noted."
He moved on.
As he passed further into the village, the forest grew denser between structures, the boundary between settlement and wilderness almost nonexistent. Roots broke through the ground between homes, thick and gnarled, their surfaces worn smooth by the feet that had been stepping over them for generations. Small creatures darted across paths without hesitation — a lizard sunning itself on a stone that had been warmed by the morning light, a bird pecking at something between the roots of a tree that grew so close to a home's wall that its branches brushed the shutters when the wind picked up.
This place did not force nature away.
It adapted around it.
Which, Mateo found, was peaceful.
The coexistence was not accidental. It was philosophical — a reflection of the village's understanding that the forest was not an obstacle to be conquered but an environment to be negotiated. The homes were built around the trees rather than in place of them. The paths followed the terrain rather than reshaping it. The training yards used the natural ground rather than leveling it.
It produced a settlement that looked, from a distance, like something the forest had grown rather than something humans had built. And that impression was not entirely wrong.
"...You adjusted again."
The voice cut through his thoughts.
Mateo slowed and stopped, finishing the motion he had been running through mentally — a sequence of weight transfers that he practiced while walking, invisible to anyone who was not specifically watching for the subtle shifts in his gait — before turning toward the source.
"...Is that a problem?"
Inés stood a short distance away, arms folded, posture perfectly balanced. Her gaze wasn't curious or irritated. It was precise — the attention of someone who observed not to understand but to verify. She had been watching him for longer than the question implied, and the question itself was not an inquiry. It was a statement dressed in the syntax of curiosity.
"You correct midway through motion, then stop before finishing," she said. "You've done it repeatedly."
Mateo exhaled quietly.
She is more observant than most. The corrections are subtle enough that they should not be visible during casual movement. Either she has been watching closely for an extended period, or her perception of physical motion is significantly more refined than her cultivation level would suggest. Both possibilities are worth noting.
"You're imagining things."
"No," she replied calmly. "I'm not."
Her tone carried no challenge. No accusation. Just the flat certainty of someone who trusted their own observation more than someone else's denial. She would not be convinced by deflection. She would only be convinced by evidence, and the evidence supported her position rather than his.
Mateo filed that assessment alongside everything else.
Inés would require more careful management than most.
Before the exchange could continue — the flow of the village shifted.
Not suddenly. Not loudly.
But noticeably.
The conversations that had been filling the morning air with their layered noise quieted, not into silence but into a lower register — voices dropping, words shortening, attention redirecting. Those nearby gave space without being told, their bodies shifting to create wider paths along the main walkway as though responding to a signal that had not been given but had been felt.
Mateo glanced slightly to the side.
"...They're not here for me... right?" he murmured.
A brief pause.
Inés shrugged faintly.
"...They're the Elders," she said. "So who knows."
Three figures approached along the path, unhurried but undeniable. Their presence did not press — it settled into the environment, aligning it naturally. The air around them felt slightly more defined, as though the space itself recognized that something with greater weight was moving through it and adjusted accordingly.
Elder Marcos walked at the front, his posture composed and unhurried — the measured stride of someone who had been walking this path for decades and saw no reason to alter his pace for anything less than an emergency. His expression was neutral but attentive, his gaze sweeping the gathered trainees with the specific quality of someone who was not looking at them but through them — assessing what lay beneath the surface rather than what was displayed on it.
Beside him, Elder Tomás Aguilar moved with a warmth that contrasted sharply with Marcos's reserve. His frame was slightly broader, his posture more open, and his face carried the easy expression of someone who genuinely enjoyed being around young people — a quality that made him popular among the trainees and occasionally exasperated the more formal members of the council.
The third was Elder Liang, who carried a thin sheet of pressed bark and a writing brush, his attention divided between the path ahead and whatever he had been documenting before the walk began. He moved with the distracted precision of someone whose body navigated the physical world on autopilot while his mind operated elsewhere entirely.
Elder Marcos stepped forward.
"...You've all been training hard, I see," he said.
His voice wasn't loud. But it didn't need to be — it carried with a clarity that seemed to bypass distance entirely, arriving at each listener's ears as though it had been spoken directly to them. The effect was subtle but unmistakable, and it drew the attention of every trainee in the yard without requiring any of them to move closer.
Elder Tomás Aguilar let out a short, hearty laugh.
"...Good. Good," he said, nodding to himself with the genuine enthusiasm of someone who found the existence of young people training to be inherently delightful. "It's good to be young."
He lifted one hand and gave a casual thumbs-up toward no one in particular. Several trainees exchanged glances. One almost smiled. Elder Liang did not look up from his bark sheet.
"Good," Elder Marcos continued, his tone even. "Then you will not be surprised when the time comes."
A pause followed.
"...Time for what?" someone asked from the back of the group — a trainee whose voice cracked slightly on the last word, betraying the nervousness that the question was meant to conceal.
Elder Marcos glanced toward the group, not unkindly — just measuring.
"...For your assessments."
A faint ripple passed through those gathered. Not fear — anticipation mixed with uncertainty, the specific tension of people who had been told something was coming and had just learned that "coming" meant "now."
"This is only a preliminary reading," Elder Tomás Aguilar said, stepping forward with a reassuring tone that managed to be both calming and completely unhelpful. "A surface scan, at best."
Elder Liang placed something onto a flat stone surface nearby, his movements careful and deliberate — the handling of someone who understood that what he carried was not fragile but was significant enough to warrant respect.
He gestured toward the stone as it was set down.
A sphere.
At first glance, it appeared dull — grey, uneven, almost mundane. The kind of object that a person might walk past without a second look, assuming it was a stone that someone had placed there for reasons that didn't concern them. But the longer it was looked at, the less simple it seemed. The surface wasn't smooth stone. It held faint internal layers, like compressed mineral veins frozen in unnatural symmetry — patterns that should not have existed in natural rock but that were too organized to be artificial in the conventional sense.
Hairline lines ran across it — not cracks, but channels. Pathways. As though the sphere had been designed to move something through itself, and the lines were the routes that something followed.
The air around it felt different.
Not heavy. Not sharp.
Reactive.
As though the sphere was not merely an object but a system — dormant, patient, and waiting for input.
Mateo's gaze fixed on it.
"...Interesting," he murmured. "...A smaller version of a testing sphere."
The observation was quiet enough that only Inés, standing nearby, could have heard it. The fact that Mateo recognized the sphere's function before it had been explained was itself notable — but the fact that he described it as a "smaller version" implied familiarity with a larger one, which should not have been possible for someone who had never left the village.
Inés heard it.
She did not react.
But she stored it.
The stone pulsed faintly. Not glowing yet — but aware of contact before it happened, responding to the proximity of spiritual energy the way a still pond responded to the approach of a stone that had not yet been thrown.
"This will show you your alignment to each affinity," Elder Marcos said.
"Nothing more," Elder Liang added, looking up from his bark sheet for the first time.
"It reads what you are aligned to," Elder Marcos continued, "but more importantly, it shows what you are not."
Elder Tomás Aguilar added calmly, "...Do not confuse potential with capability."
"The larger stone," Elder Marcos said, "will reveal what this cannot."
A pause followed.
"...Not by giving you anything," he added, "...but by showing you more clearly what is already there."
A trainee stepped forward. Young, nervous, his hand trembling slightly as he placed it against the sphere's surface.
For a breath — nothing.
Then — a glow.
Dull red. Uneven. Flaring at first, then collapsing inward as though the energy could not sustain its own expression.
"...Fire," someone muttered from the group.
"Hmm. Fire affinity, but very low," Elder Liang said, his brush already moving across the bark sheet. "Stabilize your output next time."
The trainee stepped back, visibly unsettled — not by the result itself, but by the experience of having something inside him made visible to others. The sphere had not merely tested him. It had displayed him.
One by one, trainees stepped forward.
A hand placed.
A pause.
Then — reaction.
Fire. Water. Metal.
Most were simple. Clear. Limited. The sphere responded with brief, contained glows that confirmed what most already suspected about themselves — alignments that matched their training tendencies, their physical preferences, the elements they had always felt most comfortable working with.
A girl produced a steady blue that held for several seconds before fading — water affinity, mid-tier, consistent. She stepped back looking satisfied. A boy slightly older generated a flash of grey that flickered uncertainly before settling — metal affinity, low-tier, unstable. He stepped back looking less satisfied. Another produced a warm yellow that spread evenly across the sphere's surface — earth affinity, stable and grounded. Elder Tomás Aguilar nodded approvingly.
Affinity, in cultivation, was not defined by presence alone. All possessed alignment — every living being existed in relation to the elemental forces that composed the world, the way every object existed in relation to gravity. The question was not whether alignment existed but how deep it ran.
Affinities existed across tiers. Nine levels. Each step between them marked not gradual growth — but meaningful difference. Low tiers allowed only basic interaction with the element in question. Mid tiers enabled consistency — the ability to draw on the affinity reliably under varying conditions. Higher tiers shaped understanding itself, allowing the cultivator to perceive and manipulate the element at a level that transcended technique and became intuition.
Beyond that — few progressed.
Some affinities were common. Earth. Fire. Water. Metal. Lightning. Wood. These appeared regularly across populations, distributed unevenly but without true rarity. Others emerged as variations — ice derived from water, certain metallic mutations branching from earth, wind existing at the boundary between several elements without belonging fully to any.
And beyond those — the rare.
Space.
Time.
Those were exceptional even among the largest clans and sects. The kind of affinities that drew attention not because they were powerful in themselves, but because they suggested a relationship with the fundamental architecture of reality that most cultivators could not even perceive, let alone manipulate.
Mateo watched carefully.
The sphere reveals more than alignment. The stability of the response indicates the cultivator's control over their own energy output. The duration indicates depth. The evenness indicates integration. It is not merely showing what element they connect to — it is showing how well their internal system communicates with external forces. It reveals structural instability.
"Not just affinity," Inés replied quietly, as though she had been following a similar line of thought.
"...Better design than expected," Mateo said.
Another stepped forward. Then another.
Patterns emerged. Some clear. Some weak. Some fragmented — responses that flickered between two colors without settling on either, suggesting alignment that had not yet resolved or that existed in conflict with itself.
Then — Tomas stepped forward.
No hesitation. No nervousness. He walked to the sphere with the same steady, grounded determination that characterized everything he did, placed his hand against its surface, and waited.
The reaction was immediate.
Deep. Steady.
A rich brown light spread outward — not flickering, not unstable, not the tentative glow that most trainees had produced. This was grounded. Rooted. The light moved through the sphere's internal channels with a consistency that suggested the affinity behind it was not merely present but established — built into the foundation of Tomas's cultivation base rather than sitting loosely on its surface.
Earth-aligned.
The stone responded cleanly. Strong.
Several trainees shifted. The difference between Tomas's response and the ones that had preceded it was visible to everyone, and the murmur that passed through the group carried the specific tone of people recalibrating their assumptions.
But something else followed.
For a brief moment — beneath the brown — another tone surfaced.
Faint. Subtle. A thread of color so delicate that it existed at the threshold of perception, visible only to someone who was watching the sphere's deeper layers rather than its surface.
Green.
Most did not notice. Some thought they did — a flicker at the edge of awareness that they would later dismiss as a trick of the light or the residual glow of the previous assessment.
But it remained.
Mateo didn't move. But his gaze sharpened slightly — the fractional narrowing of focus that he could not always suppress when something confirmed a hypothesis he had been holding.
"...There it is," he murmured under his breath. A dual affinity. The secondary signature is wood-aligned, consistent with the green undertone. It is undeveloped, likely undetectable by anyone who is not specifically looking for it. But it is present.
He allowed himself a small, quiet chuckle — barely audible, the kind of sound that could be mistaken for a cleared throat.
Not visible to most. Not recognized.
But present.
A secondary alignment. Not developed. Not accessible. Not yet.
Rare — not because it was powerful, but because it was difficult. At early stages, dual alignment created imbalance. Divided focus. Conflicting growth. Unstable progression. The two affinities competed for the same resources within the cultivator's system, each one pulling energy along pathways that the other did not share.
Few ever refined both paths simultaneously unless guided by someone powerful enough to understand the interaction and skilled enough to manage it.
Fewer did so successfully.
Tomas did not know.
But the foundation existed.
And foundations — if left unbalanced — shaped everything that followed.
Mateo saw it.
He said nothing.
Because it did not matter yet. The secondary affinity was dormant. It would not interfere with Tomas's earth-based cultivation at this stage. But as he grew stronger, as his core developed and his energy pathways deepened, the wood signature would strengthen as well. And when it did, the conflict between the two would begin to manifest — subtle at first, then increasingly disruptive.
Revealing it now would create unnecessary variables. Tomas would worry. The elders would investigate. Attention would be drawn to something that was not yet a problem and that Mateo was not yet in a position to help solve.
Better to wait.
Better to watch.
Better to be ready when the time came.
Tomas stepped back.
"...That normal?" Diego asked quietly from beside Mateo, his voice carrying the genuine curiosity of someone who had watched the assessment with more attention than his casual posture suggested.
"...No," Inés replied from Mateo's other side. Her tone was flat — not impressed, not concerned, simply acknowledging that what they had witnessed was outside the expected range.
Mateo said nothing.
Because it didn't matter.
Yet.
Elder Marcos's gaze lingered on Tomas for half a breath longer than it had on the others — a duration so brief that only someone watching the elder rather than the trainee would have noticed it. The look carried no surprise. No alarm. Just the careful, calibrated attention of someone filing information for later use.
"...Continue your training," he said.
That was all.
But it was not dismissal.
It was acknowledgment.
Mateo stepped forward.
He walked to the sphere with the same unhurried calm he applied to everything, placed his hand against its surface, and waited.
When his hand touched the stone — it responded.
A calm, steady resonance. Neither weak nor strong. The kind of glow that belonged to someone with modest cultivation and unremarkable alignment — the spiritual equivalent of room temperature. Present. Functional. Forgettable.
Nothing else surfaced.
The elders moved on immediately. No lingering gaze. No half-breath of additional attention. No indication that the result warranted anything beyond the cursory acknowledgment it received.
Mateo stepped back.
Unnoticed.
Exactly as intended.
The suppression had held perfectly. The sphere had read exactly what Mateo had allowed it to read — the carefully maintained surface of his cultivation, the layer he presented to the world, the version of himself that existed for the sole purpose of being unremarkable. Beneath that surface, where his true cultivation resided, the sphere had found nothing — because Mateo had ensured there was nothing to find.
The effort required to maintain that suppression during direct contact with a diagnostic tool was not trivial. The sphere was designed to read what was there, and convincing it that less was there than actually existed demanded a level of energy control that most cultivators at his apparent level did not possess.
But Mateo was not at his apparent level.
And the sphere, for all its elegant design, could only read what it was allowed to touch.
Elder Marcos stepped forward again.
"...Prepare yourselves," he said. "The next stage is not guidance."
A pause.
"...It is the path towards your future."
The words settled over the group with the weight of something that had been decided long before it was spoken. The assessment was complete. The results were recorded. And whatever came next would be shaped by what the sphere had revealed — and what it had not.
Mateo stood at the edge of the group, his expression unchanged, his posture relaxed, his presence exactly as forgettable as he intended it to be.
But his mind was already moving forward.
Tomas's dual affinity. The sphere's diagnostic capabilities. The elders' response patterns. The village's cultivation infrastructure and its limitations.
All of it cataloged.
All of it useful.
All of it — eventually — relevant.
