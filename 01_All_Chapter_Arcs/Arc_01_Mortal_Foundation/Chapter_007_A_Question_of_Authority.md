# Chapter 7 - A Question of Authority

Chapter 7  
A Question of Authority  

By the third time Mateo was told not to go around causing more trouble, he had already decided that he would.  
It was not simply stubbornness, and it was not pure mischief, though he would not have denied either entirely. It was, more than anything, indifference. A  
restriction existed for a reason. But if that reason had never been properly examined, then the restriction itself remained an open question.  
Mateo preferred clarity.  
And clarity, more often than not, required testing.  
The problem, for the village rather than for Mateo, was that clarity had been arriving with increasing frequency lately, and always in ways that produced  
headaches for the people responsible for maintaining order.  
No one had caught him at old man Chen's shop. That much was certain. The window latch had been returned to its original position. The shelves appeared  
undisturbed. The inventory, as far as Chen could determine during his morning count, was accounted for. Mostly. But the old man had spent the better part of the  
following day muttering about drafts and unreliable latches and glaring at the wind as though it owed him a personal apology, and several villagers had since  
noticed that Mateo happened to be in possession of herbs no one remembered selling him.  
No one said anything directly.  
But the glances had started, the kind that carry suspicion without enough evidence to act on. The worst kind, because they lingered.  
Then there was the matter of the boundary.  
Three days prior, a patrol runner had reported finding fresh footprints along the southeastern ridge, well past the last marker stone that indicated the accepted  
limit for unsupervised trainees. The prints were light, evenly spaced, and showed no sign of hesitation or retreat. Whoever had made them had walked out  
deliberately, stopped at a point overlooking the deeper canopy, stood there for some time, and then returned along the same path without deviating from it once.  
The elders had not named anyone.  
But Rafael had received a visit that evening, and the conversation, while brief, had involved the phrase your son more than once.  
Mateo had listened from the adjacent room, his expression neutral, his posture relaxed, and his complete lack of surprise confirming everything without  
admitting anything.  
The village, for its part, had survived these disruptions the way it survived most things: through collective sighing and the quiet understanding that certain  
problems were easier to endure than to solve.  
What helped, and what had always helped, was that the village existed inside a protection most of its residents had long since stopped thinking about.  
The forest pressed close. That much was obvious to anyone who lived there. Trees grew between homes. Roots broke through paths. Wildlife moved through the  
settlement without fear, and the boundary between village and wilderness was less a line than a suggestion.  
And yet nothing truly dangerous ever crossed it.  
Not the ridge-backed boars that could shatter stone with a single charge. Not the shadow cats that hunted in the mid-canopy and moved faster than most Qi  
Gathering cultivators could track. Not the things deeper still, the ones Tomas had described in low tones over broth, the ones that did not behave like beasts at  
all.  
None of them came close.  
Most villagers attributed this to location. The village sat in a natural valley between two ridgelines, sheltered by terrain and wind patterns that made it less  
accessible than the open forest. Some believed the density of human activity created its own deterrent, an ambient noise that beasts preferred to avoid.  
The elders knew better.  
Generations ago, before the current council, before most of the families now living here had ever arrived, someone had ascended from this village.  
Not metaphorically. Not in reputation or influence.  
They had broken through the Heaven Ascension Realm and left the Mortal World entirely.  
The details had faded since. The name survived only in fragments, spoken during ceremonies and etched into a stone marker near the village center that most  
children walked past without ever reading. But what remained, long after the person themselves had vanished into a higher world, was their aura.  
Not as a visible force. Not as anything that could be measured or channeled.  
As a presence.  
It had seeped into the land itself, into the soil and the roots and the stone beneath the village. A residue of power so vast that decades of erosion had not dissolved  
it. Heat stays in stone long after the fire is out, and it was like that: not burning, not active, and undeniably still there.  
And it was enough.  
Beasts that approached the village boundary felt it before they understood it. Not as threat. Not as pain. As wrongness. A deep instinctual signal that told them, at  
a level beneath thought and beneath even survival, that this space belonged to something they could not challenge. Most turned away without ever knowing why.  
The stronger ones paused, assessed, and chose differently. Even the creatures that operated past animal intelligence, the ones with spiritual awareness, the ones  
that could genuinely think, recognized that whatever had marked this territory was not something they wished to test.  
The humans, for the most part, felt nothing at all.  
Unless they arrived carrying malice toward the village or the people in it. Then the sensation arrived differently: a subtle pressure behind the eyes, a tightness in  
the chest, a growing unease with no identifiable source that made lingering deeply uncomfortable. It was not lethal. It was not even painful. It was discouraging.  
It made a person reconsider their intentions without ever understanding why they had reconsidered them.  
The ascended cultivator had also laid down formations, rune-based restrictions woven into the natural vein structure beneath the village. These were more  

deliberate than the passive aura. They sealed sound within certain structures, reinforced the boundary markers, and created subtle wards meant to alert the elders  
to any significant spiritual disturbance near the perimeter.  
At least, they were supposed to.  
Mateo had examined them with some interest over the past several weeks.  
Quietly. Thoroughly. And with a growing appreciation for how easy they were to adjust without anyone noticing.  

The Night Before  

The village had settled into its usual evening rhythm, the kind of quiet that is not truly quiet but simply agreed upon. Cooking fires dimmed. Conversations retreated  
indoors. The last of the trainees had left the yard, and the paths between structures held only the occasional figure moving with the unhurried purpose of someone  
heading somewhere they were expected to be.  
Mateo was not heading somewhere he was expected to be.  
He moved along the eastern path, keeping to the edge where the shadows from the overhanging canopy fell heaviest. Not because he needed to hide, exactly, but  
because being seen would require explanations, and explanations would require a version of the truth he had not yet decided on.  
The elder meeting hall sat at the far end of the village's central cluster, slightly elevated, built from heavier timber than the surrounding structures. Its roof was  
reinforced with stone tiles rather than thatch, and the foundation extended deeper into the ground than was typical, anchoring it to the bedrock beneath. These  
were not aesthetic choices. They were structural, meant to support the formations embedded within them.  
Mateo had noticed the formation stones weeks ago. They were not hidden, exactly, but they were not advertised either. Small inscribed stones set into the  
foundation at precise intervals, each carved with rune sequences that channeled ambient energy through predetermined pathways. Sound suppression. Spiritual  
dampening. Eavesdropping deterrence.  
Standard work. Well designed for its era.  
But not maintained.  
And not, as he had discovered across three separate evenings of careful observation, particularly difficult to understand.  
He crouched beside the hall's eastern wall, where the lowest foundation stone sat partially exposed by years of soil erosion. The inscriptions were still clean, the  
grooves sharp enough to trace with a fingertip. Energy moved through them in slow predictable cycles, each pulse following a path that had been carved decades  
ago and had not deviated since.  
Predictable.  
Mateo placed two fingers against the stone and closed his eyes.  
The formation's structure unfolded in his awareness, complete and immediate. Three anchor points, each connected by flow channels that cycled energy in a  
triangular loop. The sound suppression operated on the outermost layer, catching vibrations before they could pass through the walls. The spiritual dampening sat  
beneath it, absorbing fluctuations that might interfere with the primary function. And at the center, a stabilizing core that kept everything aligned.  
It was elegant, in the way old systems often are. Built with more care than efficiency, designed to endure rather than adapt.  
Mateo studied it for several minutes. There was something almost pleasant in it, following another person's reasoning through their work, seeing where they had  
been careful and where they had assumed no one would ever look.  
Then he made a decision.  
He did not break the formation. He did not disable it. He did not remove any component or sever any connection.  
He shifted three anchor points.  
By a margin so small that no standard inspection would detect it. A key turned one degree past its intended position still engages the lock, and it no longer holds  
firm. The energy continued to cycle. The inscriptions continued to glow faintly. Everything appeared functional.  
But the sound suppression layer, which had previously caught vibrations at the wall's surface, now caught them approximately two inches past the wall's surface.  
Which meant that for a distance of two inches, sound traveled freely.  
Two inches was not much.  
But two inches, multiplied across every wall in the building, compounded by the hall's natural acoustics and the height of its ceiling, meant that conversations  
held inside would carry just far enough to be heard from the corridor.  
Not clearly. Not completely.  
But enough.  
Mateo withdrew his hand and stood.  
He examined his work once more, extending his awareness through the formation to confirm that nothing appeared disrupted. The cycles continued. The energy  
flowed. The stones held their inscriptions without visible change.  
Perfect.  
He turned to leave.  
Diego was standing three feet behind him.  
Mateo did not flinch and did not startle. He simply stopped and regarded Diego with the calm patience of someone who had been expecting this interruption,  
which he absolutely had not.  
"...What are you doing here?" Mateo asked.  
"I followed you," Diego said, as though this were obvious.  
"...Why?"  
"Because you left the house at night walking in a direction that has no food at the end of it. That is suspicious behavior."  
Mateo considered this.  
It was, unfortunately, sound.  

"...How long have you been standing there?"  
"Long enough to watch you crouch next to a wall and touch rocks for ten minutes."  
A pause.  
"...And?"  
Diego folded his arms. "I have no idea what you just did. But I know it was something, because you had the face."  
"...I do not have a face."  
"You have a very specific face. It is the face you make when you are about to ruin someone's day and you are enjoying the planning phase."  
Mateo said nothing.  
Which, as Diego had learned by now, was confirmation.  
"...Are you going to tell me what you did?"  
"...No."  
"...Am I going to find out anyway?"  
Mateo resumed walking, his pace unhurried, his expression settling back into its default state of mild, unthreatening neutrality.  
"...Tomorrow."  
Diego stared at his retreating back.  
"...Why do I feel like I should apologize to someone in advance?"  
Mateo did not respond. But in the darkness, where Diego could not see it, the corner of his mouth twitched.  
Tomorrow was going to be a very productive morning.  

The Morning After  

The following day arrived with the kind of unearned brightness that suggested the weather had not been informed of what was about to happen.  
Mateo walked the main path through the village center at a pace that suggested nothing in particular was planned. The morning market had already begun its  
daily performance, vendors arranging their goods with the practiced efficiency of people who had done this so many times that the motions had become  
indistinguishable from instinct.  
To his left, an older woman was arranging clay jars of preserved vegetables on a low wooden shelf, each one sealed with wax and stamped with a mark that  
presumably meant something to someone. A man beside her was attempting to sell what he described as spiritual tea to a skeptical customer who kept sniffing  
the leaves and frowning.  
"It cleanses the meridians," the vendor insisted.  
"It smells like dirt."  
"...Spiritual dirt."  
The customer put the leaves down and walked away. The vendor watched him go with the resigned expression of a man who had expected exactly that outcome  
and had committed to the pitch anyway.  
Mateo observed the exchange without slowing, filed it away, and continued walking.  
Further along, two children chased each other between the stalls, narrowly avoiding a stack of dried fish that had been arranged with considerably more  
architectural ambition than structural integrity. The stack wobbled. The vendor lunged. The stack held. The vendor exhaled. The children did not notice any of it.  
Mateo noticed.  
He noticed everything.  
That was the problem.  
"...You are doing it again."  
Tomas' voice carried that familiar note somewhere between disbelief and resignation as he walked beside Mateo, who had very clearly chosen a path that did not  
lead to any approved training ground. Their steps fell unevenly across the ground, one path worn smooth from repetition, the other less defined and resisting  
slightly underfoot, the way unused routes tend to.  
Mateo did not turn. "...Doing what?"  
"Walking toward somewhere you are not supposed to be."  
"That is an assumption."  
Tomas gave him a long look. "You are heading toward the elder meeting hall."  
"...Correct."  
"...You are not allowed in there."  
Mateo slowed slightly and finally glanced at him. "...That depends."  
Tomas frowned faintly. "On what?"  
Mateo's gaze returned forward as he continued walking, entirely unhurried. The air felt subtly different the closer they came to the hall. Not heavier, but more  
defined, as though intent lingered there longer than it should have.  
Let's see how far this holds.  
"...On whether they realize I am not supposed to be there."  
A short silence followed.  
"...That is not how rules work," Tomas said.  
Mateo reached the door.  

"...It is," he replied calmly, placing a hand against the wood. The surface held firm beneath his touch. Simple, solid, unreinforced. Predictable.  
"...if no one is there to enforce them."  
Then he opened it.  

The Council  

The room fell silent almost immediately.  
Nine elders sat around a low table, their discussion halting mid-sentence as the door opened without announcement. The full council was present, which was  
uncommon for a routine session and suggested the agenda had carried more weight than usual.  
Elder Marcos occupied the head of the table, his posture straight but unhurried, holding the kind of stillness that comes from decades of authority exercised  
without ever raising his voice. To his left sat Elder Hiro, whose expression had already begun shifting from mild irritation toward something sharper the moment  
the door moved. Elder Vargas sat across from him, mid-sentence when the interruption arrived, his mouth still partially open around a word he no longer  
intended to finish. Elder Liang had been taking notes on a thin sheet of pressed bark and had stopped writing without lifting his brush. Elder Park sat with his  
hands folded, his gaze moving to the door with the measured patience of someone who evaluated before he reacted.  
Further along the table, Elder Doña Reyes occupied her usual seat near the corner, a position she had claimed years ago and that no one had ever suggested she  
change. She was the oldest woman on the council, her silver hair pulled back in a simple knot, her hands resting on the table with the calm weight of someone  
who had sat through enough councils to know that most urgency is manufactured. Her eyes moved to Mateo and stayed there, not in surprise, but with the quiet  
attention of someone cataloguing a detail she had not expected to be given.  
Elder Kurosawa sat beside her, his posture deceptively relaxed, one arm along the back of his chair in a way that suggested casual comfort and allowed him to  
observe every face at the table without turning his head. He had not been speaking when the door opened. He rarely spoke first. He preferred to let conversations  
arrive where he had already predicted they would go.  
Elder Tomás Aguilar sat nearest the door, which meant he was the first to see Mateo enter. His reaction was different from the others. Where Hiro's expression  
hardened and Vargas's froze, Aguilar's softened briefly, a flicker of something that was not quite amusement and was certainly not anger, before settling into the  
neutral composure the moment demanded.  
At the far end of the room, where the light from the window barely reached, Elder Shen remained unusually still. He had not been part of the discussion at all. He  
sat apart from it, fingers loosely folded, gazing at what seemed to be the sky, listening to the shape of the conversation rather than the words in it.  
"...Who allowed you in here?" Elder Hiro asked, his voice firm but not yet aggressive.  
Mateo stepped inside as though the question itself were optional.  
"...Are you asking me," he said mildly, pointing at himself with an expression of faint surprise, "or questioning your own awareness?"  
The silence that followed was different from the one before it.  
Tomas, who had stopped just inside the doorway, stared at Mateo as though something fundamental had shifted underfoot.  
...He is doing it again.  
Elder Hiro leaned forward slightly, frowning.  
"...You are acting like such a brat. What do you need, child?"  
Mateo inclined his head a fraction.  
"...I was strolling, and I ended up here somehow." He shrugged.  
Elder Liang blinked. Elder Kurosawa's eyes narrowed almost imperceptibly, not in irritation but in the precise focused way of someone who had just heard an  
opening move in a game he recognized.  
"...Then why are you here?"  
Mateo moved closer to the table, his steps calm, his posture measured in a way that did not match his age. His presence did not dominate the room. It did not  
yield to it either.  
"...Inspection."  
The word landed with far more weight than it should have.  
"...Inspection?" Elder Vargas repeated.  
Mateo gestured lightly toward the room.  
"If this meeting is meant to remain private," he said, his tone even and unhurried, "then the current arrangement is inefficient. The seating alone allows sound to  
carry outward without resistance."  
Elder Park frowned. "...What are you implying?"  
Mateo turned his gaze toward the windows, then the doorway, then back to the table.  
"You are audible from outside," he said simply.  
A brief silence followed.  
Elder Vargas shifted in his seat. "...Is that true?"  
Tomas stiffened as several gazes turned toward him at once.  
"...Yes," he admitted after a moment. "...You can hear it from outside the hall."  
The room stilled.  
Elder Liang rose and moved toward the window to test it himself, and confirmed the faint echo of voices carrying further than they should have, lingering along  
the corridor before fading unevenly.  
"...Why was this not noticed earlier?" he muttered.  
"Because no one was standing outside listening," Elder Hiro responded, though his tone had lost most of its confidence.  
Elder Doña Reyes, who had not spoken until now, lifted her gaze from the table.  

"That is not entirely accurate."  
The room turned toward her. She had not raised her voice. She had never needed to.  
"This hall was sealed," she said. "I was present when the formations were inspected after the last restoration, nearly forty years ago. Sound suppression.  
Eavesdropping deterrence. Spiritual interference dampening. They were laid into the foundation by someone who understood vein-integrated rune work at a level  
most practitioners today cannot replicate."  
She paused, and let the weight of that settle across the table.  
"They should still be active."  
Elder Marcos, who had been listening without speaking, letting the others spend their reactions before he offered his own, nodded once.  
"Liang. Check them."  
Elder Liang placed his palm flat against the wall and extended his awareness into the stone. His brow furrowed.  
"...They are active," he said slowly. "But something is off. The alignment has shifted. Slightly. As though the anchor points were adjusted by a fraction."  
"Adjusted?" The word came sharply from Elder Hiro. "By whom?"  
"That is what I would like to know."  
Elder Kurosawa leaned forward for the first time. "Adjusted is a precise word," he said carefully. "You are saying this was not degradation."  
Elder Liang hesitated, then shook his head. "The shift is too uniform. Natural decay produces inconsistency. This is clean."  
Several elders exchanged glances. The unease in the room deepened, not because the formations had failed, but because they had been changed, and no one  
present could explain when, or how, or by whom.  
Elder Tomás Aguilar, who had spent the entire exchange watching Mateo's face rather than the wall, noticed what the others missed.  
The boy's expression had not changed. Not once. Not when the formations were mentioned. Not when Liang confirmed the tampering. Not when the unease in  
the room deepened around him.  
That absence of reaction was, in Aguilar's experience, considerably more telling than any reaction would have been.  
Mateo folded his hands behind his back, already turning slightly away.  
Minimal pressure. Maximum disruption.  
"...I assumed you were aware."
