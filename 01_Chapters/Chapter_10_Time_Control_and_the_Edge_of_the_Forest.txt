Time passed.
Not in any grand or dramatic way. There were no sudden breakthroughs that shook the village, no public declarations of talent, no spectacular moments that made the elders regret letting Mateo remain unsupervised for as long as they had. Instead, the years moved quietly, folding one season into the next as though the village itself had decided to breathe more slowly.
That, in itself, suited Mateo well.
After the trouble he had caused in his younger years, he learned—not restraint, exactly, but refinement. He still slipped into places he was not supposed to enter. He still tested rules just to see whether they deserved to exist. He still caused headaches often enough that more than one elder developed the habit of narrowing their eyes whenever Mateo passed by, as though merely seeing him was a warning of future inconvenience.
But he became more careful.
More selective.
He learned when chaos was useful and when it merely wasted time.
By the time he was fourteen, most of the village had reached the same conclusion about him: Mateo had become quieter only in the sense that storms were quiet before breaking.
His body had changed more honestly than his habits.
He was taller now, though not imposing. His frame had lengthened and tightened, the softness of childhood burned away through constant training. He carried lean muscle rather than bulk, his strength hidden beneath efficient movement rather than noise. Nothing about him looked excessive at first glance. That was intentional. Mateo did not dislike being underestimated. In many situations, he found it convenient.
What had changed most was not how much stronger he had become, but how well he understood what that strength actually was.
He trained differently than the others.
The village children and young adults still practiced as they always had—through repetition, force, competition, and the simple honesty of getting hit hard enough to remember a mistake. Mateo did not reject that approach. He simply went further.
Where others practiced movement, he studied transfer.
Where others hardened the body, he focused on coordination.
Where others thought of strength as output, he thought of it as a chain that could not afford a single broken link.
Every morning before the village fully woke, he trained in silence.
Not immediately with strikes.
Never immediately.
First came standing.
He would settle his feet into the packed dirt behind the house, toes spreading slightly inside worn training shoes, feeling the texture of the ground beneath him—hard earth, slight give, shallow irregularities shaped by weather and repeated steps. He would breathe once, then let his weight descend.
Not collapse.
Descend.
The shift started in the soles of his feet, pressure distributing through heel and arch, then balancing back toward the ball where movement could be born cleanly. From there, his ankles aligned—not tensed, but held. His calves tightened in smooth bands beneath the skin, not with strain, but with readiness.
Then knees.
Then hips.
Then spine.
Then breath.
He learned to feel every stage of it.
If his heel pressed too deeply, force would drag. If his knee bent too far inward, the power would leak. If his waist turned ahead of the rest of the body, the strike would become effort instead of release. So he corrected it.
Again.
And again.
And again.
His training looked simple to outsiders. Almost boring. A step forward. A lowered stance. A held posture. A slow punch. A reset.
What they did not see was how much changed in each repetition.
Muscles along his thighs flexed and released in layered sequence, never all at once. His core tightened just enough to bind the movement together without locking his spine. His shoulders remained loose until the very final instant, allowing power to rise from the earth instead of being manufactured in the arms. His blood warmed evenly under prolonged effort, flowing more smoothly the more precisely he moved. Even his meridians, though not yet pushed into true violence, were being shaped by the pattern of his discipline. They widened subtly through repeated alignment, like narrow streams gradually becoming channels able to bear greater force.
His bones changed too.
Not visibly. Not dramatically.
But under stress correctly applied, they adapted. Ankles became more stable. The small joints in his hands thickened with use. The structure of his forearms, shoulders, hips, and spine learned to receive force without losing integrity. His body was no longer merely growing.
It was being built.
By him.
And because Mateo remembered what it meant to hold power beyond worlds, he refused to rush what most cultivators ruined through impatience.
A fast breakthrough built on weak structure was still weakness.
A flawed realm was still a flaw.
He had no interest in becoming stronger quickly if that strength would require him to correct errors later.
He wanted clean breakthroughs.
Perfect transitions.
A body, meridians, blood, bones, organs, and soul that advanced in harmony rather than scraping upward unevenly like a tower built on rotting wood.
So he trained.
He studied alchemy with the same seriousness. Not because pills fascinated him for their own sake, but because they revealed how matter wanted to transform under the right pressure. He spent time near the forge, watching steel heated and folded, increasingly convinced that shaping metal was not very different from shaping flesh. He learned the basic structures of runes, not because he needed them yet, but because symbols were simply force made visible. He experimented with talismans, healing salves, stabilizing pastes, and crude field medicine. If something could strengthen him, deepen his understanding, or keep him alive longer, he would eventually try it.
The village noticed.
Quietly.
He let them.
It was easy enough to appear merely dedicated.
It was more difficult to conceal how thoroughly he had begun to understand himself.
One afternoon, that difference became impossible not to see.
The training yard, such as it was, lay open beneath the filtered light of the forest canopy. It was nothing more than a widened basin of hard-packed dirt between houses and trees, bordered by low fencing in some places and roots in others. Beyond it, the village carried on as usual: a woman sorting herbs outside her stall, two boys arguing over spear length while one clearly had no idea what he was talking about, the forge ringing somewhere deeper along the main path.
Tomas stood across from Mateo, wooden saber in hand.
Years had sharpened him as well. His stance was steadier now, his shoulders broader, his movements cleaner. He no longer rushed because he wanted to win. He moved with focus because he wanted to understand.
"That's enough standing," Tomas said. "Move."
Mateo looked at him, then lowered into stance.
Slowly.
His right foot slid back half a step, toes pressing into the dirt with just enough force to grip without digging. His left knee bent, not dropping too far. The muscles along his thighs tightened in thin, coordinated lines, while his calves drew taut beneath the skin. His waist turned a fraction. His spine aligned. His shoulders stayed loose.
Then he exhaled.
The air around him did not change.
But the ground beneath him did.
Tomas noticed.
He always noticed.
The first strike came clean and direct, a testing cut aimed not to wound but to force Mateo into commitment. Mateo's eyes narrowed—not because the attack was fast, but because he had already read the imbalance in Tomas' rear foot before the blade moved.
He saw it all.
The slight tightening of Tomas' fingers.
The shift of muscle across his forearm.
The rotation in his shoulder preceding the cut.
Where most would have seen one action, Mateo saw sequence.
His gaze flicked once, then again, precise and rapid as if measuring lines no one else knew existed. He did not need to move far. He only needed to move correctly.
His left heel pressed harder into the ground. Power climbed from the sole through his ankle in a clean line, his calf flexing first, then the tendon behind the knee tightening just enough to stabilize the turn. His thigh engaged. His core locked for an instant.
Then he shifted.
The strike passed.
Not because he leapt away. Because he had already stepped into the place where it would fail.
Tomas turned smoothly into a second attack, lower this time, blade angling to cut off Mateo's escape and pressure his stance.
Mateo dropped.
One knee bent more deeply, the other anchored. For a brief instant he was almost squatting, but not loosely—his legs trembled with held power, every muscle loaded rather than strained. His toes dug in, his heels light. The line of force gathered beneath him.
Then it rose.
His right foot drove into the dirt, and power climbed cleanly from sole to ankle, up the shin, through the tightening calf, into the thigh and waist. His core compressed. His back remained straight. His shoulder turned only at the final instant, releasing the motion through his arm with nothing spilling out of sequence.
His forearm met Tomas' blade not by strength, but by angle.
The strike broke.
Not completely.
Enough.
Tomas stepped back and immediately corrected, shifting into a third motion faster than before. This time he came harder, forcing an angle that would have caught most opponents still recovering.
Mateo saw it.
Of course he saw it.
He always saw more than he allowed others to believe.
Even against opponents stronger than himself, Mateo's eyes could follow far more than he admitted. He just refused to reveal the extent of it unless forced. A hidden advantage remained an advantage. A displayed one invited larger problems.
So now—against Tomas, who was close enough to matter but not strong enough to force truth from him—Mateo let himself "barely" keep pace.
His head turned just enough. His pupils tracked the line of the blade. His focus narrowed on shoulder, elbow, wrist, then on the slight delay in Tomas' rear leg that told him where the follow-up would go before Tomas finished deciding it himself.
Mateo moved.
This time he stepped in.
His right foot twisted, pressing hard enough into the dirt that a crescent mark formed beneath it. His calf flexed sharply. The force rose through his planted leg, into his hips, through his tightened waist, and into his spine. He did not swing from the shoulder. He did not "throw" power.
He transmitted it.
His fist drove forward, stopping short of Tomas' ribs while his other hand redirected the blade outward.
Tomas froze.
They both held there for a moment.
Then Mateo relaxed.
"You were open."
Tomas exhaled through his nose, more annoyed with himself than the result.
"I was improving."
"You still were."
From the side, Diego spoke around a mouthful of food.
"That looked painful."
"It wasn't," Mateo said.
Tomas glanced over. "It would have been."
Diego nodded thoughtfully. "That feels close enough."
The spar continued.
Over several more exchanges, Tomas pressed harder and Mateo answered with less visible effort than should have been possible. When he stepped back, it was never wasted retreat. When he stepped forward, it was always with the exact amount of commitment required—never more. His muscles flexed and released with unnatural economy. Even his breathing seemed to obey him, deep when it needed depth, shallow when the body required speed, always timed so that strength never outran structure.
At one point, Tomas accelerated suddenly, striking from an angle meant to force genuine defense.
Mateo's awareness narrowed.
For a breath, everything seemed to separate.
The line of the blade.
The pressure in the ground.
The movement of Tomas' shoulders.
The possible responses.
He could pivot left and dissipate the strike. He could step inside and disrupt the elbow. He could drop low, let the motion pass, and take the opening beneath the centerline.
He chose the third.
His knees bent. His legs held and coiled. His right foot pressed so sharply into the ground that the packed dirt cracked faintly beneath it. His blood pounded once—harder, hotter—then evened immediately under control. The strength moved through him like a current, not wild, not delayed, climbing from foot to leg to waist to shoulder to hand without losing a fragment.
Then he was there.
Too close.
Too correct.
Tomas' strike failed before completion. Mateo's knuckles stopped just beneath his sternum.
Silence settled over the yard.
Diego lowered his food.
"…That one looked painful too."
Tomas stared at Mateo's fist, then at Mateo's face.
"You slowed down before the end."
"Yes."
"Why?"
Mateo straightened. "Because finishing it changes the conversation."
Tomas frowned. "That's vague."
"It's sufficient."
It was not the full answer, of course.
The truth was much simpler.
Mateo could have moved faster. He could have stripped the exchange of uncertainty entirely. In some moments, especially when his focus sharpened and his perception unfolded inward, he could see so many options at once that choosing the right one felt less like thought and more like inevitability.
But he preferred not to expose that.
He was enjoying himself.
Enjoying training. Enjoying learning. Enjoying the quiet absurdity of village life, where elders grumbled, Diego stole food from his own future self, and Tomas kept trying to solve a problem named Mateo as if that were a reasonable use of his time.
Rushing strength would end that sooner.
And Mateo was not ready for it to end.
By evening, he had resumed his other routines. He spent an hour near the forge, watching heat collect inside iron until it changed color and intention together. He checked two alchemical mixtures left to settle in covered bowls behind the house, adjusted one of them, discarded the other. He traced an almost-correct rune into packed dirt with a stick, stared at it for several breaths, then erased it because "almost correct" was another way of saying "wrong."
That night, he stood at the edge of the village where the dirt path gave way to broken roots and deepening shadow.
The forest had always been there.
At ten, it had been interesting.
At fourteen, it had become necessary.
Beyond the last cluster of homes, the air changed. It smelled less of smoke and cooking and metal, more of bark, damp soil, old growth, and hidden movement. The trees thickened as though conversation meant nothing to them. The ground lost its predictability. Stray paths faded. Even the wind seemed to carry less permission.
Mateo stared into it for a long moment.
Less structure.
More response.
Better pressure.
That mattered.
The village had been good for building control. The forest would test whether that control meant anything outside routine.
Behind him, the village still lived in its familiar rhythm—dirt roads winding between simple homes, open training spaces, quiet shops, people who preferred the honesty of effort over the polish of city refinement. It was enough.
But it was no longer all he wanted.
He looked down at the pendant in his hand.
It pulsed.
Stronger now. More often.
Closer.
For a moment, the world seemed to stretch again—not truly slow, but resolve differently. He felt paths before he chose them. Felt pressure before it arrived. Felt that strange inner quickening that reminded him, faintly, of lightning—not merely as force, but as certainty.
Then it passed.
Mateo closed his hand around the pendant and looked back toward the forest.
"Soon," he said quietly.
This time, when he stepped forward, he did not stop at the edge.
